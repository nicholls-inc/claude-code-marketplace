A heat pump does not make heat. It moves heat that already exists outdoors into your house. That distinction is why it can warm a home when the air outside is colder than the air inside.

**Cold is not the absence of heat**

Heat is energy, and even air at 0°C holds plenty of it. Only at absolute zero, about -273°C, is there none left to take. So a winter garden is a large store of low-grade heat. The difficulty is that heat naturally flows from warm to cold, not the other way. A heat pump uses a small amount of electricity to push it uphill.

**The refrigerant cycle**

Your fridge already works this way. A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, around -40°C or lower. The cycle has four steps.

1. **Evaporator (outside unit).** Cold refrigerant passes through a coil in the outdoor unit while a fan blows outside air across it. The refrigerant is colder than the air, even on a freezing day, so it absorbs heat from the air and boils into a gas.

2. **Compressor.** This is the part that uses electricity. It squeezes the gas, which raises its pressure and temperature sharply, much as a bicycle pump gets hot when you use it. The gas might now be 50 to 70°C, hotter than your indoor air.

3. **Condenser (indoor side).** The hot gas passes through a coil connected to your radiators, underfloor heating or hot water cylinder. Because the gas is hotter than the water or air there, heat flows into your home. As it loses heat, the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid.

4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid is released through a small opening, its pressure drops, and it becomes very cold again. It returns to the outdoor coil and the loop repeats.

The trick is the compressor. By changing the pressure, it shifts the refrigerant's temperature so that it is colder than outdoors on one side and hotter than indoors on the other.

**Why it is efficient**

Moving heat takes far less energy than creating it. A heat pump typically delivers 2.5 to 4 units of heat for every unit of electricity, a ratio called the coefficient of performance (COP). A gas boiler can never beat 100 percent, and in practice sits around 85 to 92 percent. A COP of 3 means 300 percent. Efficiency falls as the outdoor temperature drops, because the compressor has to lift the heat further. Modern units still work well at -15°C or colder, but a COP nearer 2 on the coldest days is realistic.

**What this means for your decision**

- **Running costs.** Electricity usually costs more per unit than gas, so savings depend on the price ratio and your COP. Where electricity costs under about three times as much as gas, a heat pump is often cheaper to run. Check your own tariffs.
- **Your house matters.** Heat pumps work best at lower flow temperatures (35 to 50°C), compared with 65 to 80°C for many boilers. Good insulation, draught-proofing and larger radiators or underfloor heating help a great deal. A leaky house with small radiators may need upgrades first.
- **Upfront cost.** Installation is typically several times the price of a new boiler, though grants or subsidies may be available where you live. Ask for a heat loss survey and a quote that includes any radiator changes.
- **Carbon.** Even on a grid that still uses some fossil fuel, a heat pump usually cuts household emissions substantially, and the gap widens as the grid gets cleaner.
- **Practicalities.** You need outdoor space for the unit, and possibly room for a hot water cylinder. Units make some fan noise, so placement matters.

**Bottom line**

A heat pump works in cold weather because outdoor air still contains heat and the compressor lifts it to a useful temperature. It is a strong choice for a well-insulated home, a boiler near the end of its life, or anyone wanting lower emissions. If your home is poorly insulated or electricity is expensive relative to gas, improve the building or compare tariffs first. A qualified installer's heat loss calculation will tell you what your house needs.
