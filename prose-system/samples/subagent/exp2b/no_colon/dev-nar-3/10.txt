On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually mild winter day. The temperature had climbed above forty degrees after a bitter cold snap, and people were out in the streets near the harbor. Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, shipped up from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol, and by extension into munitions for a war that had just ended.

The tank had been built quickly and cheaply in 1915. Its walls were too thin, it was never properly tested, and nobody bothered to check its design. It leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups. Rather than fix the problem, the company painted the tank brown to hide the stains. Only days before the disaster, a fresh shipment of warm molasses had been pumped into the cold, already strained container.

Shortly after noon, residents heard a low rumble and a sound some compared to a machine gun, the rivets shooting out of the tank. Then the steel gave way. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged outward at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a pressure wave of air ahead of it, and it destroyed everything in its path.

The wave crushed buildings and flung a railroad car from its tracks. It tore a section from the elevated railway structure, bent girders as if they were wire, and knocked a firehouse off its foundation. Wagons, horses, and people were swept away. Because the temperature was mild, the syrup flowed far more freely than anyone might expect, but it soon began to cool and thicken, and that proved deadly. Victims were trapped as the molasses stiffened around them. Rescuers waded through a sticky, knee-deep mess, and many of those pulled out were suffocated or badly crushed.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Among the dead were children who had been playing near the waterfront and workers eating lunch. Police, firefighters, sailors from a nearby ship, and Red Cross staff all joined the effort, and some rescuers had to cut through the syrup to reach survivors. Recovery of the bodies took days. The harbor ran brown, and the smell lingered for decades. Cleanup crews used salt water from fireboats, and sand to soak up the remains.

The aftermath was as significant as the disaster itself. Residents filed one of the earliest large class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company claimed that anarchists had bombed the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court heard years of testimony and concluded that the structure had simply failed. Purity Distilling was held liable and paid damages, in the end about 600,000 dollars, a modest sum even for the time.

The case helped push for stricter building regulations. Engineers were now required to certify their plans, and the era of casual construction began to close. Old-timers in the North End like to claim that on hot summer days, the neighborhood still smells faintly sweet.
