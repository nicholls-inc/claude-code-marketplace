Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon. The rules were adopted with good intentions, but they have grown into one of the most expensive and least examined mandates in local government. They force everyone to pay for something that many people do not want, and they shape our cities in ways that almost nobody would choose if the question were put to them directly.

Consider first what a parking mandate actually does. A typical rule says that a new apartment building must include one or two spaces per unit, or that a restaurant must provide a certain number of spaces for every thousand square feet of floor area. Those numbers are not discovered through careful study of demand. Many of them trace back to a handbook of parking generation rates that drew on a small number of suburban sites, often with plenty of free parking and no transit, and planners copied them from one city to the next. The result is a figure that sounds precise but rests on very little evidence, and it is then written into law as though it were a fact of nature.

The cost of meeting that figure is enormous. A surface space takes up roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles and the driveways, which is often more room than the apartment it is meant to serve. A space in a structured garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost even more. Those costs do not disappear. Developers pass them on to tenants and buyers, so a household that owns no car still pays for parking through higher rent, and the poorest renters, who are the least likely to own a vehicle, are subsidizing the storage of other people's cars.

The mandates also quietly kill housing and small businesses before they are ever built. A modest lot that could hold eight apartments might hold only five once the required parking is drawn onto the plan, and the project may no longer pencil out at all. Older neighborhoods are full of charming corner shops, duplexes, and small apartment buildings that would be illegal to build today because they lack the spaces the code demands. When a city insists on parking, it is insisting that its next generation of buildings be bigger, costlier, and more spread out than the ones that made its best districts so appealing.

Defenders of the rules raise an understandable worry, which is that removing them will flood the streets with parked cars and make life miserable for existing residents. This fear deserves a direct answer. Eliminating a requirement is not the same as banning parking. Developers who believe their tenants will want spaces will still build them, because a building that cannot attract customers is a bad investment, and lenders and market-rate buyers will keep asking for them in car-oriented places. What changes is that the amount of parking becomes a business decision informed by real demand rather than a political number set decades ago. Cities that have tried this, including Buffalo, which became the first large American city to drop its requirements citywide in 2017, and more recently a wave of cities in California, Minnesota, and elsewhere, have generally seen developers keep building plenty of parking where it makes sense and skip it where it does not.

If spillover onto the street really does become a problem, there are better tools than a blanket mandate. A city can price curb parking so that a few spaces on every block stay open, and it can direct the revenue back to the neighborhood that generates it. It can issue residential permits that protect existing residents from commuters. These measures treat the actual problem, which is that a scarce public resource is being handed out for free, rather than forcing every new building to hide the problem behind a private garage. Free curb space always runs short, and a minimum requirement for off-street spaces does not repair that. It only spreads the shortage across more concrete.

There is also a climate and fiscal case that is hard to ignore. Parking mandates make driving the easy default by guaranteeing that a free space waits at every destination, and that makes it harder for buses, trains, and bike lanes to compete. They spread buildings apart, which lengthens every trip and thins out the customers who keep a main street alive. Vast paved lots also generate almost no property tax per acre compared with the buildings that could stand in their place, so cities end up with a thin tax base and a long list of roads and pipes to maintain.

None of this means that cars have no place in a city or that every neighborhood is ready to live without them. Plenty of people need a vehicle for work, for caring for family, or because they live somewhere that transit does not reach, and their needs are real. The point is that those needs are best met by letting builders, owners, and neighbors decide how much parking is worth paying for in each particular place, instead of by a single formula applied everywhere.

Cities that remove their minimums are not abandoning planning. They are admitting that a number invented long ago should not outrank the judgment of the people who actually live, build, and do business there. The sooner more cities make that admission, the sooner they can start building the homes, shops, and streets that their residents say they want.
