In late August 1854, cholera struck Soho, a crowded district of London. Within ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few hundred yards of one another had died. Cholera is a disease that causes violent diarrhoea and rapid dehydration, and in those days it could kill a healthy adult in a day or two. Nobody knew what caused it. The dominant belief was the "miasma" theory: that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting waste and filthy ground. The theory seemed sensible in a city that stank.

John Snow, a physician who lived nearby, doubted it. He had already made his name as a pioneer of anaesthesia, famously administering chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. Cholera, though, had been on his mind for years. During the epidemic of 1848-49 he had noticed something that miasma could not explain. The illness began with stomach and bowel symptoms, not with anything in the lungs, which is where you would expect trouble if people were breathing a poison. Snow concluded that people must be swallowing something. In 1849 he published a short essay arguing that cholera was carried in contaminated water. Few took it seriously.

The Soho outbreak gave him a chance to test the idea. He walked the streets, knocked on doors, and asked the families of the dead how they lived and what they drank. Rather than trusting impressions, he collected facts: where each victim lived, and when they fell ill.

He also did something that proved decisive. He drew a map. On a street plan of the neighbourhood, he marked each death with a small black bar, stacked at the address where it happened. Deaths piled up thickly around one spot, the public water pump on Broad Street, where residents drew water for drinking and cooking. The pattern was not proof by itself, since many people lived near the pump for other reasons, but it was suggestive. What came next is where Snow's work became real detective work.

He looked for the exceptions. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed about five hundred people, yet only five died. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street employed seventy workers and had no deaths at all. The workers were given free beer, and the brewery had its own well, so they never went to the pump. Both cases were awkward for the miasma theory, since the air was equally bad outside their doors.

Then came the opposite kind of case. Snow heard of deaths at a distance from Soho, including a woman in Hampstead, far to the north, and her niece. He tracked down the explanation. The woman had once lived in Broad Street, and she liked the taste of the water from that pump so much that she had a bottle of it delivered to her regularly. She drank it, fell ill, and died. So did her niece, who drank from the same supply. Nobody else in her neighbourhood was affected.

Snow also questioned the families of the people who had died near Broad Street but who appeared at first not to use the pump. In many cases he found that a child or relative had sometimes fetched water from it, or that people had drunk there at a shop or a tavern.

By September 7, Snow was confident enough to take his findings to the local board of guardians, the officials responsible for the parish. He urged them to act. The next day they ordered the handle removed from the pump, so that nobody could draw water from it.

The removal of the handle is often told as the moment that ended the epidemic, but the truth is more complicated. The outbreak had already peaked and was fading by then, partly because many residents had fled the area and partly because the disease was running its course. Snow himself acknowledged that the numbers had been falling. Still, the story endures, because the handle came to symbolise the idea that you could stop a disease by cutting off its source.

Snow's detective work did not end there. He wanted to know why the water had become dangerous. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who had been sceptical of Snow's ideas, set out to disprove them and ended up strengthening them. Whitehead discovered that the outbreak began with a baby living at 40 Broad Street, who had died of cholera days earlier. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspool that was leaking and sat very close to the well. When the cesspool was examined, it was found to be in a poor state. Contaminated waste had seeped into the water the neighbourhood drank.

The authorities were slow to embrace Snow's conclusion. A official inquiry found the evidence insufficient and clung to the idea of bad air. Snow died in 1858 without seeing his view widely accepted. Only later, as germ theory took hold and the cholera bacterium was identified, did his work receive its full credit. Today he is regarded as one of the founders of epidemiology: the study of how diseases spread through populations. His method, careful questions, a good map, and a hunt for the cases that did not fit, remains the model.
