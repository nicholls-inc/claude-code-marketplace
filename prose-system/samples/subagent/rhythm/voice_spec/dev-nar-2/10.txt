Apollo 13 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida on 11 April 1970, with James Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert aboard. It was the third mission planned to land men on the Moon. Lovell, the commander, had already flown to the Moon on Apollo 8 and was making his fourth spaceflight. Haise was to pilot the lunar module, and Swigert was to stay in lunar orbit with the command module. Swigert had joined the crew late. Ken Mattingly, the original command module pilot, had been exposed to German measles and doctors judged that he had no immunity to it, so Swigert took his place three days before launch.

The launch had one fault. The centre engine of the second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the other four engines burned longer to make up the difference. The spacecraft reached orbit as planned. The crew named the command module Odyssey and the lunar module Aquarius. After leaving Earth orbit they made a course change on the second day that took them off the trajectory that would have looped them back to Earth without any further burn. This was needed to reach the landing site at Fra Mauro, and it was routine at the time.

The accident had its origin on the ground. The service module carried two tanks of liquid oxygen, which fed the fuel cells that made the spacecraft's electricity and also supplied air for the crew. The second tank had been fitted to an earlier spacecraft, removed for a modification, and dropped by about two inches during the removal. This probably loosened a fill tube inside it. Before launch the tank would not empty normally, so technicians turned on its internal heaters to boil off the oxygen. The heaters were run from a 65-volt ground supply, but their thermostatic switches were rated for 28 volts. The switches fused shut, and the temperature inside the tank rose to something near 500 degrees Celsius. This damaged the Teflon insulation on the wiring. Nobody saw it happen, because the temperature gauge in the tank read only as high as 38 degrees.

On the evening of 13 April, about 56 hours into the flight and roughly 330,000 kilometres from Earth, Houston asked Swigert to switch on the fans that stirred the oxygen in the tanks. A short circuit in the damaged wiring set the insulation alight, and the pressure inside the second tank rose until it burst. The explosion blew a panel off the side of the service module. Swigert reported, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," and Lovell repeated it a moment later. A master alarm sounded, the electrical voltage fell, and the pressure in the second tank dropped to zero. The first tank was also leaking. Looking out of a window, Lovell saw gas streaming into space. Within about two hours the service module had lost all of its oxygen, and with it two of its three fuel cells.

The command module could run on its batteries for only a few hours, and it needed what was left of them for re-entry. The crew therefore moved into Aquarius and used it as a lifeboat. It had been designed to keep two men alive for about two days on the Moon, and now it had to support three men for four days. The landing was abandoned. Flight director Gene Kranz and his team in Houston, together with the crew, turned to the question of how to get them home.

The service module engine could in principle have turned the spacecraft around at once, but nobody knew what condition it was in after the explosion. The controllers chose instead to let the spacecraft swing round the Moon and use the lunar module's descent engine to speed up the return. The crew passed behind the Moon at a height of about 254 kilometres. At its greatest distance they were 400,171 kilometres from Earth, which is still the farthest that any people have been. About two hours after closest approach they fired the descent engine for just over four minutes. This shortened the trip and set the splashdown in the Pacific Ocean on 17 April.

Conditions aboard were poor. To save power the crew switched off almost everything, including the heating, and the cabin temperature fell to about 3 degrees Celsius. Water vapour condensed on the walls. Water for drinking was limited to about 200 millilitres per person each day, and the crew slept very little. Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection. Lovell later recalled the cold as the worst of it, though this was his own judgement and the others may have weighed it differently.

A further problem came from the air. Carbon dioxide had to be removed with lithium hydroxide canisters. Aquarius had round canisters for two men, which began to fill up, and the spare canisters in Odyssey were square and did not fit the lunar module's fittings. Engineers in Houston built an adapter from items known to be on board. It used a plastic bag, cardboard from a flight manual cover, a sock, a hose from a spacesuit and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew step by step, and the crew built the device and fitted it. The carbon dioxide level in the cabin dropped to a safe figure.

The spacecraft also drifted off its course, and a second burn of the descent engine was needed at about 105 hours. The guidance computer had been powered down to save electricity, so the crew aligned the spacecraft by hand, using the edge of the sunlit part of the Earth as a reference. Meanwhile Mattingly, who had been left on the ground, worked in a simulator with engineers to write a procedure for restarting the command module on very little power. The usual start-up took hours and used more power than the batteries could supply, so the sequence had to be rewritten. The new checklist was read up to the crew and they followed it.

About four hours before re-entry the crew released the service module, and for the first time saw what had happened to it. Lovell said that one whole side of the spacecraft was missing. Photographs showed the panel gone and equipment hanging from the opening. The crew then moved into Odyssey, powered it up, and released Aquarius about an hour before re-entry. Nobody on the ground knew whether the explosion had harmed the heat shield on the command module.

Re-entry on 17 April was also marked by an anxious wait. The radio blackout caused by the heat of re-entry usually lasts about three minutes. This time it lasted four minutes and 27 seconds, longer than planned, because the spacecraft had entered at a slightly different angle. Then the controllers heard Swigert's voice, the parachutes opened, and Odyssey landed in the South Pacific near Samoa at 18:07 UTC. The ship USS Iwo Jima recovered the crew. The mission had lasted 142 hours and 54 minutes. All three men were tired and thinner, and Haise was ill, but they were otherwise in good health.

A review board chaired by Edgar Cortright investigated the accident. It traced the fault to the heater switches and the damaged wiring in the second oxygen tank, and to the chain of decisions that had left the damage unnoticed. NASA redesigned the tanks, removed the fans from them and added a third tank. The next mission, Apollo 14, flew in January 1971 and landed at Fra Mauro. Apollo 13 is often called a successful failure, a view that rests on the fact that it never reached the Moon but brought its crew home alive.
