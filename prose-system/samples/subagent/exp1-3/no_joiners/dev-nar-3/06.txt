On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying a surprising break in the weather. The day before, the temperature had hovered near two degrees Fahrenheit. Now it had climbed above forty, and workers, children and horses crowded the waterfront streets. Towering over them stood a steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It was fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held about two and a half million gallons of molasses.

The tank had never been trusted by the people who lived near it. It had been built quickly in 1915, and it groaned when filled. It leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the syrup in cans. Rather than repair it properly, the company painted it brown so the stains would not show. No one had run a full test of its strength. A fresh shipment had arrived only days earlier, and the sudden warmth thinned the molasses and made it expand.

Shortly after noon, residents heard a sound like a machine gun as rivets shot loose from the steel. Then the tank burst. A wave of molasses roughly twenty five feet high at its crest swept out across the neighborhood at around thirty five miles an hour. It carried a pressure heavy enough to crush buildings and bend the supports of the elevated railway. A train driver barely stopped in time as the track sagged beside him.

The wave tore a nearby firehouse from its foundations and trapped firefighters inside. Wagons, horses and people were thrown against walls and buried under wreckage. Those caught in the flood found that the syrup grew thicker and stickier as the cold air worked on it. Swimming was impossible, and every movement tightened its hold. Rescuers waded into the mess to reach survivors, and sailors from a nearby ship and Red Cross nurses joined them. Within minutes the street was a brown lake several feet deep.

Twenty one people died, among them children, and about one hundred fifty were injured. Cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed saltwater from fireboats and spread sand and sawdust over the streets. The harbor ran brown into the summer, and locals insisted that on warm days the neighborhood still smelled sweet for decades afterward.

The legal aftermath proved just as important as the disaster itself. The company blamed anarchists and claimed that a bomb had destroyed the tank. Hundreds of residents sued in one of the earliest class action cases in Massachusetts. A court appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, spent years hearing testimony from experts. He concluded that the tank had been poorly designed and badly tested and that the company was responsible. It paid out settlements to victims and families.

The flood changed how buildings were made in America. Cities began to require engineers to certify their plans and to approve safety calculations before large structures could rise. A catastrophe born of carelessness helped bring modern building rules into being.
