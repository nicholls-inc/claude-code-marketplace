On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, in Boston's crowded North End, a steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, gave way beside Commercial Street and released about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, roughly 8,700 cubic meters, which had been stored there to be turned into industrial alcohol, a use that grew more urgent as Prohibition approached and munitions makers wanted the product too. The tank stood some fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, had been built hastily in 1915 with thin plates and few rivets, and had never been properly tested, for the company had not bothered to fill it with water first. It leaked so freely that neighbors collected the syrup in pots and the company, instead of mending the seams, painted the tank brown so the stains would not show. A fresh shipment from the Caribbean had been pumped in the day before, and the temperature, which had hovered near zero in the preceding days, had risen above forty degrees Fahrenheit, factors that investigators would later weigh among the causes.

Witnesses described a rumbling like a passing train, then a sound like machine-gun fire as the rivets sheared, and then a wave of molasses, reported at fifteen feet at its crest in places, that swept out at something like thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed the nearby streets, lifted a freight car and a firehouse off their footings, buckled the girders of the elevated railway, and flattened wooden houses so that their occupants were carried along inside the wreckage. Twenty-one people died, among them children who had been gathering firewood near the tank and men eating lunch at the firehouse, and about a hundred and fifty more were injured. The cold air quickly thickened the syrup, so that those caught in it were held fast and could barely be dug free, and rescuers, among them sailors from a docked training ship, soldiers, nurses and Red Cross workers, waded in to cut people out, a task that went on for days because the molasses clung to everything it touched and hid bodies beneath it.

The cleanup took weeks and a great many hands, with firefighters using salt water from fireboats and crews spreading sand and sawdust, yet the smell lingered for years, and residents said the harbor ran brown until summer and that the neighborhood still smelled sweet on hot days long afterward. The company first blamed anarchists, claiming that the tank had been bombed, a story that fitted the fear of the time, but families sued, and in one of the earliest big class actions in Massachusetts an auditor appointed to hear the evidence spent three years sorting through thousands of pages of testimony from some 3,000 witnesses. He concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, and in 1925 the company was ordered to pay damages, with settlements of roughly 7,000 dollars paid to the families of the dead, which was a modest sum even then.

The disaster is remembered partly for its strangeness, but its lasting effect was practical, since it helped bring in tighter oversight of construction, so that architects and engineers had to sign off on plans and calculations before large structures were built, a change that shaped building codes well beyond Boston.
