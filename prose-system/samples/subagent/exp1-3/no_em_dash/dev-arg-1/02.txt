**Scrap the Parking Mandate**

Every city that requires a minimum number of parking spaces for new buildings is running an experiment in central planning. The planners guess how many cars a restaurant, apartment block or dentist's office will attract, write the guess into law, and force builders to comply. The guess is almost always wrong, and the people who pay for the error are everyone who never asked for it. Cities should remove minimum parking requirements entirely.

Start with what the rules actually do. A typical code demands something like one or two spaces per apartment, a set number per thousand square feet of retail, and a space for every few seats in a restaurant. These ratios were often copied from manuals compiled decades ago, based on a handful of suburban sites measured at peak demand. Donald Shoup, the UCLA scholar who made this his life's work, found that the figures in the standard reference were drawn from small samples and had little statistical grounding. Yet they became law in thousands of towns.

The first cost is money, and it lands on housing. A surface space costs a few thousand dollars to build. A space in an underground garage can cost fifty thousand dollars or more, and in expensive cities far more. Developers do not absorb that cost. They pass it to tenants and buyers, or they build fewer homes, or they abandon the project. A requirement of one space per unit can make a small apartment building financially impossible, even when future residents would happily live without a car. Bundling parking into the price of every home means a household that owns no car still pays for a space it will never use. That is a regressive tax, and it falls hardest on the poor, who own fewer cars.

The second cost is land. Parking is hungry for space. A single surface space with its share of driveway and aisle takes up roughly three hundred square feet, about the size of a studio apartment. In many American downtowns, the area devoted to parking rivals or exceeds the area devoted to the buildings it serves. That land could hold homes, shops, offices or parks. Instead it sits empty most of the day, paying little in tax and generating no street life. Cities then wonder why their centers feel hollow and why their tax base struggles to cover the cost of roads and pipes.

Third, the rules lock in car dependence. If every destination must come with a lot, destinations spread out, and spreading out makes walking, cycling and transit less practical. That in turn makes owning a car feel mandatory, which seems to justify the next round of parking requirements. It is a loop that planners built and can unbuild.

The usual objection is that without mandates, the streets will fill with cars and neighborhoods will suffer. This worry deserves a direct answer. First, removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Builders will still provide spaces where customers demand them. A suburban grocery store will not open without a lot, and no law is needed to tell it so. What changes is that the number becomes a business decision based on real demand, not a figure imposed by an ordinance. Second, where street parking is truly scarce, the right tool is pricing, not mandates. Charging a fair rate for curb space, and returning the revenue to the neighborhood, keeps spaces available and gives residents a reason to welcome change. Shoup has shown that well-run meter districts can fund sidewalks and street trees while cutting the time drivers spend circling.

Evidence from real places supports this. Buffalo, New York eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and it did so without the predicted chaos. Developers there have built projects with parking that matches the market, and some have built with much less. Minneapolis, Portland and Edmonton, along with a growing list of other cities, have followed with their own reforms. Cities that have gone this way report more housing proposals, cheaper projects, and the reuse of old buildings that could not have met modern parking rules. Many historic main streets are illegal to build today precisely because their buildings lack the required lots. Removing the mandate lets a corner shop reopen in a former house without buying the neighbor's yard.

A fair critic will say that some developers will underbuild and some neighbors will be annoyed. That is true, and it is a price worth paying. The current system errs in the other direction every day, producing empty lots, costly housing and ugly streets, and nobody notices because the harm is spread thin. Flexibility lets mistakes be small and local, and the market can correct them. Mandates make mistakes universal and permanent.

There is also a basic matter of fairness and humility. A city council has no special insight into how many cars a future tenant will own. A builder who risks real money has every reason to find out. When the person bearing the cost makes the decision, the decision improves. When a distant formula decides, we get the oversized lots that dot every highway strip.

Removing minimums does not remove choice. It restores it. People who want a home with a garage can still find one. People who want an affordable apartment near a train station will finally be allowed to build and rent one. A city that trusts builders and residents to weigh the trade-offs will get more homes, more life on its streets and a tax base that grows instead of paving over itself. The minimum parking requirement was a guess that hardened into law. It is time to let it go.
