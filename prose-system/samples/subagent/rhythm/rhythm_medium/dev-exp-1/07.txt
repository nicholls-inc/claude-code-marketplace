A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists in the outdoor air into your home, and the surprising part is that this works even when the air outside feels bitterly cold. Cold is only a relative idea. Air at minus five degrees Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy, because it is nowhere near absolute zero, and a heat pump is built to extract some of that energy and carry it indoors.

The trick is the refrigerant, a special fluid that circulates in a sealed loop between an outdoor unit and your indoor heating system. In the outdoor unit the refrigerant is kept at a very low pressure, and at low pressure it boils at a very low temperature, well below the temperature of the winter air. Because the air is warmer than the refrigerant, heat flows naturally from the air into the fluid, which causes it to evaporate into a gas. Nothing unusual is happening here, since heat always flows from warmer things to colder ones, and the refrigerant has simply been made colder than the outdoors.

The gas then passes through a compressor, which is the main part that uses electricity. Squeezing a gas raises its pressure and, as a direct consequence, its temperature, much as a bicycle pump warms up when you use it. The refrigerant leaves the compressor much hotter than the air inside your home, often reaching fifty or sixty degrees. It then flows through a heat exchanger indoors, where it gives up that heat to the water in your radiators or underfloor pipes, or to the air in your rooms. As it releases heat, the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid. Finally it passes through an expansion valve, which drops its pressure sharply and chills it again, and the cycle begins afresh.

So the pump does not defy any rule about heat flowing from hot to cold. It uses the compressor and the valve to make the refrigerant colder than the outside air at one end of the loop and hotter than your radiators at the other. Electricity pays for the moving and squeezing, not for the heat itself.

This is why heat pumps are so efficient. A good electric heater turns one unit of electricity into one unit of heat, and a gas boiler typically delivers somewhat less than one unit of heat for each unit of gas burned. A heat pump might deliver three or even four units of heat for each unit of electricity, because most of the energy is simply collected from the air. That ratio is called the coefficient of performance, and it is the number to ask installers about. It falls as the weather gets colder, because the pump has to work harder to lift heat across a bigger temperature gap, but modern units still perform respectably at minus ten or lower.

For you as a homeowner, the practical question is whether your house suits one. Heat pumps run most efficiently when they deliver water at a lower temperature than a boiler does, so they work best in well insulated homes with large radiators or underfloor heating. If your house is draughty or has small old radiators, you may need to improve the insulation or upgrade some radiators first, and a competent installer should calculate the heat loss of each room before quoting. You will also need space outside for the unit and usually a hot water cylinder inside.

Costs deserve a clear look too. The installation is usually more expensive than a new gas boiler, although grants are available in many places. Running costs depend on the price of electricity relative to gas, and a heat pump's efficiency narrows that gap considerably. If you can pair it with a time-of-use tariff or solar panels, the savings improve further. Over a decade or more, many households find a heat pump competitive, and it also cuts carbon emissions substantially, especially as the electricity grid gets cleaner.
