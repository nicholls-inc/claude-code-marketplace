**Sharing a Secret in Public: How Public-Key Cryptography Works**

Imagine you want to tell a friend a secret, but the only way to reach her is by shouting across a crowded square. Everyone can hear every word. You have never met her, so there is no code word you agreed on beforehand. It sounds impossible. Yet this is exactly what happens every time your phone connects to a bank or a shop online, and it works because of a clever trick with numbers.

**The old problem**

For most of history, secret messages needed a shared secret. You and your friend would agree on a key in advance, perhaps a codebook or a rule for shuffling letters. Anyone who knew the key could read the message. Anyone who did not could not. The trouble was getting the key to your friend in the first place. If you sent it by courier, the courier could be bribed. If you shouted it, everyone heard. The key itself needed protecting, and that needed another key, and so on.

In the 1970s, researchers found a way out. Their idea was to use a kind of maths that is easy to do in one direction and extremely hard to undo.

**One-way jobs**

Think about mixing paint. Stir yellow into blue and you get green in seconds. Now hand someone the green and ask them to separate it back into yellow and blue. They cannot. The mixing was easy; the unmixing is hopeless.

Numbers have jobs like this too. Multiplying is the best-known example. Take two large prime numbers, which are numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves, such as 7, 13 or 101. Multiply 101 by 113 and you get 11,413 with a bit of effort. Now suppose I give you only 11,413 and ask which two primes made it. You would have to try dividing by 2, 3, 5, 7 and so on until something fits. With small numbers that is tolerable. With primes that are hundreds of digits long, even the fastest computers on Earth would need longer than the age of the universe.

That gap between "easy to do" and "practically impossible to undo" is the foundation of everything that follows.

**Mixing colours in public**

Here is the paint idea turned into a method for agreeing on a secret. It is usually told with colours, so let us do that first.

You and your friend, whom I will call Asha, publicly agree on a common colour, say yellow. Everyone hears this. It is not a secret.

Now each of you privately chooses a colour of your own and tells no one. You pick red. Asha picks blue. You mix your private red into the shared yellow and get orange. Asha mixes her private blue into the yellow and gets green. Then you swap these mixtures in public. You send her your orange; she sends you her green. Anyone listening sees both.

Finally, you add your private red to the green you received. Asha adds her private blue to the orange she received. Both of you now hold yellow plus red plus blue, the same final mixture. The listener holds yellow, orange and green. To reach your colour they would have to unmix, and unmixing is the thing we said cannot be done.

That shared final colour is your secret. Nobody carried it across the square. You each built it from pieces, and only you two had the missing private ingredient.

**The same trick with numbers**

Paint cannot be used by computers, so we replace the mixing with arithmetic. The tool is called the remainder, or modulo. Take a clock face with 7 hours. If it is 5 o'clock and you wait 4 hours, you land on 2, because 9 goes round the dial and leaves 2. We write that as 9 mod 7 = 2. You already do this when you tell the time.

Now we need an operation that is easy forwards and hard backwards. Raising a number to a power, then taking the remainder, fits. Let us use a small example, keeping in mind that real systems use numbers with hundreds of digits.

The two of you agree in public on two numbers: a base, 3, and a modulus, 17. Everyone hears these.

You secretly choose 6. Asha secretly chooses 5.

You compute 3 to the power 6, which is 729, and take the remainder after dividing by 17. That is 729 minus 714, so 15. You send 15 across the square.

Asha computes 3 to the power 5, which is 243. Dividing by 17, the remainder is 5. She sends 5.

Now you take her 5 and raise it to your private power 6. That is 15,625. The remainder after dividing by 17 is 15,625 minus 15,623, which is 2. Asha takes your 15 and raises it to her private power 5, which is 759,375. The remainder after dividing by 17 is 2.

You both land on 2. That is your shared secret.

What did the eavesdropper hear? The numbers 3, 17, 15 and 5. To find your secret she would need to work out your private 6, or Asha's private 5, from what she heard. With tiny numbers she can simply try them all, and finds the answer in moments. With a modulus hundreds of digits long, trying them all is out of the question. Going forward, raising to a power, is quick. Going backward, finding which power produced a given remainder, has no known shortcut.

Why do both of you reach the same number? Because the order of multiplication does not matter. Raising 3 to the power 6, then raising the result to the power 5, gives the same as raising 3 to the power 5, then to the power 6. Both equal 3 to the power 30. Each of you has done half the work in a different order, and the halves meet in the middle.

**What is the secret used for?**

The shared number is then used as a key for ordinary scrambling of the actual message. Scrambling with a shared key is fast, so it is perfect for the bulk of the conversation. The clever part was only ever needed to get that key into both hands safely.

This method is named after two of the people who published it, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, in 1976. It is not the only approach. A second family of ideas gives each person a pair of keys: a public one, which they hand out freely, and a private one, which they never share. Anything locked with the public key can only be unlocked with the private key. You can picture a padlock. Asha posts open padlocks to everyone. You snap one shut on a box holding your message and send it back. Only she holds the key. The RSA system, which uses the prime-multiplication trick from earlier, works this way. Your browser uses a modern version of one or other of these methods every time you see the little padlock symbol.

**What can still go wrong?**

The method protects against listening. It does not by itself protect against impersonation. If someone stands in the square pretending to be Asha, you will happily agree a secret with the impostor. To handle that, real systems add certificates: stamps of approval from trusted organisations confirming that a public number really belongs to the person or website claiming it.

There is also a longer-term worry. Some mathematicians believe that a future kind of computer, a quantum computer, could unmix certain numbers far faster than today's machines. Researchers are already designing replacements built on different hard problems.

**The takeaway**

Two strangers can build a shared secret in public because some jobs are easy to do and nearly impossible to undo. Each person keeps one private ingredient. Each publishes a mixture. Combining your own private ingredient with the other person's mixture gives you both the same result, while a listener who has only the mixtures is stuck at the hard step. It is a little like a conjuring trick performed in front of a full audience, and it is the reason secrets can travel across the open internet at all.
