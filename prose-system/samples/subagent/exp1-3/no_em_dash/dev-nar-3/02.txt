On a mild January day in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy. Wednesday, January 15, was unusually warm after a hard cold snap, and around noon the neighborhood's dockworkers, children, and housewives were going about their business near Commercial Street. Looming over them stood a steel tank, fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, a thick syrup used to make rum and industrial alcohol.

Residents had long known something was wrong with the tank. It groaned. It leaked so badly that molasses ran down its sides, and neighborhood children collected the sticky drips in cans. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown, so the stains would not show. The structure had been built hastily, with thin steel and little testing. Only once had it been filled to the brim to see whether it would hold, and even then no one checked it carefully.

At about 12:40 p.m., the tank failed. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rapid popping of rivets shearing loose, followed by a deep rumble. Then the tank burst open. A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen feet high in places, surged into the streets at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a crushing force. The wave tore a section of the elevated railway off its supports, knocked a firehouse off its foundation, and flattened wagons, horses, and buildings alike.

People caught in the flood had almost no chance to run. The molasses was heavy and thick, and as it cooled in the winter air it grew stickier, trapping victims in place. Rescuers waded in, but the syrup clung to their legs and clothes, and every movement was a struggle. Firefighters, police, soldiers, and sailors from a nearby ship rushed to help. Medical workers set up a makeshift hospital nearby. The search for survivors continued for days.

In the end, twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. The dead included children and workers who had been eating lunch nearby. Cleanup took weeks. Crews used salt water from fireboats and sand to scour the streets, but the smell of molasses lingered for years, and on warm days, residents claimed they could still catch it.

The aftermath changed the way American buildings were made. The company blamed anarchists, claiming a bomb had destroyed the tank. A long court case followed, one of the first major class action suits in Massachusetts. After hearing years of testimony, an auditor found that the tank had been poorly designed and carelessly built, and the company was ordered to pay damages. Its engineers had never even been licensed. The case helped push states to require that architects and engineers certify their plans, and it strengthened the idea that companies must answer for the safety of what they build.

Today, a small plaque marks the spot.
