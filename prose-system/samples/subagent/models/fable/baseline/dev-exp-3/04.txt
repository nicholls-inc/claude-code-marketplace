# How two strangers can agree on a secret in public

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met and can only talk over a line that an eavesdropper, Eve, hears in full. They want to end up sharing a secret number that Eve does not know, so they can use it to scramble their later messages.

This sounds impossible. Anything Alice tells Bob, Eve hears too, so it seems Eve must end up knowing whatever Bob knows. For most of history the only answer was to meet in advance, or send a trusted courier, and hand over the secret in private.

In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a way round this. It rests on one idea: some sums are easy to do and very hard to undo.

## A picture first: mixing paint

Mixing two paints takes seconds. Separating the mixture back into the original two colours is practically impossible. That gives a way to build a shared secret.

1. Alice and Bob agree, openly, on a common colour, say yellow. Eve hears this.
2. Each picks a private colour and tells no one. Alice picks red; Bob picks blue.
3. Each mixes their private colour into the yellow. Alice gets orange; Bob gets green. They send these mixtures to each other, and Eve sees both.
4. Alice adds her red to Bob's green. Bob adds his blue to Alice's orange.

Both now hold the same colour: yellow plus red plus blue. The order of mixing makes no difference.

Eve has seen yellow, orange and green. To make the final colour she would need pure red or pure blue, and she cannot extract either from a mixture. Combining orange and green does not help, because that gives two parts yellow, which is a different colour.

The real method does the same thing with numbers.

## The numerical version of mixing: clock arithmetic

We need a sum that is easy forwards and hard backwards. It comes from remainders.

On a 12-hour clock, 5 hours after 10 o'clock is 3 o'clock, not 15. You wrap round at 12 and keep only what is left over. Mathematicians do the same with any "clock size". We will use 23: after any sum, divide by 23 and keep the remainder. So 25 becomes 2, and 50 becomes 4, because 50 = 2 × 23 + 4.

Now take the number 5 and keep multiplying by 5, wrapping round each time:

| Power of 5 | Working | Result on the 23-clock |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 5 | 5 |
| 2nd | 5 × 5 = 25 | 2 |
| 3rd | 2 × 5 = 10 | 10 |
| 4th | 10 × 5 = 50 | 4 |
| 5th | 4 × 5 = 20 | 20 |
| 6th | 20 × 5 = 100 | 8 |

The results jump about with no visible pattern: 5, 2, 10, 4, 20, 8. That is what makes the sum hard to reverse.

Going forwards is easy. If I ask for the 6th power of 5 on the 23-clock, you multiply and get 8. Going backwards is not. If I say "some power of 5 gave 8, which power?", there is no neat way to work it out. Ordinary powers grow steadily, so you can home in on the answer, but here the wrapping destroys that. You are reduced to trying powers one after another.

Raising to a power on a clock is our paint-mixing. The power you used is the private colour hidden inside the result.

## The exchange, with real numbers

**Step 1: agree the public numbers.** Alice and Bob openly agree on the clock size 23 and the starting number 5. Eve hears both.

**Step 2: each picks a private number.** Alice picks 6. Bob picks 15. Neither number is ever sent.

**Step 3: each sends a public number.** Alice works out the 6th power of 5 on the 23-clock, which the table shows is 8, and sends 8 to Bob. Bob works out the 15th power of 5 on the 23-clock, which is 19, and sends 19 to Alice. Eve hears both.

**Step 4: each combines the other's public number with their own private one.**

- Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power, 6. On the 23-clock the answer is **2**.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private power, 15. On the 23-clock the answer is **2**.

They have reached the same number, 2, and it never travelled down the line.

## Why they get the same answer

This is the paint rule again: the order of mixing does not matter.

Alice's number 8 is really "5 raised to the 6th". When Bob raises it to the 15th, he has in effect multiplied 5 by itself 6 × 15 = 90 times. Bob's number 19 is really "5 raised to the 15th". When Alice raises it to the 6th, she has multiplied 5 by itself 15 × 6 = 90 times.

Both have worked out the 90th power of 5 by different routes. Wrapping round the clock as you go does not change the final remainder, so they agree.

## Why Eve is stuck

Eve knows the clock size 23, the starting number 5, and the two public numbers 8 and 19. To get the secret she needs one of the private numbers, which means answering "which power of 5 gives 8 on the 23-clock?" That is the backwards question.

Multiplying the two public numbers together does not help her. That gives the 21st power of 5 (6 + 15), not the 90th. It is the orange-plus-green mistake.

With a clock of size 23, Eve can simply try every power and will have the answer in a minute. The protection comes from scale. In practice the clock size is a number hundreds of digits long, and the private numbers are similarly huge. Alice and Bob's forward sums stay quick, because there are shortcuts for raising to large powers: square the number, square the result, and so on, doubling the power at each step. No comparable shortcut is known for Eve's backwards sum. Trying every possibility would take the fastest machines in existence far longer than the age of the universe.

One caveat is that nobody has proved a fast backwards method cannot exist. The security rests on the fact that decades of effort by mathematicians have not found one.

## Where the name comes from

Each person has two numbers: a **private key**, which they keep (6 and 15), and a **public key**, calculated from it, which they can show to anyone (8 and 19). Publishing the public key does not reveal the private one, because that would need the backwards sum. That is the "public-key" idea.

Once Alice and Bob share their secret number, they use it as the setting for an ordinary scrambling method that garbles each message in a way only someone with the same number can reverse. This happens automatically, in a fraction of a second, whenever a padlock symbol appears beside a web address.

## One gap that remains

The method keeps a listener out. It does not tell Alice who is at the other end. If Eve can intercept and replace messages, not merely overhear them, she could pose as Bob to Alice and as Alice to Bob, agreeing a separate secret with each. So real systems add a check of identity, using related mathematics, in which a trusted organisation vouches that a public key really belongs to the person claiming it.

The central result still stands: two people can build a shared secret in full public hearing, without ever having exchanged one in private.
