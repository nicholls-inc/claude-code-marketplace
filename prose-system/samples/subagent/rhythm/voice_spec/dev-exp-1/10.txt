A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists outside the house to the inside, and it can do this even when the outside air is cold, because air at 0°C still contains a lot of heat. Heat only stops being available at absolute zero, which is about minus 273°C, so a winter day in Britain is a long way above that point.

The machine is a sealed loop containing a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature. Outside the house, a fan draws air across a coil. The refrigerant in that coil is kept colder than the air, at perhaps minus 10°C on a 2°C day. Heat flows from the warmer air into the colder refrigerant, which makes the refrigerant boil and turn into a gas. This works for the same reason a cold drink warms up on a table: heat always moves from warmer things to colder ones, so the trick is to keep the refrigerant colder than the air outside.

The gas then passes through a compressor, which is an electric pump. Squeezing a gas raises its temperature, as you notice when a bicycle pump gets warm. The compressor takes the refrigerant from a few degrees below zero to something like 50 to 60°C. At that temperature it is hotter than the water in your radiators or underfloor heating, so heat flows from the refrigerant into that water. As it gives up heat, the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid. It then passes through an expansion valve, which drops its pressure and temperature sharply, and it goes back outside to start the cycle again.

The point that matters for your decision is the amount of heat delivered for each unit of electricity. The compressor uses electricity, but most of the heat delivered was collected from the air. A good heat pump typically delivers between 2.5 and 4 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity it uses. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance, and for a whole season it is usually quoted as the seasonal performance factor. A gas boiler cannot go above about 90 to 95 per cent efficiency, which is a ratio of less than one. An electric immersion heater is exactly 1 to 1. A heat pump at 3 to 1 is therefore using about a third of the electricity to deliver the same heat.

The ratio falls as the outside air gets colder, because the compressor has to lift the refrigerant through a bigger temperature gap. At 7°C a heat pump might manage 4 to 1, and at minus 5°C perhaps 2.5 to 1. It keeps working below that, and many current models are rated to work at minus 20°C, but they use more electricity per unit of heat. The ratio also falls when the heat pump has to produce hotter water. This is why heat pumps suit houses with well-insulated walls and loft, and with radiators large enough to heat a room on water at 45 to 50°C rather than the 70°C that many gas boilers send out.

Cost per unit matters as well. In Britain, electricity has recently cost around three to four times as much per kWh as gas. A heat pump with a seasonal ratio of 3 to 1 therefore costs roughly the same to run as a gas boiler, and a ratio above that makes it cheaper. A ratio below that makes it dearer. The result depends on your tariff and on how well the system is designed for your house.

Installation costs more than a boiler, typically £10,000 to £14,000 before any grant, and the outdoor unit needs space and makes a low hum. In my view, the sensible first step is to ask an installer for a room-by-room heat loss calculation, because it shows whether your radiators and insulation suit a heat pump and what ratio you could realistically expect.
