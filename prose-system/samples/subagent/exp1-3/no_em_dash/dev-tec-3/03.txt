Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). The charger is a controlled source, and the cell's own electrochemistry decides how much of that control it can actually use.

Stage one is constant current. The charger forces a fixed current, commonly 0.5C to 1C (a 1C current would fill the nominal capacity in one hour). Lithium ions leave the cathode, cross the electrolyte and separator, and insert themselves, or intercalate, between the graphite layers of the anode. The cell's terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage (OCV) plus the current times the internal resistance, and the OCV rises as the anode fills. A typical cell climbs from roughly 3.0 V toward a 4.2 V limit. This voltage ceiling is a hard safety and longevity constraint. Above it, the electrolyte oxidizes, the cathode degrades, and at the anode, lithium can plate as metal instead of intercalating, which can form dendrites and cause short circuits.

Stage two begins when the terminal voltage reaches the ceiling. The charger now holds voltage constant and lets the current fall. This is the slowdown, and it has two causes that compound.

First, the voltage budget is fixed. The terminal voltage equals the OCV plus the resistive drop, so as the OCV approaches 4.2 V, the headroom left for the I times R term shrinks. The allowed current falls toward zero as OCV approaches the limit. A circuit analogy helps: the cell behaves like a capacitor in series with a resistor, charged from a fixed-voltage source, so current decays roughly exponentially.

Second, the physics behind that resistance gets worse near full. Lithium diffuses slowly through solid graphite, so concentration builds up at particle surfaces while the interior lags. Near full, the surface sites are nearly occupied, leaving few vacant sites to accept more ions, and the concentration gradient driving diffusion flattens. Kinetics at the interface also slow down. Pushing harder is not an option, because higher current would raise the surface potential of the anode toward the plating threshold.

Charging ends when the current drops to a cutoff, typically 0.05C to 0.1C. The last 20 percent of capacity can therefore take as long as the first 80 percent. Fast-charge designs work around this by tapering current in steps, using temperature control, or deliberately stopping near 80 percent, where the cell is still charging at a high rate.
