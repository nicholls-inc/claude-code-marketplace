Apollo 13: The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 13, 1970, the three men aboard Apollo 13 were about 200,000 miles from Earth and feeling fine. Commander Jim Lovell, lunar module pilot Fred Haise and command module pilot Jack Swigert had just finished a television broadcast showing viewers around their cramped spacecraft. Few networks carried it. Two Moon landings had already happened, and the public had begun to treat lunar flight as routine. In two days Lovell and Haise were due to land in the Fra Mauro highlands while Swigert circled overhead.

Swigert was a late addition. Three days before launch, the backup crew's Charlie Duke caught German measles, and doctors found that the prime command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had no immunity. NASA swapped Mattingly out for Swigert, his backup. Mattingly never got sick. He would spend the mission on the ground, and before it ended he would matter as much as anyone in the air.

At 55 hours and 54 minutes into the flight, Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the cryogenic tanks in the service module, the large cylinder behind the crew cabin that carried the ship's oxygen, power and main engine. Stirring kept the super-cold oxygen from settling into layers and giving false gauge readings. It was a chore the crew had done before. Swigert flipped the switches.

Seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights came on. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." When the ground asked him to repeat, Lovell answered: "Houston, we've had a problem. We've had a main B bus undervolt." One of the ship's two main electrical circuits was losing power.

At first nobody in the cabin or on the ground understood what had happened. Some controllers suspected bad instruments, since it seemed impossible for so many things to fail at once. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming away from the ship. "We are venting something out into space," he reported. Oxygen tank 2 was gone, and tank 1 was leaking. Those tanks fed the fuel cells that made the command module's electricity and water, and they supplied the crew's breathing air. The Moon landing was off. The question now was whether the crew could get home at all.

The command module, named Odyssey, was the only part of the ship built to survive reentry into Earth's atmosphere. It had batteries for that final plunge, but they held only a few hours of power and could not be wasted. So flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers decided to treat the lunar module, Aquarius, as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, water, batteries and engine. It was designed to keep two men alive for about two days on the lunar surface. Now it would have to keep three men alive for close to four days.

Lovell and Haise hurried into Aquarius and powered it up while Swigert shut down Odyssey. He finished with about fifteen minutes of power left in the failing fuel cells. The command module, cold and dark, would wait to be revived just before reentry.

The ship was headed for the Moon on a path that would not bring it back to Earth, because the crew had made a planned course change earlier to aim for their landing site. Mission Control rejected the idea of firing the service module's big engine to turn around directly. Nobody knew how badly the explosion had damaged it, and firing it might destroy the ship. Instead, the crew would swing around the Moon and use its gravity to slingshot home. About five and a half hours after the explosion, Lovell fired the lunar module's descent engine, the one meant to land on the Moon, to put them back on a "free return" path.

On April 14 Apollo 13 passed behind the far side of the Moon, about 158 miles above the surface. At that moment the crew were farther from Earth than any people had ever been, a record that still stood decades later. Haise and Swigert took photographs of the landscape they would not land on. Lovell, who had orbited the Moon on Apollo 8, was focused on the next step. Two hours after the closest approach, the crew fired the descent engine again for a longer burn that cut the trip home by about ten hours and moved the splashdown to the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships waited.

Then came the hard part: waiting, and staying alive while they waited.

To save power, almost everything was switched off. The heaters were off, and the cabins grew cold, eventually falling to about 38 degrees Fahrenheit. Moisture condensed on the walls and panels. The men wore extra layers and slept badly, if at all. Water was rationed because the lunar module's supply also cooled its electronics. Haise, drinking too little, developed a kidney infection and ran a fever.

The most urgent danger was invisible. Every breath the crew exhaled added carbon dioxide to the air, and the lunar module's filters were meant for two men over a short stay. The command module had plenty of spare filters, but they were square, and the lunar module's filter slots were round. As carbon dioxide levels climbed toward dangerous territory, engineers in Houston gathered the items they knew were on board: plastic bags, cardboard covers from the flight plan, suit hoses, socks and gray duct tape. They built an adapter that let a square filter work in the round system, then read the assembly steps to the crew over the radio. The astronauts called the result "the mailbox." When they connected it, the carbon dioxide readings began to drop.

Navigation was another headache. The spacecraft was surrounded by a glittering cloud of debris from the explosion, which made it impossible to sight stars and check their position the usual way. When a small course correction became necessary, the crew did it by hand. Lovell kept the edge of the Earth's shadow lined up in the window while Haise and Swigert called out timing, and they fired the engine for 14 seconds. It worked.

Meanwhile, Mattingly and a team of engineers worked around the clock in simulators on the ground, writing a procedure to bring the frozen command module back to life using the scarce battery power left. Every switch had to be thrown in the right order, because a single mistake could drain the batteries before reentry. They also had to account for the possibility that the cold, wet electronics would short out when turned on.

On April 17, a few hours before reaching Earth, the crew cast off the damaged service module. As it drifted away they saw for the first time what had happened: an entire side panel had been blown off, leaving wiring and equipment exposed. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. Swigert then followed Mattingly's procedure and powered up Odyssey, which came back to life. The crew moved into the command module and released Aquarius, the lifeboat that had kept them alive. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," said a voice from Mission Control.

Reentry always produced a few minutes of radio blackout as superheated air surrounded the capsule. On Apollo 13 the blackout ran longer than expected, and the silence in Mission Control and in living rooms around the world stretched out uncomfortably. Then Swigert's voice came through, and soon after the main parachutes opened. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, and the crew was picked up by the USS Iwo Jima. They were thinner, exhausted and, in Haise's case, sick, but they were alive.

A review board later traced the accident to oxygen tank 2. Years earlier, a design change had raised the voltage used by the tank's heaters, but small safety switches inside the tank were never upgraded to match. During a ground test before launch, the heaters ran for hours, the switches welded shut, and temperatures inside the tank climbed high enough to damage the insulation on its wiring. When Swigert stirred the tank in flight, a spark ignited it.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon. NASA came to call it a "successful failure." The crew and the people on the ground had faced a disaster nobody had planned for, and they improvised their way through it until the three men were home.
