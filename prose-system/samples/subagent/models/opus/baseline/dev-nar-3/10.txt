The Great Molasses Flood

January 15, 1919, was unusually mild for a Boston winter. The day before, the temperature had stood near 2 degrees Fahrenheit. By noon it had climbed to about 40, and people in the North End were outside enjoying it. Children played near the waterfront. Workers took their lunch in the sun. Men from the city's paving department ate in their yard on Commercial Street.

Above them stood a steel tank about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The molasses was shipped in from the Caribbean and turned into industrial alcohol, which was used to make munitions, among other things. The tank had been built in a hurry in 1915. It had never been properly tested, and from the start it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping molasses to take home. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown so the streaks would show less.

At about 12:30 p.m., witnesses heard a deep rumble and a sound like machine-gun fire as the rivets popped loose. Then the tank split open.

A wave of molasses, at first perhaps 25 feet high, poured into the street at an estimated 35 miles per hour. It hit with enough force to bend the steel girders of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue and nearly knocked a train off the tracks. It flattened houses and knocked a firehouse off its foundation. Horses, wagons and people were picked up and thrown. A large section of the tank's steel wall landed in a playground.

The worst part came after the wave passed. Molasses is thick, and in the cold air it grew thicker. People who survived the first impact were trapped in a sticky mass that held them like quicksand. The harder they struggled, the more tired they became. Some drowned. Others were crushed under wreckage they could not climb out of.

Rescuers came quickly. Sailors from a navy training ship docked nearby ran into the mess, along with police, firefighters and Red Cross volunteers. They waded through waist-deep molasses to pull people free. It was slow, exhausting work, and the search for bodies went on for days. Twenty-one people died, among them children and a ten-year-old boy who had been gathering firewood. About 150 more were injured.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats, which cut through the molasses better than fresh water, and scrubbed the area with sand. Boston Harbor ran brown into the summer. Longtime residents said that on hot days the North End smelled sweet for decades afterward.

The company first claimed the tank had been blown up by anarchists. Survivors and families sued, and the case dragged on for years. In 1925 a court-appointed auditor ruled that the tank had been too weak for its load and that the company was responsible. United States Industrial Alcohol paid about $628,000 in damages, a large sum at the time.

The disaster had a lasting effect on how buildings are made. Massachusetts, and later other states, began requiring that engineers check and sign off on the plans for large structures. Today a small plaque on Commercial Street marks the spot where one of the strangest disasters in American history took place.
