Imagine you want to agree on a secret with someone you have never met, but the only way to reach them is by shouting across a crowded room where everyone can hear every word. It sounds impossible, because a secret that has been spoken aloud is no longer secret. For most of history, people who wanted to communicate privately had to meet beforehand and agree on a shared code, or key, and then guard it carefully. That works for spies and soldiers, but it does not work for a shopper who wants to send a card number to a website they have never visited. In the 1970s, mathematicians found a surprising way around the problem, and the idea behind it is simpler than you might expect.

The easiest way to see the trick is with paint. Suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud on a starting colour, say yellow, and everybody in the room hears it. Alice secretly picks a colour of her own, perhaps red, and mixes it into a pot of the yellow. Bob secretly picks a different colour, maybe blue, and does the same with his own pot. Each of them then sends the mixed result across the room for all to see. Alice now holds Bob's mixture, and she stirs her own secret red into it, while Bob takes Alice's mixture and stirs in his secret blue. Both end up with exactly the same final colour, because each has combined yellow, red and blue, and the order in which you mix paint makes no difference to the result. The eavesdropper has seen the yellow and both of the mixtures, but nobody can unmix paint to discover which colour went into it, so the eavesdropper cannot reproduce the final shade.

Mathematics needs an operation that behaves like mixing paint, which means one that is easy to do in one direction and practically impossible to undo. Clock arithmetic provides exactly that. On a clock, if it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, you do not get fifteen o'clock but three, because you wrap around after twelve. Mathematicians call this working with remainders: you divide by the size of the clock and keep only what is left over. We can use a clock of any size we like, and for our example we will use one with twenty-three positions. Dividing 100 by 23 gives 4 with 8 left over, so on this clock the number 100 simply lands on 8.

The second ingredient is repeated multiplication, usually called taking a power. Writing 5 to the power of 3 means multiplying 5 by itself three times, which gives 125. If we combine the two ideas, we can ask for the remainder after dividing a power of 5 by 23. For instance, 5 multiplied by itself twice is 25, which lands on 2 on our clock, and from there the numbers continue to jump around in a pattern that looks quite random. Working forwards, from the power to the remainder, is quick and mechanical. Working backwards is the hard part. If I tell you that some power of 5 landed on 8 and ask which power it was, you have no shortcut, and you must try the powers one at a time until you hit the right answer.

With our tiny clock that guessing takes seconds, but the real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits in their size, and the search then becomes hopeless. Even if every computer on Earth worked on the problem together, they would not finish in any span of time that matters to a human being. Going forwards stays easy, because there are clever methods that keep the numbers small by taking remainders at every step, and that asymmetry is what we have been looking for.

Now we can replay the paint story with numbers. Alice and Bob publicly agree on the clock size, 23, and on the starting number, 5. Alice secretly chooses the number 6. She works out 5 to the power of 6, which is 15,625, and finds its remainder on the 23-hour clock, which is 8. She announces 8 to the whole room. Bob secretly chooses 15, works out 5 to the power of 15 on the same clock, and gets 19, which he announces. Each of them has now published a mixture while keeping the original ingredient hidden. The eavesdropper has heard 23, 5, 8 and 19, but not 6 or 15.

Next comes the step that corresponds to adding the second colour. Alice takes Bob's announced number, 19, raises it to her secret power of 6, and reduces the answer on the clock, which gives 2. Bob takes Alice's announced number, 8, raises it to his secret power of 15, and reduces it in the same way, which also gives 2. They have arrived at the same number without ever sending it, and that number, 2, is their shared secret. You can check the arithmetic yourself with a calculator, although the numbers get large, and it is worth doing once to see that the two paths really do meet.

The reason they meet is a fact you learned at school: when you raise a power to another power, the powers multiply together. Alice's journey amounts to 5 raised to the power of 15 times 6, and Bob's amounts to 5 raised to the power of 6 times 15, and since multiplying 6 by 15 gives the same answer as multiplying 15 by 6, both journeys end in the same place. The remainders on the clock do not disturb this, because taking remainders along the way gives the same result as taking one at the end. That is the mathematical version of saying that the order of mixing does not matter.

The eavesdropper, meanwhile, is stuck. To copy what Alice or Bob did, they would need one of the secret powers, and to find it they would have to solve the backwards problem of working out which power of 5 produced 8 or 19. With a clock of our size they could simply try every possibility and succeed within a minute, but with a clock of hundreds of digits that route is closed to them. Nobody has found a fast general method for doing it, although nobody has proved that such a method cannot exist, so the safety of the scheme rests on a very well-tested belief rather than on a certainty.

People often describe this arrangement in terms of public and private keys. The numbers that Alice and Bob shout across the room, such as 8 and 19, are the public parts, which anybody may see, while the numbers they keep hidden, 6 and 15, are the private parts. Related schemes, the best known being RSA, use a similar one-way trick built on the difficulty of splitting a huge number back into the two large primes that were multiplied to make it. In those schemes a person can publish a public key that lets anybody lock a message, while only the owner of the matching private key can unlock it, rather like a padlock that anyone can snap shut but only one person holds the key to open.

In everyday life you meet all this whenever your browser shows a padlock beside a web address. Your device and the website quickly run an exchange like the one above, and they end up with a shared secret that nobody watching the connection could have worked out. They then use that secret as the key for a much faster, ordinary scrambling method to protect the rest of the conversation, because the clock arithmetic is too slow for large amounts of data.

There is one weakness worth knowing about. Nothing in the exchange proves who is on the other end, so a clever attacker standing in the middle could play Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, creating two separate secrets and quietly relaying everything between them. To defend against this, websites carry certificates, which are digital statements vouched for by organisations that browsers already trust, and these let your device confirm that it is really talking to the site it meant to reach. Together, the one-way arithmetic and the identity checks allow two strangers to build a private channel in public, which is the thing that once seemed impossible.
