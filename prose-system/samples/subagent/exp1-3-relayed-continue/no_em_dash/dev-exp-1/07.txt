A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists. That is why it can warm your home when it is colder outside than inside.

Cold is relative. Air at 0 C still holds a great deal of thermal energy, because heat only vanishes completely at absolute zero, about minus 273 C. Even a freezing winter day contains heat that can be collected, provided a machine can concentrate it.

How the machine works

A heat pump is a refrigerator run in reverse. It contains a sealed loop of refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, often well below minus 20 C. The loop has four main parts.

1. The outdoor coil. A fan pulls outside air across it. The refrigerant inside is colder than the air, so heat flows into it and the liquid boils into a gas.
2. The compressor. It squeezes the gas, which raises its pressure and sharply raises its temperature, typically to 50 C or more. This is the same effect that warms a bicycle pump as you use it.
3. The indoor coil. The hot gas passes through water in your radiators or underfloor heating, or through air in a ducted system. The gas is hotter than that water, so it releases heat and turns back into a liquid.
4. The expansion valve. The liquid is released into a lower pressure, which cools it well below the outdoor temperature, and the cycle starts again.

Heat naturally flows only from hot to cold. The compressor makes that work in your favour by changing the temperatures inside the loop. The refrigerant is made colder than the outdoors so it can collect heat, then hotter than your radiators so it can deliver it. The electricity runs the compressor and fans. It is not the source of most of the heat.

Why this can beat a boiler

A gas boiler converts fuel to heat at best around 90 to 95 percent efficiency, so one unit of gas gives less than one unit of heat. A heat pump delivers roughly three to four units of heat for each unit of electricity, because most of the output was collected from outside. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance, or COP. Seasonal figures of 3 to 4 are common for well installed systems.

The catch: colder days and hotter radiators

Performance falls as the outdoor temperature drops, because the compressor must lift heat across a bigger gap. At minus 10 C a modern unit may achieve a COP of about 2, which is still better than a boiler but below the mild-weather figure. Many current models keep running down to minus 20 C or lower.

The temperature of your heating water matters just as much. Water at 35 to 45 C is efficient. Water at 70 C, which radiators designed for boilers often expect, drags the COP down. Heat pumps work best with larger radiators or underfloor heating in a house that holds its heat.

What this means for your decision

- Insulation. A heat loss survey will size the unit correctly. Improving loft and wall insulation first often pays off, and it lets you buy a smaller, cheaper unit.
- Emitters. You may need some larger radiators and a new hot water cylinder.
- Running costs. Electricity usually costs more per unit than gas, so savings depend on your local price ratio. If electricity costs less than roughly three times as much as gas, a heat pump with a COP of 3 or more is usually cheaper to run. Smart or time-of-use tariffs help further.
- Upfront cost. Installation typically costs more than a new boiler, though grants may cover part of it where you live.
- Space and noise. You need an outdoor spot with good airflow, and the unit makes a low hum.

Heat pumps are proven in cold climates such as Norway, Finland and Canada. The physics works in winter. The real question is whether your house, your radiators and your electricity tariff make the numbers add up. Ask two or three installers for a heat loss survey and a quote, and compare the projected running costs with your current gas bills before you decide.
