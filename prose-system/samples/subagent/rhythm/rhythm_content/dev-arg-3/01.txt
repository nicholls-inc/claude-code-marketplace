Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it deliberately, with time set aside for it in the first years of school, rather than leaving it to emerge from whatever a child happens to pick up. The case against is not foolish, and it deserves a fair statement before I answer it. Children now grow up among keyboards and touchscreens, nearly every adult writes by hand only for shopping lists and birthday cards, and a crowded curriculum has to find its hours somewhere. Several American states and a number of school systems elsewhere have at various times dropped cursive from their required standards, and Finland, which is often held up as a model of sensible education policy, moved away from compulsory cursive in 2016 in favour of print and typing. If the purpose of handwriting is to get words onto a page, and a keyboard does that faster and more legibly for most people most of the time, then teaching children to join their letters can look like instruction in a craft that has outlived its use, no more necessary than teaching them to trim a quill.

That argument runs together two different questions, however, which are whether handwriting still matters and whether the joined form of it matters. On the first the evidence is considerably stronger than the sceptics tend to allow. Studies of young children who learn letters by drawing them, such as the work of Karin James and colleagues at Indiana University, have found that the act of forming a letter with a pen activates reading-related regions of the brain in a way that typing or tracing the same letter does not, and that children who practise by hand recognise letters more reliably afterwards. Virginia Berninger's long programme of research on writing in school-age children found that the fluency of handwriting predicted the length and quality of their compositions, which is plausible on its face because a child who has to concentrate on forming each letter has less attention left over for deciding what to say. The much-quoted finding that students taking notes by hand understand lectures better than those typing has weakened under attempts to replicate it, and I would not rest a case on it, but the broader picture, in which handwriting supports early literacy and frees working memory for composition, has held up across many studies and many countries.

If handwriting should be taught, then the remaining question is what kind, and here the case for joined-up script rests on something humbler than neuroscience, namely the practical business of writing a great deal by hand, quickly, for years. A child who prints every letter lifts the pen between each one, and a page of print is therefore a long series of separate strokes, each beginning and ending with a pause. Joined writing turns a word into a single movement, and when it is taught well the hand learns common letter sequences such as the ending of a verb or the double consonant in the middle of a word as units of motion, in much the way a pianist learns a phrase and not a row of single notes. Teachers who work with children who struggle, and in particular with dyslexic children, often report that this unified movement helps spelling to stick, because the word is stored in the hand as well as in the eye, and the lack of any gaps between letters removes the common print errors of reversed letters and uneven spacing. I should be honest that the research comparing print and cursive for speed and legibility is mixed, and that several studies find little difference once children have had equal practice in each. That finding is the reason I treat joined writing as a sensible default rather than a miracle, but a default matters a great deal when millions of children are being taught, and the sensible default for a skill that will be used at speed is the form that was designed for speed.

Speed matters because the pen has not left the school. Pupils in England sit their GCSE and A-level examinations by hand, as do students in most countries, for papers that run to hours and demand pages of continuous prose, and a candidate who writes slowly or illegibly is penalised by a bottleneck that has nothing to do with what they know. Examiners have long reported that handwriting which cannot be read costs marks, and nobody who has watched a seventeen-year-old's hand cramp in the final half-hour of a three-hour paper will think this is a trivial matter. Children who have been taught a fluent, efficient joined hand by the age of eight or nine arrive at those papers with a tool that does not fail them, and children who were taught nothing in particular often arrive with an improvised, laborious, half-print half-scrawl that they invented for themselves and never improved. The argument that keyboards will take over the examination hall is one I accept as likely in the long run, but it has been likely for twenty years without happening, and a policy for today's seven-year-olds has to be built for the examinations they will face in ten years' time rather than for the ones some reformer hopes will exist.

There is also the plain matter of reading. A person who has never been taught cursive cannot read it, and a great deal of writing, past and present, is cursive: the letters of grandparents, the comments a teacher scribbles in the margin of an essay, the notes left on a kitchen table, and the primary documents that historians and genealogists and ordinary curious people want to see for themselves. An American teacher recounted a few years ago that some of her students could not read the Declaration of Independence in its original form, and whether or not that particular story has been polished in the retelling, it points to a real loss, because a culture that stops teaching its children to read its own handwriting turns every handwritten record into something that requires a translator. Learning to write a script is much the best way of learning to read it, since the child who has formed a letter a hundred times will recognise it in anyone's hand, and it is a modest enough price to ask for access to the whole written inheritance of the language.

The objection from time deserves a direct reply, because it is the objection that actually drives policy. Schools are short of hours, and every minute given to handwriting is a minute taken from something else. The cost, though, is much smaller than the debate suggests: a few short, regular sessions a week over two or three years is the amount most schools that teach joined writing currently give, and the return on those minutes is a skill that serves a child in every subject for the rest of their schooling. What the objection also overlooks is that typing, the usual proposed replacement, is a skill children acquire with remarkably little help, because they meet keyboards and screens at home, in the playground, in the ordinary traffic of life, and the evidence that formal touch-typing lessons in primary school produce lasting gains over the child's own gradual improvement is thin. Handwriting is exactly the opposite kind of skill, one that does not arise spontaneously and that children left to themselves develop badly, so that the school is the only place where it will be taught properly if it is taught at all. A curriculum that spends its scarce hours on what children will learn anyway, and cuts what they will not, has its priorities the wrong way round.

Equity points the same direction. Not every household can put a laptop or a tablet in front of every child, and a school that shifted its teaching of writing onto devices would be building its early literacy on a resource that its poorest pupils have least of. Paper and a pencil are the cheapest and most reliable writing technology there is, they need no charging and no connection and no licence, and a child who writes fluently with them can work anywhere and be assessed anywhere. Handwriting taught well is therefore a form of insurance for exactly the children who are least able to buy another one, and any reform that weakens it will take that insurance away from them first.

None of this requires the schoolroom of the old copybook, with its marks for the elegance of a loop and its rap on the knuckles for a crooked slope. The sensible aim is a fluent, legible, reasonably fast hand, taught with a simple model in the first years, with joins introduced once the individual letters are secure, and then left to develop a personal character as the child grows, as every adult's handwriting does. Children should be free to print capitals and to drop joins where their own hand prefers, since the research that supports joined writing supports fluency and automaticity and says nothing in favour of uniformity. The mistake would be to let the reasonable doubts about calligraphy and rigid conformity persuade us that the whole enterprise is dispensable, when the evidence on early literacy, on composition, on examinations, on reading and on fairness all points toward children being taught, patiently and early, to write a connected hand.

So the answer to the question is yes, and the reason is the one that has justified teaching any foundational skill. The point is that a child who can write fluently, and read what others have written, owns a tool for thinking and remembering that does not depend on anyone's battery, and a school that withholds it in order to make room for fashions in technology gives up something solid for something that was never in danger of being lost. We can teach keyboards as well, and should, but we should not mistake the arrival of a new way of writing for the obsolescence of the old one, which is still the first way most children will put their thoughts on paper and the way they will be asked to do it when it counts.
