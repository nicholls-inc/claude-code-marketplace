On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unseasonably warm afternoon. The temperature had climbed to around forty degrees after a bitter cold snap, and the neighborhood's mostly Italian and Irish working families went about their business. Children played near the waterfront. Workers ate lunch. Beside Commercial Street loomed a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, holding more than two million gallons of molasses.

The tank was only a few years old, and it had never been treated kindly. Built quickly to profit from wartime demand for industrial alcohol, which was used in munitions, it was never properly tested. When first filled, it leaked so badly that the company simply painted it brown to hide the seeping syrup. Neighbors collected the drips in cans. Workers had heard strange groans from the structure, yet no one acted on the warnings. The steel was thinner than it should have been, and the rivets were weak.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun as rivets popped, followed by a rumble. Then the tank burst open, and a wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high at its crest surged out through the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles per hour.

The wave tore buildings from their foundations. It bent the elevated railway's supports and knocked a train car off its track. It crushed a firehouse, trapping men inside, and swept wagons, horses and people along in a brown tide. Those caught in it found the thick syrup nearly impossible to escape. It cooled quickly in the winter air and grew stickier as it did, gluing victims in place. Rescuers waded in to reach them, sinking to their knees. Some of the dead were so thickly coated that they were hard to recognize.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Horses perished in the street. Rescuers, including sailors from a nearby ship and Red Cross workers, labored through the afternoon and into the night. Clearing the mess took weeks. Crews sprayed saltwater from fireboats and spread sand and sawdust to soak it up. The harbor ran brown well into the summer, and for decades, people claimed the North End still smelled sweet on hot days.

The legal aftermath proved just as important as the disaster itself. The company blamed anarchists and claimed the tank had been sabotaged. After a lengthy class action lawsuit, one of the first of its kind in Massachusetts, a court-appointed auditor concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound and that the company was responsible. It eventually paid roughly half a million dollars in settlements to victims' families and others.

The flood contributed to reforms in the building trade. Massachusetts tightened requirements for engineering oversight, and architects and engineers were expected to sign off on their plans. A strange disaster, it led to lasting changes in how large structures are designed and approved.
