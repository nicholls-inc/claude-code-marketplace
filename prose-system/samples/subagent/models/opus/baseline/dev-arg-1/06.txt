**Let the Market Park the Car: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements**

Somewhere in your city's zoning code is a table that decides more about how your neighborhood looks than any architect or mayor does. It says a restaurant needs a certain number of parking spaces for every hundred square feet of dining room. An apartment building needs one or two spaces per unit. A church needs one for every few seats. Most of these numbers were set decades ago, often copied from neighboring towns or from manuals built on thin data, and almost nobody now alive chose them. Cities should delete them.

Minimum parking requirements are meant to solve a real worry. If a new building doesn't supply its own parking, its tenants and customers will fill the curbs nearby and annoy the neighbors. That concern is fair. The remedy has turned out to be far worse than the problem.

Start with cost. A parking space is not free just because nobody pays at the gate. A surface space uses about 300 square feet of land once you count the aisles. A space in a garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one can cost much more. When the law requires those spaces, the cost goes into rents, mortgages and the price of a sandwich. Everyone pays it, including people who don't own a car. In most American cities a meaningful share of households have no vehicle, and they tend to be poorer, older or younger than average. Mandatory parking quietly makes them subsidize drivers.

Then look at what never gets built. On a small urban lot, the required garage often decides whether a project can work at all. A developer who could fit twenty apartments may be able to fit only twelve once the ramps and stalls are squeezed in, or may walk away. Small infill buildings, corner shops on old lots and conversions of historic buildings are hit hardest, because they can't spread a parking podium across a large footprint. In the middle of a housing shortage, we are using the zoning code to forbid homes in order to protect space for cars.

The requirements also shape cities physically. Ask why so many commercial strips are a row of buildings stranded behind seas of asphalt, and the answer is usually in the code. Required lots push buildings apart, which makes walking unpleasant, which makes people drive, which seems to prove that all the parking was needed. The rule creates the demand it claims to measure. The urban planner Donald Shoup, whose book *The High Cost of Free Parking* made this case at length, pointed out that many minimums are set to meet peak demand on the busiest day of the year. For the other 364 days, much of that pavement sits empty while still adding heat, runoff and distance.

The evidence from cities that have changed course is encouraging. Buffalo dropped citywide parking minimums in 2017. Minneapolis followed in 2021, and other cities, from Auckland to a growing list of American cities, have removed or sharply cut theirs. California has barred parking mandates near major transit. The sky has not fallen. Developers in these places still build parking, because many tenants and customers want it and lenders still expect it. They just build closer to what people actually use, and some projects that would have been impossible under the old rules now get built. Abolishing minimums doesn't ban parking. It takes the decision away from a decades-old table and gives it back to the people who pay for the spaces and know who will use them.

The strongest objection is spillover: the fear that residents of a new building with little parking will take all the curb space on surrounding streets. That deserves a direct answer, and there is one. Curb space is public property, and cities already have tools to manage it. Residential permit zones, meters priced to keep a few spaces open on each block, and time limits all deal with crowded curbs far more precisely than forcing every building to supply its own lot. Shoup's other big idea, charging the right price for on-street parking and spending the money on the block that generates it, gives neighbors a reason to welcome that solution instead of fighting it. Managing the curb is cheaper than mandating garages, and it targets the actual problem.

Some will say that removing minimums is an attack on drivers. It isn't. A driver who wants a space can still rent or buy one, and businesses that depend on drivers will still provide parking because it's in their interest. What ends is the requirement that everyone else pay for it too, whether they use it or not.

Cities don't make bakeries build a minimum number of ovens or require offices to install a set number of elevators per floor. They trust owners to provide what their customers need. Parking should be treated the same way. Scrapping parking minimums costs little and asks for no new spending. It could lower housing costs and make streets more walkable, which few other reforms can do as cheaply. Cities should delete that table.
