Two people who have never met, whom I will call Asha and Ben, want to agree on a secret number that they can use as a key to scramble their messages. The only link between them is a line that anyone can listen to, and a stranger called Eve is recording every word that crosses it. This seems impossible, because whatever Asha tells Ben, Eve hears too. In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a method that solves it, with earlier work by Ralph Merkle behind it, and it now runs every time a phone connects to a secure website. The method depends on a kind of calculation that is quick to do in one direction and very slow to undo.

The calculation uses clock arithmetic. Most people know that on a twelve-hour clock, five hours after ten o'clock is three o'clock, because the count wraps round after twelve. We can build a clock with any number of hours. The example here uses a clock with 23 hours. On it, 7 plus 20 is 27, and since 27 is one full turn of 23 plus 4, the answer is 4. In general you divide by 23 and keep the remainder. Mathematicians call this working modulo 23, but nothing more is needed than dividing and keeping what is left over.

Now take powers. Five to the power of 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. Dividing 125 by 23 gives 5 with 115 used up, so the remainder is 10. On a 23-hour clock, 5 to the power of 3 lands on 10. Doing this forwards is easy. Pick the base, pick the power, multiply, and keep the remainder.

Going backwards is the hard part. Suppose someone tells you that 5 raised to some power lands on 8 on the 23-hour clock and asks which power it was. With a clock this small you can simply try 1, 2, 3 and so on, and you will find the answer, which is 6, after six attempts. The trouble is that the same trial-and-error gets far slower as the clock grows. The remainders jump about in no pattern that helps you guess, so seeing that a power landed on 8 tells you nothing about whether the power was near 6 or near 600. For a very large clock, no one has found a fast way to work backwards, and the best known methods would take longer than the age of the universe.

Here is how Asha and Ben use this. First they agree in the open on two numbers, the size of the clock and a base to raise to powers. These are 23 and 5, and they are not secret. Eve hears them, and that does no harm. Next, Asha picks a private number that she tells no one, say 6. She works out 5 to the power of 6 on the clock. That is 15,625, and dividing by 23 leaves 8. She sends Ben the number 8. Ben picks his own private number, say 15, and works out 5 to the power of 15 on the clock, which comes to 19. He sends Asha the number 19. In the language of public-key cryptography, 6 is Asha's private key and 8 is her public key, and 15 and 19 are the same pair for Ben.

Now each of them uses what they received. Asha takes Ben's 19 and raises it to her private power of 6. Working on the 23-hour clock, 19 squared is 361, which leaves 16. Squaring 16 gives 256, which leaves 3, so 19 to the power of 4 is 3. Then 19 to the power of 6 is 16 times 3, which is 48, and that leaves 2. Asha's answer is 2. Ben takes Asha's 8 and raises it to his private power of 15. Carrying out the same steps, he also ends with 2. They now share the number 2, and neither of them ever sent it.

The reason they agree is that powers of powers multiply. Ben's public number is 5 to the power of 15, and Asha raises it to the power of 6, so she has computed 5 to the power of 15 times 6, which is 5 to the power of 90. Asha's public number is 5 to the power of 6, and Ben raises it to the power of 15, so he has computed 5 to the power of 6 times 15, which is also 5 to the power of 90. Multiplication gives the same result in either order, and the clock wrapping round does not change that. Each person has reached the same place by a different road, and each road needed one private number that only that person knew.

Consider what Eve has. She heard 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the shared secret she needs either Asha's 6 or Ben's 15. To get Asha's 6 she must work out which power of 5 lands on 8, which is the backwards problem. On this tiny clock she can do that in moments, which is why 23 is only a teaching example. Knowing 8 and 19 separately does not let her combine them into 2, because the combining step needs one of the private powers.

Real systems use the same steps with much larger numbers. A clock size of 2,048 bits, which is a number of about 617 decimal digits, is common, and the private powers are similarly huge. A computer carries out the forward calculation in a fraction of a second. Nobody has found a way to do the backward calculation on a clock that size in any realistic time. It has not been proved that no fast method exists, so the security rests on decades of failed attempts by mathematicians rather than on a proof. Many systems now use a variation that replaces the clock with points on a curve, which gives the same protection with much shorter numbers.

There is one weakness that the clock does not fix. The method protects Asha and Ben against an Eve who only listens. It does not tell Asha that the person she is exchanging numbers with is really Ben. If Eve can intercept the line and answer each of them in the other's name, she can agree one secret with Asha and another with Ben, and pass messages between them while reading everything. To stop this, websites prove who they are with digital certificates, which are signed statements from organisations that browsers already trust. That step relies on the same kind of one-way mathematics, and it is what turns a private conversation into a private conversation with the right person.
