In the last days of August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district of central London. Cholera is an infection of the intestine that causes violent diarrhoea and vomiting. A patient can lose so much fluid that the skin turns grey and blue, and many people died within a day or two of the first symptoms. London had already been through epidemics in 1832 and 1848, and thousands had died in each. Most doctors believed the disease was carried by foul air, which they called miasma, rising from rubbish, sewage and damp ground. The bacterium that causes cholera had not been identified, and the idea that germs cause disease was not yet accepted.

John Snow, a physician who lived at 54 Frith Street in Soho, held a different view. He was born in York in 1813, the son of a labourer, and had trained as a surgeon's apprentice before becoming a doctor in London. He was best known for his work on anaesthesia and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during the birth of her son Leopold in 1853. In 1849 he had published a short essay arguing that cholera was an illness of the gut, that people caught it by swallowing something, and that it spread through water fouled by the waste of the sick. Few doctors agreed with him. He had no direct proof, and he was looking for it.

The outbreak began around 31 August and was concentrated in a few streets near Broad Street, now called Broadwick Street. Within three days 127 people had died. Within ten days the number was close to five hundred, and many residents had left the area. Snow lived a short walk from the worst of it. He obtained the list of deaths from the local registrar, visited the houses, and asked families what they had eaten and where they had got their water. Most households in Soho took water from public pumps, and there were thirteen of them in the district.

Snow marked each death on a street map as a black bar at the address where it happened. The bars were thickest around the pump on Broad Street. For most of the dead, that pump was the nearest one. When he asked, he found that in several households where the nearest pump was a different one, the family had still preferred the Broad Street water and sent children to fetch it. He also looked for places where people lived close to the pump and did not fall ill. A workhouse on Poland Street housed 535 inmates and recorded only five deaths. It had its own well on the premises. A brewery on Broad Street employed about seventy men, and none of them had cholera. They had their own well too, and they were given malt liquor to drink during the day.

One case took him further afield. A widow in Hampstead, several miles to the north, died of cholera on 2 September. She had once lived in Broad Street, and she liked the taste of its water so much that a cart brought a bottle of it to her house each day. A niece who drank from the same bottle also died. Nobody else in her neighbourhood was ill.

On 7 September Snow presented his findings to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, which was responsible for the area. The Board ordered the handle taken off the Broad Street pump the next day, so that nobody could draw water from it. The deaths were already falling by then, probably because so many people had fled and because the sick had died. Snow said as much himself. It is often told that removing the handle ended the epidemic, but the figures do not support that, and in my view the episode matters more for how the evidence was gathered than for what the Board did.

The pump's source was still unexplained. The Reverend Henry Whitehead, a curate in the parish who had doubted Snow's theory, began his own enquiries and found that a baby girl at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with diarrhoea in the last days of August and died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the baby's nappies and emptied the water into a cesspool in front of the house. Examination of the well later showed that the cesspool was only a few feet away and that the brickwork of the well was cracked, so waste could seep into the water people were drinking.

Snow published a second edition of his book on cholera in 1855, with the map included. A committee of the General Board of Health reviewed the outbreak and decided that the evidence did not show water to be the cause. Snow died of a stroke in 1858, aged 45, still without general agreement. The cholera bacterium was named by Robert Koch in 1883, after which his account was widely accepted, and later public health work on clean water and sewage drew on the method he had used in Soho.
