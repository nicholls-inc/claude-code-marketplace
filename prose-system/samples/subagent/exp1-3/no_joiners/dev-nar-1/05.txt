In the last days of August 1854, a terrible sickness fell on Soho, a crowded and noisy district of London. Within three days, more than a hundred people living near Broad Street were dead. By the end of the outbreak, over six hundred in the neighbourhood had died. The illness was cholera, which causes violent vomiting and diarrhoea and can drain the life out of a healthy adult in a single day. Londoners had already suffered through earlier waves of it, and nobody knew where it came from.

Almost everyone of standing believed they did know. The accepted view was that cholera spread through miasma, a poisonous foul air rising from rotting rubbish, damp soil and filthy streets. It seemed sensible. The poorest districts stank, and they were also where the sick were found. Doctors, officials and newspapers all repeated the idea as settled truth.

One man disagreed. John Snow was a physician who lived and worked only a short walk from Broad Street. He was the son of a labourer from York, quiet, careful and a teetotaller who drank only water that he had filtered. He had already made his name by giving chloroform to women in childbirth, including Queen Victoria herself. Snow was a man who trusted measurement over reputation. Years earlier, he had formed a different theory about cholera. He believed it was swallowed, not breathed in. Something in contaminated food or drink, he thought, passed into the gut and multiplied there. In 1849 he had published his argument, but few people paid it any attention.

When the Broad Street outbreak began, Snow saw his chance to test his idea. He went door to door, asking families where the dead had lived and what they had drunk. He drew up a list of the deaths and then marked each one as a small black bar on a street map, stacked up at the address of each victim. Soon a pattern leapt off the page. The bars crowded thickly around one spot, a public water pump on Broad Street. In those days, people without plumbing fetched their water from pumps like this one. Snow found that nearly all of the victims lived close to it and had drunk from it.

Snow was careful to look for cases that did not fit. Those proved the most revealing. Close to the pump stood a workhouse housing more than five hundred poor residents, yet only a handful of them died. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own private well, so its residents never needed the Broad Street pump. A brewery on nearby Broad Street also escaped almost untouched. The workers there were given a daily allowance of beer, and they also had a well on the premises, so they scarcely touched the pump water at all.

Then came a stranger case still. A widow who lived in Hampstead, far from Soho, died of cholera, and so did her niece. It seemed impossible, until Snow tracked down the facts. The widow had once lived in Broad Street and loved the taste of its water. Each day she had a large bottle of it carried out to her in Hampstead. She had drunk from it just before she fell ill.

Snow took his evidence to the Board of Guardians, the local officials who ran the parish. They were not convinced of his theory, but the proof was striking enough that they agreed to act. On the eighth of September, the handle was removed from the Broad Street pump. Without a handle, nobody could draw water from it.

The sickness did fade soon after, though it is fair to say it had already been falling as many residents had fled the district. That left room for doubt, and officials did not instantly accept Snow's conclusions. The cause of the infection was still missing. A local curate named Henry Whitehead had begun as a sceptic, convinced Snow was wrong. But he was a thorough man too, and he began his own investigation among the neighbours he knew so well. He found what Snow had not. A baby girl living at number forty Broad Street had fallen ill with diarrhoea just before the outbreak began, and her mother had rinsed the soiled nappies into a leaking cesspool beside the well. Dirty water had been seeping into the pump's supply. The starting point had been found.

Snow died in 1858, aged only forty five, still without full recognition. Yet his work slowly proved its worth. Later scientists identified the bacterium that causes cholera and confirmed beyond doubt that it travels in contaminated water. Cities began to build proper sewers and to protect their drinking supplies. Snow's method of gathering facts, mapping them and following the pattern is now seen as the birth of modern epidemiology, the study of how disease moves through populations. Millions of lives have been saved because one patient man chose to knock on doors and count the dead.
