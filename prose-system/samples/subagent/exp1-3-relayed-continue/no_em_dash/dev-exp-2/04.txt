A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to the world's official clock time every so often, so that our clocks stay in step with the spinning Earth.

To see why it exists, you need to know that there are two ways to measure a day. The first is astronomical: how long the Earth takes to turn once. The second is atomic: counting the extremely regular vibrations of atoms, which gives a second that never wavers. Since 1972, the world has run on Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC, which is built from atomic clocks but is supposed to stay within a second of the Earth's rotation.

The trouble is that the Earth is a poor timekeeper. Tides drag on its spin, the molten core sloshes, and earthquakes and shifting ice nudge it around. On average the planet has been slowing slightly, so a day is a little longer than the 86,400 atomic seconds the clocks count. Left alone, atomic time would slowly race ahead of the sun. To close the gap, international timekeepers inserted a leap second when needed, making the final minute of a day 61 seconds long. The clock reads 23:59:59, then 23:59:60, then midnight. Between 1972 and 2016, 27 of them were added, most recently on New Year's Eve 2016.

So why stop? The main reason is that computers dislike them. Most software assumes every minute has exactly 60 seconds. A minute with 61 can confuse systems that rely on exact timing, such as financial exchanges, telecommunications networks, satellite navigation and data centres. Leap seconds have caused outages and glitches, including crashes at major websites in 2012. Because they are announced only a few months ahead and arrive at irregular intervals, they cannot be programmed in neatly. Some tech companies instead "smear" the extra second across a whole day, but each firm does it differently, which creates its own inconsistencies.

There is also a recent twist. For reasons scientists do not fully understand, the Earth has lately been spinning slightly faster, not slower. That raises the possibility of a negative leap second, where a second is removed. Nobody has ever tried that, and engineers fear it could cause even worse problems than adding one.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of units, voted to abandon leap seconds by 2035. The gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation will be allowed to grow, to a size to be decided later, perhaps a minute or more, before any correction is considered, possibly not for a century. For most people the change will be invisible. Noon will still be close to when the sun is highest. Only astronomers and a few specialists will need to adapt.
