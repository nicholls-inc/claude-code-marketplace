In late August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded, working-class district of London. The disease strikes with terrible speed. Violent vomiting and diarrhoea drain the body of fluid, the skin turns blue-grey, and a healthy person can be dead within a day. Within a few days of the outbreak's start, hundreds of people living within a few streets of one another had died, and many of the survivors fled the area. London had seen cholera before, in 1831 and 1848, and no one yet agreed on what caused it.

The prevailing belief among doctors and officials was the miasma theory, which held that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting waste, sewers and marshy ground. It was a reasonable guess in a city where the smell of the streets could be overwhelming, and it shaped public policy. Dr John Snow, a respected physician who had pioneered the use of anaesthesia in childbirth and surgery, was unconvinced. During the earlier epidemic of 1848 and 1849 he had reasoned that cholera must be something swallowed, because it attacked the gut first and not the lungs. He suspected that people took in some poisonous material from water or food contaminated by the waste of the sick. He had published this idea in 1849, but few were persuaded.

Snow lived and worked close to the stricken streets, and when the outbreak erupted he set out to test his idea. He did not have a microscope image of the culprit, since the bacterium responsible would not be properly identified for decades. What he had was a willingness to count and to knock on doors. He obtained the list of the dead from the local registrar and began plotting where each victim had lived. Deaths clustered tightly around the junction of Broad Street and Cambridge Street, and the pattern pointed towards a public water pump on Broad Street, where residents drew their drinking water.

Snow then went looking for exceptions, which proved as revealing as the rule. A workhouse nearby housed hundreds of inmates, yet very few of them died. When he investigated, he learned that the workhouse had its own well and did not rely on the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped almost untouched. The men there were given beer as part of their pay and had their own supply of water as well, so they rarely drank from the pump. In contrast, he found a woman who lived far away in Hampstead and who had died of cholera. Her family told him that she had liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it brought to her every day. A niece who drank it as well also fell ill and died.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow took his findings to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, the local officials responsible for the area. He urged them to remove the handle of the pump so that nobody could draw water from it. The guardians were sceptical, but they agreed to act, and the handle came off the next day. Snow himself understood that the outbreak was already declining by then, as many residents had fled and many of the most vulnerable had already died, so the removal of the handle did not clearly end the epidemic. The case for his theory rested on the detective work that came before it.

Snow's work was then picked up by a local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead. Whitehead had at first doubted Snow's theory, but he knew the neighbourhood and its people far better than the doctor did, and he carried out his own inquiries. Among other things, he identified what may have been the original source of the contamination. A baby girl living at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with diarrhoea shortly before the outbreak, and her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies in a bucket and poured the water into a cesspool just a few feet from the well beneath the pump. When the cesspool was later dug up, its brickwork was found to be decayed, which would have allowed the filth to seep into the well. Whitehead came to accept Snow's conclusion, and the two men worked together afterwards.

Snow made a map of the outbreak, marking each death with a small bar on the house where it occurred, and it has become one of the most famous images in the history of public health. It did not win over the authorities at once. An official inquiry rejected the idea of contaminated water and kept to the miasma view. Snow died in 1858, still largely unvindicated. Yet his method, which relied on careful counting, mapping and the search for patterns in who fell ill and who did not, laid the groundwork for modern epidemiology. Within a few decades, as the germ theory of disease gained ground, the cause of cholera was understood, and Snow's investigation became widely seen as the moment when the science of tracing an epidemic to its source began.
