## The problem

Suppose you and a stranger want to agree on a secret number, but the only way to talk is by shouting across a crowded room. Everyone hears everything. It sounds impossible. If every word is overheard, how can anything stay secret?

For thousands of years the answer was that it could not be done. To share a secret you first had to meet in private and swap a key, a shared code that both of you keep hidden. Then in the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around this. It is called public-key cryptography, and it protects almost everything you do online, from banking to messaging.

The idea rests on one observation. Some mathematical jobs are easy to do but extremely hard to undo. Public-key cryptography uses that imbalance.

## The paint analogy

Here is the classic picture. Imagine Alice and Bob each have a pot of paint, and they agree publicly on a shared starting colour, say yellow. Everyone watching knows it is yellow.

Alice secretly picks her own colour, say red, and mixes it into the yellow. She sends the resulting orange-ish mixture across the room. Bob secretly picks blue and mixes it into the yellow. He sends his greenish mixture across.

Now Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his secret blue. Both end up with yellow, red and blue all blended together. The same final colour.

An eavesdropper saw yellow, and saw the two mixtures. But paint cannot be unmixed. To get the final colour they would need to add either red or blue, and they know neither. The secret colour is shared, yet it never travelled across the room.

The maths below does exactly this, with numbers in place of paint.

## Clock arithmetic

The tool we need is something you have already used: a clock.

If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. We wrap around when we pass 12. Mathematicians call this "arithmetic modulo 12", and write the wrapping with the word "mod". So 15 mod 12 = 3.

We can wrap around any number, not just 12. Take a clock with 23 hours. Then 25 mod 23 = 2, because 25 is one full lap (23) plus 2 left over.

Now we need one more ingredient: powers. 5 to the power 3 means 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. On our 23-hour clock, 125 mod 23 = 10, since 23 × 5 = 115 and 125 minus 115 leaves 10.

Here is the key fact. Working forwards is easy. Given 5 and the power 3, you can quickly find 10. But working backwards is hard. If someone says "5 to some power, wrapped on a 23-hour clock, gave me 10", you cannot tell which power was used except by trying them one at a time. With a small clock that is quick. With a clock of several hundred digits, it would take longer than the age of the universe, even for the fastest computers.

That one-way street is our unmixable paint.

## The exchange, step by step

Alice and Bob agree out loud on two public numbers:

- The clock size: 23
- A base number: 5

Anyone can hear these. They are the yellow paint.

**Alice picks a secret number**, 6. She tells nobody. She computes 5 to the power 6, wrapped on the clock. That is 15625, and 15625 mod 23 = 8. She announces **8** publicly.

**Bob picks a secret number**, 15. He computes 5 to the power 15, wrapped on the clock, and gets 19. He announces **19** publicly.

These two announcements are the mixed paints. Everyone in the room now knows 23, 5, 8 and 19.

**Alice combines** Bob's public 19 with her secret 6. She computes 19 to the power 6, wrapped on the clock. The answer is **2**.

**Bob combines** Alice's public 8 with his secret 15. He computes 8 to the power 15, wrapped on the clock. The answer is also **2**.

Both of them now hold the secret number 2, and it never crossed the room.

## Why does it come out the same?

This is not luck. Alice's final number is really "5 to the power (Bob's secret times Alice's secret)". Bob's is "5 to the power (Alice's secret times Bob's secret)". Multiplying numbers in either order gives the same answer, so the two results match. Wrapping around the clock does not disturb this.

That is the whole trick: a calculation that comes out identical whichever of the two secrets you apply first.

## Why the eavesdropper is stuck

The listener knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the shared secret, they would need Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. To get 6, they must answer: "5 to what power, wrapped on a 23-hour clock, gives 8?"

With a clock this small, they can just try every power until one fits, and they will find it within moments. That is why real systems never use a clock this small. Real ones use a clock with a number hundreds of digits long. Honest users still compute their public numbers quickly, using shortcuts for big powers. But the trial-and-error search for the hidden power becomes hopeless. There is no known fast method for it on ordinary computers.

So security here does not come from hiding the method. The method is public and has been studied by experts for decades. It comes from the gap between a job that is quick to do and a job that is practically impossible to reverse.

## Public keys and private keys

You may wonder where the name "public-key" comes from. In the exchange above, Alice's secret number is her **private key**, and the number she announces (8) is her **public key**. The two are linked by the one-way calculation. Anyone can know the public key. Only she knows the private one.

This idea can also be used in a second way, which feels like a padlock. Imagine Bob makes thousands of identical open padlocks and hands them out freely, while keeping the only key. Anyone who wants to send him a private message puts it in a box, snaps one of Bob's padlocks shut on it, and sends it. Once it is locked, even the sender cannot reopen it. Only Bob, with his private key, can. The padlock is the public key, and the key in Bob's pocket is the private key.

A well-known system called RSA works like this. Its one-way job is different from the clock trick. It relies on multiplying two huge prime numbers, which is easy, versus splitting the result back into those two primes, which is not. Try it yourself: 7 × 13 is quick, but if I only tell you the answer is 91, you have to search for the factors. With numbers hundreds of digits long, the search becomes impossible in practice.

## What happens in real life

When your browser shows a padlock symbol, a version of this exchange has just happened between your device and a website you have never met. Your two machines agree on a shared secret in public, then use that secret as the key for a faster everyday code that scrambles the rest of the conversation. The slow, clever maths is used briefly at the start. The fast code does the heavy lifting afterwards.

## The big idea

Two people who have never met can agree on a secret in front of an audience, because some calculations are easy to perform and nearly impossible to undo. Each person mixes a private ingredient into a shared public one. They swap the mixtures, add their private ingredient again, and arrive at the same result. The audience, lacking either private ingredient, cannot reproduce it.

Nothing secret was ever sent. It was built independently at both ends.`})
