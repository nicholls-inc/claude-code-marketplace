**Stop Estimating. Start Forecasting.**

Ask any engineer what happens after they give an estimate. It becomes a date. The date becomes a promise. The promise becomes a meeting where someone asks why the promise was broken. Somewhere in that chain, the original number stopped being a guess and started being a contract. That is why software teams should stop estimating how long work will take.

The case against estimation starts with the evidence. Studies of software projects, from the Standish reports to decades of academic work on planning fallacy, keep finding the same thing: we are bad at this, and practice does not fix it. The reason is structural. Software work is mostly discovery. If we knew exactly how to build something, we would copy it. The hours go into the parts we did not know were there: the undocumented service, the flaky test, the requirement that turns out to mean two things. An estimate asks you to price what you cannot yet see.

The damage goes beyond inaccuracy. Estimates distort behavior. Engineers pad, so work expands to fill the padding. Managers know about the padding, so they trim it, and engineers pad harder next time. Teams cut corners to hit a number they never believed in. Planning meetings, where eight people debate whether a ticket is a 5 or an 8, burn hours that could have shipped the ticket. Nobody has ever delivered faster because the story points were well calibrated.

The usual objection is that the business needs dates. It does, but a date is not the same thing as an estimate. Planning needs a forecast, and a forecast can be built from data you already have. Track how many items your team finishes per week. Break work into slices small enough that sizes barely matter, a day or two each. Then count. If you finish roughly six items a week and forty remain, you are looking at seven weeks, give or take, and the range widens honestly as the work gets farther out. This is less precise than a single confident number, but the confident number was fiction anyway.

This approach also changes the conversation with managers for the better. Instead of "how long will this take," the question becomes "what is the most valuable thing we can finish in the next two weeks, and what should we cut if we learn something new?" Scope becomes the lever, because scope is the only variable that was ever adjustable. Deadlines that are truly fixed, like a regulatory date or a launch tied to an event, get handled by shrinking the work until it fits, not by pretending we predicted the future.

Some will say this is a luxury for mature teams. I think the opposite. Teams that are new, or working in unfamiliar territory, are the ones whose estimates are most wrong and whose trust is most fragile. Small slices and visible throughput give them honest signals fast.

None of this means working without a plan. It means replacing a ritual that produces false precision with a practice that produces real information. Stop asking people to predict. Ask them to ship small, measure what happened, and tell you what the data says. You will get better dates, calmer teams, and far fewer meetings about why the last date was wrong.
