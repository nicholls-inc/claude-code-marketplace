How Two Strangers Can Agree a Secret in Public

The puzzle

Imagine Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret number, perhaps to scramble their messages so nobody else can read them. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Whatever Alice says, Eve, who is eavesdropping, hears. Whatever Bob says, Eve hears too.

This sounds impossible. If every word is overheard, how can anything stay secret? For most of history, people assumed it was impossible. Two parties needed to meet first, in private, and swap a key. In the 1970s, mathematicians showed there is a way round the problem. It rests on a simple idea: some calculations are easy to do but very hard to undo.

Mixing paint

Start with a picture rather than numbers. Suppose Alice and Bob each have a tub of paint. They agree, publicly, that the starting colour is yellow. Eve knows this too.

Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red, and mixes it into the yellow. She sends the mixture to Bob. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, say blue, mixes it into the yellow, and sends that mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures travel across the line.

Now Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her private red. Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his private blue. Both end up with the same thing: yellow plus red plus blue. The order of mixing does not matter. They now share a colour that was never sent anywhere.

Eve has the two mixtures and the yellow. But unmixing paint is very hard. She cannot pull the red out of Alice's mixture. If she adds her own colours, she gets a different result. The trick is that mixing is easy and unmixing is nearly impossible.

Numbers can play the role of paint. We need a mathematical operation that is easy one way and hard to reverse.

Clock arithmetic

Here is the school maths we need. When you tell the time, you already use "wrap around" arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You divide by 12 and keep the remainder.

Mathematicians call this "mod". We write 15 mod 12 = 3, which means "the remainder when 15 is divided by 12 is 3". We can use any size of clock. On a 23-hour clock, 25 mod 23 = 2, and 46 mod 23 = 0.

Next, recall powers. 5 to the power 3 means 5 x 5 x 5 = 125. Now combine the two ideas: work out a power, then keep only the remainder after dividing by 23.

Why this is easy forwards and hard backwards

Take 5 to the power 6, mod 23. We compute 5^6 = 15,625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 with 8 left over, so the answer is 8. That took a few seconds.

Now run it backwards. Suppose I tell you "5 to some power, mod 23, gives 8" and ask which power. With a small clock you can just try 1, 2, 3 and so on until you hit 8. But real systems use a clock with hundreds of digits. The answers jump around with no pattern, and trying every power would take longer than the age of the universe. Meanwhile, going forwards stays quick, because computers can do it with a clever shortcut of repeated squaring.

So we have our one-way street: easy to calculate, practically impossible to reverse.

The exchange, step by step

This method is called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, after two of the people who published it in 1976. Here it is with small numbers so you can follow along.

Step 1, public agreement. Alice and Bob announce two numbers openly: the clock size 23 and a base number 5. Eve hears both. This is the "yellow paint".

Step 2, private choices. Alice picks a secret number, 6. She tells no one. Bob picks a secret number, 15. He tells no one either.

Step 3, mixing. Alice calculates 5^6 mod 23, which is 8, and sends 8 to Bob. Bob calculates 5^15 mod 23, which is 19, and sends 19 to Alice. Eve hears 8 and 19. These are the two paint mixtures.

Step 4, the final mix. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power: 19^6 mod 23. This comes to 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power: 8^15 mod 23. This also comes to 2.

They both have the number 2. It is their shared secret, and it never travelled along the line.

Why do they match? Because of how powers work. Alice effectively computed 5 to the power (15 x 6), and Bob computed 5 to the power (6 x 15). Multiplication does not care about order, so both land on the same answer. It is the same reason the paints could be mixed in either order.

What Eve is left with

Eve knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the shared secret she would need either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. To get 6, she must answer "5 to what power gives 8, mod 23?" With such tiny numbers she can simply try them all, and she would find the answer quickly. That is why this toy example is not safe.

In real use, the clock is enormous, with numbers running to hundreds of digits, and the secret powers are just as large. Trying them all is impossible. The best known methods are still hopelessly slow. Nobody has proved that a fast method does not exist, but after decades of effort by many clever people, none has been found.

Where "public key" comes in

The name public-key cryptography comes from a close cousin of this idea. In that version, each person makes a pair of linked numbers: a public key that they publish to the world, and a private key that they never share. Things locked using the public key can be unlocked only with the private key. It is like a padlock that anyone can snap shut, while only the owner holds the key to open it.

The famous RSA system works this way. Its one-way street is different: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but taking the result and finding the two primes is extremely hard. Imagine being given 8,633 and asked for its factors. You would have to hunt around (they are 89 and 97). Now imagine a number with 600 digits.

In practice, your phone or laptop combines these tools. Public-key methods are used briefly at the start of a connection to agree a secret. Then a faster scrambling method, using that secret, protects the rest of the conversation. This happens every time you visit a secure website, in a fraction of a second.

One important catch

The exchange hides the secret from someone who only listens. But what if Eve does more than listen? Suppose she cuts the line and pretends to be Bob when talking to Alice, and pretends to be Alice when talking to Bob. Each would happily agree a secret with Eve instead of with each other.

To stop this, the two sides also need a way to check who they are talking to. The web handles this with digital certificates, which are like identity cards vouched for by trusted organisations. The mathematics of agreeing a secret is only half the story. Knowing you are agreeing it with the right person is the other half.

The big idea

The beauty of the whole method is that secrecy does not come from hiding the steps. Everything Alice and Bob do is public, and the method is published in textbooks. The secrecy comes from the private numbers they keep to themselves, and from a calculation that is easy to do but nearly impossible to undo. Two strangers, speaking openly in a crowded room, end up sharing something that nobody else in the room can work out.
