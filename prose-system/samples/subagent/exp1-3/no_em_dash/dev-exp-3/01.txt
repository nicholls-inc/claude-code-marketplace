How Two Strangers Can Share a Secret in Public

The problem

Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met. They want to agree on a secret, say a number, that only the two of them know. They can only talk by shouting across a crowded room, and everyone in the room hears every word. It sounds impossible. If the secret is spoken aloud, the listeners hear it too.

Yet this is exactly what your phone and a website do every time you see a padlock in your browser. The two machines have never met, an eavesdropper can read every message between them, and still they end up with a shared secret that the eavesdropper cannot work out. The trick is called public-key cryptography, and the core idea is simpler than it sounds. It rests on one observation: some jobs are easy to do but very hard to undo.

Easy to do, hard to undo

Think of mixing paint. If you mix yellow paint with a splash of a secret colour, getting the result is easy. Getting back from the mixed paint to the exact secret colour is practically impossible. You cannot un-mix paint.

Here is how Alice and Bob use that idea. They agree publicly on a starting colour, say yellow, and everyone in the room hears it. Alice privately picks a secret colour and mixes it with the yellow. Bob does the same with his own secret colour. They swap their mixtures in public. Now Alice adds her secret colour to Bob's mixture, and Bob adds his secret colour to Alice's mixture. Both end up with the same final blend: yellow plus Alice's secret plus Bob's secret. The order of mixing does not matter.

The eavesdropper saw the yellow and both mixtures, but never saw either secret colour. To get the final blend, they would need one of the secrets, and they cannot un-mix the paint to find it.

Real computers cannot mix paint, so they use a mathematical version of the same trick. It needs only one piece of school maths, which you may know already: clock arithmetic.

Clock arithmetic

On a 12-hour clock, if it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock. You went past 12 and wrapped around. Mathematicians write this as "15 mod 12 = 3", which just means "divide by 12 and keep the remainder". The "mod" part is the wrapping around.

Nothing stops us using a clock with a different number of hours. We will use a clock with 23 hours. So "mod 23" means "divide by 23 and keep the remainder". For example, 30 mod 23 = 7.

We also need powers. 5 to the power of 3 means 5 x 5 x 5 = 125. Combine the two ideas and you get something like "5 to the power of 3, mod 23". That is 125 divided by 23, which leaves a remainder of 10. So the answer is 10.

Why is this useful? Because the operation is one-way. Suppose I tell you that 5 to some secret power, mod 23, equals 8. Finding that secret power is a guessing game. You would try power 1, power 2, power 3, and so on, until you hit 8. With a 23-hour clock that takes a minute. With a clock of several hundred digits, even the fastest computers on Earth would need longer than the age of the universe. Going forward is quick. Going backward is hopeless. That is our paint-mixing trick in numbers.

The exchange, step by step

Alice and Bob start by agreeing, in public, on two numbers: the clock size, 23, and a base number, 5. The whole room hears these. They are not secret.

Step 1. Alice picks a secret number, 6, and tells no one. Bob picks a secret number, 15, and tells no one.

Step 2. Alice computes 5 to the power 6, mod 23. That is 15,625, which leaves a remainder of 8. She shouts "8" across the room. This is her public key, the mixed paint.

Bob computes 5 to the power 15, mod 23. The answer is 19. He shouts "19". This is his public key.

Step 3. Alice takes Bob's number, 19, and raises it to her own secret power: 19 to the power 6, mod 23. The answer is 2.

Bob takes Alice's number, 8, and raises it to his secret power: 8 to the power 15, mod 23. The answer is also 2.

Both of them now hold the number 2. They never said it aloud, and it travelled nowhere.

Why do they get the same answer?

This is the part that feels like magic, so it is worth seeing why it works. Alice's number is 5 raised to her secret power. Bob then raises that to his secret power. Bob's route therefore equals 5 raised to (Alice's secret x Bob's secret). Alice's route is 5 raised to Bob's secret and then to her own secret, which is the same multiplication done in the other order. Since 6 x 15 equals 15 x 6, both routes land on the same place. It is the same reason that mixing the colours in either order gives the same blend.

What the eavesdropper sees

The listener in the room knows the clock size 23, the base 5, Alice's public number 8 and Bob's public number 19. To find the secret 2, they would need Alice's secret power (6) or Bob's secret power (15). Recovering either means working backward from "5 to some power, mod 23, equals 8", the guessing game from before.

With numbers this small, you could crack it in a few minutes with a pencil. That is why real systems use enormous numbers, hundreds of digits long. At that size, the forward calculation still takes a computer a fraction of a second, because there are clever shortcuts for working out huge powers. But the backward guessing game has no known shortcut for the usual choices of clock and base. The gap between easy and hard becomes astronomical.

Why "public key"?

The name comes from the fact that each person has two numbers. The private key is the secret power, which never leaves their head or device. The public key is the number computed from it, which can be shouted to the whole world safely. Anyone may know your public key, because knowing it does not help them find your private key. The two keys are linked by a one-way calculation.

The same idea has other uses. Some systems let anyone lock a message using your public key so that only your private key can open it. Others let you "sign" a message with your private key so anyone can check, using your public key, that it really came from you. These are built on different one-way problems, such as the difficulty of splitting a huge number into its prime factors, but the principle is identical: easy one way, hopeless the other.

A caution

There is one weakness worth knowing about. The method hides the secret from people who only listen, but it does not prove who you are talking to. A cunning attacker could stand between Alice and Bob, pretend to be Bob when talking to Alice and pretend to be Alice when talking to Bob, and run two separate exchanges. Alice would think she shared a secret with Bob when she had actually shared one with the attacker. To prevent this, websites use certificates, which are digital identity cards vouched for by trusted organisations, so that your browser can check it is really talking to the site it thinks it is. The signing idea from the previous section is what makes those certificates possible.

The big picture

Two strangers can agree on a secret in public because of a gap in difficulty. Raising a number to a power on a clock is quick. Working out which power was used is, for big enough numbers, beyond any computer. Each person keeps a private number, shares a public number made from it, and combines the other's public number with their own private one. Because multiplication does not care about order, both arrive at the same secret. Anyone listening has the pieces but not the one thing that would let them put the pieces together.
