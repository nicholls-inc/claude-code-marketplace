On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted three men away from Florida on the third mission meant to land humans on the Moon. The crew was Jim Lovell, the veteran commander, along with Fred Haise, who would pilot the lunar module, and Jack Swigert, who flew the command module. Swigert was a late arrival. Days before launch, doctors found that the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles. Swigert trained in his place and flew with barely any time to settle into the role.

The spacecraft had two halves. The command module, named Odyssey, was the cone shaped cabin where the crew lived and where they would return to Earth. Attached behind it was the service module, a cylinder that held the engine, the batteries, the water and the oxygen. The lunar module, named Aquarius, rode in a compartment below, waiting for the day Lovell and Haise would take it down to the surface.

For two days the trip went smoothly. The crew even broadcast a television show from space. Mission Control was so relaxed that the flight controllers on duty barely noticed how dull the work felt. Then, about fifty six hours into the flight and roughly two hundred thousand miles from home, Houston asked Swigert to flip a switch that stirred the supercritical oxygen in the tanks of the service module. Stirring kept the readings accurate.

Swigert threw the switch. A few moments later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Lovell's voice came down the radio with calm understatement. Swigert had already said that they had a problem, and Lovell repeated it for the controllers who had not caught it. Warning lights lit across the panel. A main electrical bus had lost power. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space.

The cause lay years in the past. An oxygen tank inside the service module had been damaged during ground testing, and a heater switch inside it had been fused shut by a power surge nobody understood. When Swigert stirred the tank, bare wires sparked inside pure oxygen. The tank burst and tore a panel off the side of the service module. A second tank was crippled. The fuel cells that made electricity and water needed that oxygen. Within hours the command module would be dead.

On the ground, flight director Gene Kranz and his teams worked through the problem in shifts. Landing on the Moon was abandoned within the hour. The only question now was how to bring three men home alive. The answer was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, its own power and its own engine, but it had been built to support two men for two days on the surface. Now it would have to sustain three men for four days.

Swigert and Haise worked through the transfer of supplies and guidance data while Lovell studied the trajectory. The ship was on a path that would swing it around the Moon and, if nothing were done, carry it back toward Earth. That was lucky, but the path would have missed the planet by a wide margin and taken far too long. The crew would need to speed up the return. After passing behind the Moon, they fired the descent engine of Aquarius for about four minutes. This shortened the trip home by many hours and aimed it at the Pacific Ocean.

While they passed around the far side of the Moon, the crew reached a distance from Earth that no human had ever traveled, nearly two hundred and forty nine thousand miles. They had no time to enjoy it. They photographed the lunar surface sliding below them, and then the Moon fell behind and Earth shrank slowly ahead of them.

The days that followed were a test of endurance. Power had to be rationed to the last watt, so the crew shut down nearly everything. The cabin temperature dropped close to freezing. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. Moisture condensed on the walls. Each man was allowed only a few ounces of water a day, and all three lost weight and grew dehydrated. They slept little, because it was too cold, and because the lunar module was cramped and noisy.

A more dangerous threat came from the air itself. Three breathing men produce carbon dioxide, and Aquarius had filters that could handle only two men for two days. The spare filters in the command module were square, while the receptacles in the lunar module were round. Engineers in Houston gathered the items the crew had on board, including cardboard from flight manuals, plastic bags, a sock, hoses and duct tape. They built a crude adapter on a table and read the instructions to the crew step by step. Swigert and Haise assembled it, and the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

Navigation brought its own trouble. The computer in the lunar module was not meant to steer the whole stack of spacecraft, and with the damaged ship venting debris, the stars were hard to read. Lovell used the sunlit edge of the Earth as a visual reference to correct their course. Later he fired the engine by hand for a short burst to refine the path. A slight error might have meant skipping off the atmosphere or burning up.

Meanwhile Ken Mattingly, who had been grounded for the measles that never came, sat in a simulator on the ground. His job was to develop a procedure for restarting Odyssey from cold using only the small amount of battery power left. The command module needed those batteries for reentry, and the budget was tight. Mattingly and the engineers worked out a sequence, and he tested it again and again until it fit within the limits.

On April 17, the crew prepared for the end. They jettisoned the damaged service module, and as it drifted away they saw how much of it had been blown apart. Then they climbed back into Odyssey and powered it up, following Mattingly's procedure. Finally they cast off Aquarius, which had saved them, and watched it fall behind.

Reentry brought the final wait. A spacecraft returning from the Moon is surrounded by hot plasma that blocks radio signals, and the blackout normally lasts about three minutes. This time it stretched longer because of the steeper path, and Mission Control waited in silence. Then a voice came through. Swigert called in, and the room erupted.

Odyssey fell under three parachutes and splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, a few miles from the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima. Within minutes the crew was lifted aboard. They were thinner, exhausted and unshaven, but all three were alive.

An investigation led by Edgar Cortright found that the tank failure traced back to the earlier testing damage and to a chain of overlooked decisions. NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks and added safeguards, and the Apollo program continued with later landings. The mission never reached the Moon, yet many consider it the agency's finest hour. The men and women on the ground, and the three in the cold lifeboat, had solved a problem no one had planned for, and brought everyone home.
