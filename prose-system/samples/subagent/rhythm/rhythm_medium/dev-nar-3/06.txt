On the unseasonably warm afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Dockworkers ate lunch, children played in the streets, and horses and trucks rattled along Commercial Street beneath the elevated railway. Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It was fifty feet tall, about ninety feet across, and filled with more than two million gallons of molasses, which the company intended to ferment into industrial alcohol and, in a world soon to be dry, munitions.

The tank had been built hastily in 1915, and almost no one had examined it carefully. The company's president, Arthur Jell, had not consulted an architect or engineer about the design, and the steel was thinner than it should have been. Residents had long noticed that the tank groaned and leaked, and the molasses seeped so freely that neighborhood children collected it in cups. Rather than repair the structure, the company reportedly painted it brown to hide the stains. A shipment of warm molasses had arrived only a day earlier, and the sudden change in temperature may have increased the pressure inside.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, the tank failed. Witnesses described a rumble like a passing train and a sound like machine-gun fire as the rivets shot out of the steel. A wave of molasses, perhaps twenty-five feet high at its crest, burst outward and raced through the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It swept away wagons, crushed buildings, and knocked a section of the elevated railway off its supports. A firehouse was torn from its foundation, and the men inside were thrown into the mess.

Because the weather had been warm, the molasses moved quickly at first, but it soon cooled and thickened in the January air. Those caught in it found themselves trapped in a heavy, clinging mass that held them in place and made rescue desperately difficult. Rescuers from the Navy, the Red Cross, and the local police and fire departments waded into the sticky flood, and soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship joined them. Many of the victims could not be reached for days. In the end, twenty-one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Horses were killed in great numbers, and the cleanup took weeks, with crews spraying salt water and sand on the streets. The harbor ran brown until summer.

The disaster led to one of the first major class-action suits in Massachusetts. The company insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, but an auditor appointed by the court heard months of testimony and concluded that the tank had been poorly built and inadequately tested. In 1925 the company was ordered to pay damages, and it settled with the victims' families for roughly six hundred thousand dollars.

The flood also changed how Americans build things. Engineers began to demand that architects and builders submit plans with calculations, and that licensed professionals approve them. Some Bostonians still claim that on hot summer days, the old neighborhood faintly smells of molasses.
