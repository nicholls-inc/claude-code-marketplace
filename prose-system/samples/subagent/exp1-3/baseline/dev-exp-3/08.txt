**Sharing a Secret in Public**

**The problem**

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to agree on a secret number, to use as a key for scrambling their messages. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Eve, who is listening, hears every word they say.

This sounds impossible. If Eve hears everything, how can they end up with something she does not know?

The answer is to use a kind of maths that is easy to do in one direction and extremely hard to undo. Alice and Bob each mix a private ingredient into a shared public one. They swap the mixtures, and each then adds their own private ingredient again. Eve sees the mixtures but cannot separate them.

**The paint analogy**

Imagine Alice and Bob agree, openly, on a starting colour: yellow. Everyone can hear this, Eve included.

Alice secretly picks a colour, say red, and mixes a big bucket of it with the yellow. Bob secretly picks blue and mixes his own bucket with the yellow. They send each other their mixed buckets. Eve sees both mixtures.

Now Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his secret blue. Each ends up with yellow, red and blue combined. The order of mixing does not matter, so they hold exactly the same colour.

Eve has the two mixtures, but she cannot un-mix paint to pull out the red or the blue. If she adds more paint to either bucket, she gets a different colour from the one Alice and Bob share.

The mixing is easy, and the unmixing is practically impossible. Real cryptography copies this idea using numbers instead of paint.

**The number version: clock arithmetic**

The maths needs one tool you may know from telling the time: wrapping around. On a 12-hour clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, because you go past 12 and start again. Mathematicians call this "remainder arithmetic". We write "mod" for it. So 15 mod 12 = 3, because 15 divided by 12 leaves a remainder of 3.

Instead of a 12-hour clock, we use a bigger one. Our example uses a 23-hour clock, so every answer is a whole number from 0 to 22. Alice and Bob also agree on a starting number, which plays the part of the yellow paint. We will use 5. Both of these numbers are public.

We also need powers. "5 to the power 3" means 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. Powers grow fast, which is where the usefulness comes from.

**Step by step**

1. **Public choices.** Alice and Bob agree, out loud, on the clock size 23 and the starting number 5. Eve hears them.

2. **Private choices.** Alice picks a secret number, 6. Bob picks a secret number, 15. They tell nobody.

3. **Mixing.** Alice calculates 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625, and finds its remainder on the 23-hour clock. 23 × 679 = 15,617, and 15,625 − 15,617 = 8. So Alice's mixture is 8.

   Bob calculates 5 to the power 15 on the same clock and gets 19. (That power is a huge number, 30,517,578,125, but the remainder is small.)

4. **Swapping.** Alice sends Bob her 8. Bob sends Alice his 19. Eve hears both numbers.

5. **Adding the private ingredient again.** Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power, 6. That is 19 to the power 6 = 47,045,881. Its remainder on the 23-hour clock is 2. (Check: 23 × 2,045,473 = 47,045,879, and 47,045,881 − 47,045,879 = 2.)

   Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power, 15. The remainder is again 2.

Both of them arrive at 2. That is their shared secret, and neither of them ever sent it over the line.

**Why does it match?**

Alice's final number is "5 to the power 15, then to the power 6". Bob's is "5 to the power 6, then to the power 15". Both come to 5 to the power 90, because 15 × 6 = 6 × 15. This is the paint-mixing order not mattering, written as arithmetic. The clock wrapping does not disturb it.

**What does Eve know, and why is she stuck?**

Eve knows the clock size (23), the starting number (5), and the two mixtures (8 and 19). To find the secret, she needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15.

In our tiny example she can simply try every possibility. Is there a power of 5 that gives 8 on this clock? She tries 1, 2, 3 and so on until she hits 6. That takes seconds, because the clock only has 23 positions.

Real systems use a clock with hundreds of digits. Raising a number to a power on such a clock is quick for a computer, because there are clever shortcuts. But going backwards, from the answer to the power, has no known shortcut. Eve would have to try possibilities one by one, and the number of possibilities is far larger than the number of atoms in the Earth. All the computers in the world, working for the age of the universe, would not get through them.

That is the key idea. Going forward is cheap. Going backward is, as far as anyone knows, hopelessly expensive. Security rests on that gap.

**Why it is called public-key cryptography**

In this scheme, each person has a private number (Alice's 6) and a public number made from it (Alice's 8). The public number can be shown to the whole world. The private one never leaves its owner. The two are linked by the easy-forward, hard-backward maths above.

This is what the name means. The "public key" is the mixture you hand out freely. The "private key" is the secret that made it.

This method of agreeing a secret was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, so it is named after them. A different system, called RSA, uses another one-way problem: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but taking the answer and finding the two primes is not. RSA can also be used to lock a message with someone's public number so that only their private number can open it. The principle is the same.

**The catch: who are you really talking to?**

There is one weakness. Suppose Eve does not just listen but sits in the middle, pretending to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob. She does one exchange with each, and ends up with a secret shared with each of them. Then she can read and pass on everything. Alice and Bob would have no idea.

The maths alone cannot stop this, because it does not tell you whose number you have received. Real systems add a way to prove identity. For example, a trusted organisation vouches that a public number really belongs to a particular website, in a signed certificate. Your browser checks this when you see the padlock symbol.

**In practice**

When you visit a secure website, your computer and the site's computer do a version of this exchange in a fraction of a second. They end up with a shared secret key, and then use it to scramble everything else you send. The key exchange is slow compared with ordinary scrambling, so it is used only to set up the secret. After that, faster methods take over.

**Summary**

- Alice and Bob agree publicly on a starting point.
- Each mixes in a private number and sends the result.
- Each mixes their private number into what they received.
- Because the order of mixing does not matter, they get the same result.
- Eve sees only the mixtures, and unmixing them is infeasible.

Two strangers, a public line, and a shared secret that never travelled along it.
