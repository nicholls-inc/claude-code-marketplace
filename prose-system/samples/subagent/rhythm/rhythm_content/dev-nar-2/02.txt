On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Cape Kennedy carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. Swigert had joined the crew only days earlier, replacing Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons, and the swap went smoothly enough that nobody gave it much thought as the Saturn V climbed away. The flight had its first small fright when the center engine of the second stage shut down early, but the remaining engines burned longer to compensate, and the spacecraft reached Earth orbit and then fired toward the Moon on schedule. Two days of routine cruising followed, during which the crew separated their command and service module, named Odyssey, from the third stage, turned around, docked with the lunar module, named Aquarius, and pulled it free, and the television audience, which had grown bored with lunar missions, largely ignored a broadcast the crew made on the evening of April 13.

Shortly after that broadcast ended, at about 9:07 in the evening Houston time, Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine step that agitates the supercritical contents so the gauges read accurately. Swigert flipped the switch, and a little over ninety seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt a shudder. Swigert reported a problem, Lovell repeated it with the now-famous phrasing that Houston had a problem, and within moments the instruments showed that one oxygen tank was empty and the other was falling, while two of the three fuel cells that supplied electricity and drinking water were dying. Investigators later traced the cause to damage done years earlier on the ground. The tank's heaters had been built for a different voltage than the ground power used at the Cape, and during a prelaunch test they had run hot enough to melt the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside, leaving bare wires that sparked when the fans were switched on and ignited the oxygen. The resulting explosion blew off a panel of the service module and crippled everything the spacecraft depended on.

For the next several minutes the crew and the controllers tried to make sense of readings that would not add up, and Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas venting into space, which told him what the instruments had not. The command module could run for a few hours on its batteries, which were needed for the final descent, and it was clear that the Moon landing was lost and that the only real question was whether the three men could be brought home. The solution, which had been discussed in studies though never practiced, was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat, since it had its own oxygen, water, power and a descent engine. Haise and Lovell moved into Aquarius and powered it up, while Swigert shut down Odyssey to preserve what little battery capacity remained, and the lunar module, designed to support two men for roughly two days on the surface, would now have to keep three alive for nearly four.

The trajectory presented another problem. The spacecraft had been placed on a path that would have looped around the Moon and then missed the Earth by a wide margin, a deliberate safety choice that now worked against them. Controllers decided that the quickest way home was to swing around the Moon and burn the lunar module's descent engine to speed the return, and about five hours after the explosion Lovell and Haise did exactly that, correcting the course to bring them back toward the Pacific. The crew passed behind the Moon, more than two hundred thousand miles from home and farther from Earth than any humans had ever been, and a few hours after emerging they fired the engine again, this time for a longer burn that shortened the trip by about ten hours and aimed them at a splashdown in the Pacific.

Life aboard Aquarius grew steadily worse. To save power the crew turned off nearly everything, including the heaters, and the cabin temperature dropped to a few degrees above freezing, so that water condensed on the walls and the men slept poorly in their cold flight suits. Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever, and all three were short of water because rationing meant they drank only a few ounces a day, and the real danger was the buildup of carbon dioxide. The lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which absorb the gas, were designed for two men and were filling up, while the command module carried plenty of canisters of a different shape that would not fit the lunar module's sockets. Engineers on the ground gathered the same items the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock and duct tape, and built a connector that would let a square canister work in a round receptacle, then read the instructions to the crew step by step. The adapter worked, and the carbon dioxide level fell back into a safe range.

Meanwhile, another team in Houston, including Mattingly, who was working on the ground and who had never contracted measles, wrote a procedure for restarting the command module, a job that nobody had planned for because Odyssey was meant to be powered up from a ready state, not revived from a cold, nearly dead one, and the checklist had to be written, tested in simulators and approved within a few days. The order of switching on systems mattered because the batteries held just enough charge for reentry, and the team found ways to bring up only the essentials, taking care to limit the power drawn. Navigation was another concern, because the crew had to align the guidance platform without the usual star sightings, as debris from the explosion glittered around the ship and blocked the view, so they used the Sun to set the alignment, and in the final hours they made a last small course correction by hand using the Earth's terminator as a reference.

On April 17 the crew separated from the service module and for the first time saw the damage. A whole side panel was missing, and wires and insulation trailed out of the gap, which Lovell reported to the ground and which investigators would study for months. Then they jettisoned Aquarius, which had saved them, and Odyssey fell on alone toward the atmosphere with its heat shield intact, though nobody could be fully sure of that. Reentry meant the usual radio blackout, which normally lasts about three minutes, and this time it stretched longer than expected because of the shallow angle, and controllers in Houston waited in silence as the minutes passed, with no signal from the capsule. Then Swigert's voice came through, and the parachutes opened, and at about 1:07 in the afternoon Houston time on April 17 the capsule splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship Iwo Jima. The crew were weary, dehydrated and cold, but all three were alive, and Lovell had lost nearly fourteen pounds.

The investigation that followed, led by a board under Edgar Cortright, found that the accident was not caused by a single failure so much as by a chain of small ones, and it led to a redesign of the oxygen tanks, the removal of the stirring fans' hazardous wiring, and the addition of a third oxygen tank and a spare battery in the service module. NASA concluded that the mission, though a failure in its aim, had shown the value of the lunar module as a refuge and the skill of the ground teams, and Apollo continued with four more landings. The flight is remembered less as a failure than as one of the finest improvisations in the history of spaceflight, a mission that was declared a success only because it ended with every man returning home.
