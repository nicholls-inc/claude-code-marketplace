On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unexpected thaw. After days of bitter cold, the temperature had climbed above forty degrees, and workers, children and horses crowded the streets near the harbor. Looming over the neighborhood stood a steel tank fifty feet tall and ninety feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, which was used to make munitions during the war and, soon after, liquor. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply. Its walls were thin, its rivets were poorly set, and nobody had properly tested it by filling it with water first. It leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the syrup in cans. Rather than repair it, the company painted it brown so the seepage would be harder to see. Residents also reported strange groans and rumbles from the structure, but their complaints went unanswered.

A little after half past noon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a sound like machine gun fire as rivets shot out, followed by a deep roar as the steel split open. A wave of molasses rushed into the streets at roughly thirty five miles an hour. At its front it stood perhaps twenty five feet high. It was heavy, sticky and almost impossible to outrun.

The wave tore buildings from their foundations and bent the supports of the elevated railway. It swept up wagons, horses and people. A nearby firehouse was shoved off its footings, and firefighters inside were trapped in the wreckage. Within seconds, a stretch of the neighborhood was buried under a thick brown flood. The syrup soon cooled in the winter air and thickened, which made things worse. Rescuers waded through it up to their knees, struggling to reach victims who were pinned or drowning in the muck.

Twenty one people died, and about a hundred and fifty were injured. The dead included children and workers who happened to be passing by. Some of the bodies were so coated in molasses that they were hard to identify. Crews pumped salt water from fire boats onto the streets and covered them with sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown for months, and residents said the sweet, sickly smell lingered for decades on hot summer days.

The aftermath became a landmark in American law. United States Industrial Alcohol claimed anarchists had bombed the tank, a story that suited the fears of the time. But a court appointed auditor spent years hearing testimony from experts and survivors. In 1925 he concluded that the tank was structurally unsound and that the company was responsible. The firm paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements. It was one of the first major class action suits in the country, and the case helped push states to require engineers to review and approve large structures.

The disaster remains a strange and sobering story, in which something as ordinary as molasses became deadly through carelessness.
