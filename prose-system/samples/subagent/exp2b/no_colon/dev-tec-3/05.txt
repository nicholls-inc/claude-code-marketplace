A lithium-ion cell is charged with a method called constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV). It behaves like a battery that you push current into until its terminal voltage reaches a ceiling, and then you hold that ceiling while the current dies away.

In the first phase, a charger forces a fixed current into the cell. A typical rate is 0.5C to 1C, meaning a current that would fill the nominal capacity in two hours to one hour. Terminal voltage climbs steadily as lithium ions move from the cathode, usually a layered metal oxide, through the electrolyte and into the graphite anode. The voltage the charger sees is the sum of two things. One is the cell's open-circuit voltage, which rises with state of charge as the electrodes fill up. The other is an overpotential, roughly the current multiplied by the cell's internal resistance, plus extra terms from the electrochemistry. For a common 4.2 V cell, the charger switches modes when the terminal voltage reaches 4.2 V. At that point the cell is typically around 70 to 80 percent full.

In the second phase, the charger holds the voltage at 4.2 V and lets the current fall on its own. Charging ends when the current drops to a small fraction of the original, often 0.05C to 0.1C.

The slowdown follows from simple circuit logic. The voltage is capped, but the open-circuit voltage keeps rising as the cell fills. The gap between the cap and the open-circuit voltage is what drives current through the internal resistance, so the current is roughly that gap divided by the resistance. As the cell fills, the gap shrinks, and the current shrinks with it. The result is an exponential-like decay, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor.

Several physical effects add to this. Lithium must diffuse into the bulk of the graphite particles, which is slow, so the particle surface fills well before the core. Surface concentration therefore rises faster than the average, which pushes the voltage up early and triggers the switch to constant voltage while much of the particle interior is still empty. The current then has to wait on diffusion to even out the concentration. Nearly full graphite also offers fewer vacant sites, so the reaction itself becomes more sluggish.

The ceiling exists for safety and lifetime. Pushing the voltage higher accelerates electrolyte decomposition and can plate metallic lithium on the anode, which degrades capacity and can form dendrites that short the cell. Fast charging into a nearly full cell raises exactly this plating risk, so the taper is a feature, not a flaw.

For an engineer, the practical takeaway is that the first 80 percent of charge is quick and set by current limits and heating, while the last 20 percent is slow and set by voltage limits and diffusion. Cold temperatures worsen both the resistance and the plating risk, which is why chargers derate current in the cold.
