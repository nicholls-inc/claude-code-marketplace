Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it with more confidence than many of them currently do, because the case against it rests on a picture of the classroom that has little to do with what children actually spend their days doing. The usual argument runs that nobody writes by hand anymore, that keyboards have won, and that the hours spent on looped ascenders and exit strokes could be handed to coding or to touch-typing. It is an attractive argument, partly because the premise is half true, and a policy built on a half-truth is worth examining carefully before it is adopted. The strongest reason to keep cursive is not nostalgia, and it is not even beauty; it is that a fluent, fast, legible hand is one of the cheapest and most durable tools a child can be given for thinking on paper, and joining letters is the most reliable route anyone has found to that fluency.

Children in primary school write by hand for a very large share of the day, and they will go on doing so through secondary school, where the great majority of examinations in most countries are still handwritten under timed conditions. A pupil who writes slowly or illegibly in those conditions is penalised twice, once because they get less down in the time available and again because a marker who cannot read a word cannot award a mark for it. Studies of English schoolchildren going back to the 1990s have found that handwriting speed correlates with the quality and quantity of written composition, and the plausible mechanism is simple: a child who must devote attention to forming each letter has less working memory left for what the sentence is meant to say. Automaticity in the hand frees the mind for content. Whether print or cursive is the better vehicle for that automaticity is a fair question, but it is only worth asking if one accepts that the automaticity matters, and the keyboard enthusiasts often skip this step by assuming handwriting is already a solved problem that can be let go.

Print letters are formed one at a time, with the pen lifted after each, so that every word is a series of separate starts, whereas a joined hand turns the word into a single motion, and that continuity is what allows speed without collapse into scrawl. Teachers who have watched children move from print to a joined style in the upper years will often say that the quickest writers in a class are almost never the ones still printing, and that the ones who print fast tend to produce a hybrid of their own making, half joined by accident, which is harder to read than either a clean print or a clean cursive. The point is not that every child should end up with a copperplate script, since almost nobody does and nobody should. The point is that a taught, systematic set of joins gives children a base from which they can develop a personal hand that is quick and still legible, whereas leaving them to improvise gives them a base of nothing in particular. Italic-based and simplified joined styles, with few or no looped entry strokes, take away most of what makes traditional cursive laborious to learn, and schools that choose them keep the benefit without the baroque cost.

There is also a body of research on what handwriting does to the learning of letters and reading, though it should be described with some caution. Experiments with young children, including work by Longcamp and colleagues, have found that children who learn letters by drawing them show better recognition afterward than children who learn the same letters by pressing keys, and brain-imaging studies suggest that handwriting engages reading-related regions more than typing does. The much-cited finding that students taking notes longhand understood lectures better than those typing was only partly reproduced when other researchers tried it again, and anyone arguing for handwriting on that evidence alone is standing on soft ground. What can be said is that nothing in the research supports the opposite proposition, that typing is a superior way to learn the shapes of letters or to compose in the early years, and that the evidence for handwriting leans consistently, if modestly, one way. When the case for a long-standing practice has modest positive evidence and the case for dropping it has none, the burden of proof belongs with those proposing the change.

The best objection is about time, since a school day is finite and every minute spent on joins is a minute taken from something else. That is true, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a dismissal. Joined handwriting is not taught well in long sessions; short daily practice, with attention to posture, grip and the shape of a few letter families at a time, tends to work, and the total hours over a primary career are modest compared with, say, the time devoted to spelling lists. Teaching typing is a worthy aim and the two can sit side by side, because they serve different purposes: the keyboard is the right tool for long drafts, editing and presentation, and the pen is the right tool for the quick note, the exam script, the margin, the form filled out at a counter, the shopping list. Finland, which is often cited as a country that abandoned cursive, in fact removed the requirement of a particular style in its 2016 curriculum in favour of print-script and keyboard skills, and Finnish educators have debated the change since, while the country's international results have drifted down from their peak for reasons that nobody seriously attributes to handwriting at all. Nothing about the Finnish example shows that dropping cursive helped anyone.

A second objection concerns children who struggle, those with dyslexia, dyspraxia or other difficulties, for whom cursive is said to be an extra burden. The evidence here points the other way more often than critics expect. Many specialists in dyslexia have recommended joined writing precisely because it reduces the confusion between letters that are mirror images, such as b and d, which require quite different entry strokes when joined, and because the continuous flow is thought to support the motor memory of spelling patterns. Not every child will benefit, and some with significant motor difficulties are better served by a keyboard from early on, which is the argument for flexibility in how schools respond to individuals. It is not an argument for removing the taught skill from everyone because it is hard for some, any more than difficulty with arithmetic is an argument for abolishing long division in a class where most children can learn it.

A final consideration is easy to dismiss and harder to answer once stated. A great deal of the written record of the past two centuries, from family letters to census forms to the annotations in the margin of a grandparent's cookbook, is in joined handwriting. A generation that cannot read it will depend on transcribers for access to its own history, and the loss, while quiet, is real. Children who can write a joined hand can read one, and reading cursive is a skill that is lost within a single generation if it is not taught. The signature is a related case, because it remains a legal act in much of life, and a person who has never developed a fluent personal hand is left with a printed name or a scribble, neither of which does the job well.

None of this requires pretending that handwriting is more central than it is. Computers are essential and children should be taught to use them well, and any curriculum that neglected typing, digital composition and editing would be failing them. The claim here is narrower and more defensible, that a child who leaves primary school with a quick, clear, joined hand has a skill that serves them across the whole of their schooling and well beyond, and that the way to produce that skill is to teach it directly, with a method, early enough that bad habits have not set. England's national curriculum has expected pupils to be joining their writing in the lower years of key stage 2 for some time, and the sensible response to the pressures on the timetable is to teach it efficiently rather than to abandon it. Several American states that saw cursive dropped from their standards after the Common Core era have since passed laws restoring it, which suggests that the cost of the omission became visible to the people living with it.

Schools should therefore keep teaching joined-up handwriting, choose a simple joined style, give it brief daily practice in the early years, and treat the keyboard as a complement rather than a replacement. Children are not asked to write beautifully, only to write well enough that their hand stops being the thing they have to think about, and on the available evidence the most dependable way to get them there is to teach them to join their letters.
