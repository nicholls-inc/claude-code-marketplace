On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with the ordinary noise of a working neighborhood. Along the waterfront stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which was shipped up from the Caribbean and used to make industrial alcohol, a material that mattered a great deal in the final months of the First World War and, soon afterward, to the makers of rum. The tank had been built quickly, in 1915, and cheaply. Its walls were thinner than they should have been, no one had tested it properly, and the company's president never examined the work. When it was first filled, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups, and the company responded by painting the tank brown so the stains would be harder to see.

Shortly after half past noon, a fresh load of warm molasses had recently arrived, and the temperature had risen well above freezing after a cold snap. Witnesses later described a low rumble and a sound like a machine gun as the rivets began to shear. Then the tank gave way. A wave of molasses perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high rushed outward at roughly thirty-five miles an hour, carrying with it a mass of twisted steel and splintered wood. It tore a building from its foundations, bent the girders of the elevated railway, and knocked a firehouse sideways into the harbor. Horses, wagons, and people were caught in a sticky current that was heavier than water, and anyone who fell into it found it almost impossible to escape.

Rescuers arrived quickly, among them sailors from the training ship Nantucket, police, Red Cross workers, and soldiers. They found a street that looked like a battlefield, with victims buried under a thick brown coating that cooled and thickened as the afternoon wore on. Many of the injured could not be reached for hours, and the work of searching went on for days. Twenty-one people died, some drowned or crushed and some suffocated, and around a hundred and fifty more were injured. The harbor ran brown well into the summer, and for decades residents insisted that the neighborhood still smelled of molasses on hot days.

The aftermath was as important as the disaster itself. Survivors and families sued the company, which insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, a claim that fit the anxious mood of the time. A court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, spent years hearing testimony from more than seven hundred witnesses and examining the engineering evidence. In 1925 he concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, and the company paid roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements. The case became one of the first major class action suits in Massachusetts, and it helped push the state toward requiring architects and engineers to sign off on large construction projects. A strange and almost comic event had exposed a plain failure of corporate responsibility, and it left behind a lasting change in how buildings are approved.
