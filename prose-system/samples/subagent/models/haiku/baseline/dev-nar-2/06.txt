Houston, We've Had a Problem: The Story of Apollo 13

At 2:13 p.m. Eastern time on April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 13 from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center. To most of the world it looked like a routine mission. Apollo had already put men on the Moon twice, and this flight was headed for Fra Mauro, a rugged highland region the earlier crews had not reached. The commander, Jim Lovell, had flown two Gemini missions and had orbited the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise was on his first spaceflight. Jack Swigert had been pulled into the crew only days before launch, after doctors learned that Ken Mattingly, the original command module pilot, had been exposed to German measles. Mattingly never fell ill, but the rules were the rules, and Swigert, the backup, took the seat.

Fifty-five hours into the flight, the ship was roughly 200,000 miles from Earth. At 9:07 p.m. Eastern time on April 13, Swigert heard a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. He radioed Houston with a calm understatement: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming from the service module into the dark. An oxygen tank there had ruptured, and the blast had damaged the plumbing and wiring around it. One of the three fuel cells that produced the ship's electricity and drinking water was gone, and the main electrical bus was losing voltage. The crew's first instinct was to check their instruments, and their second was to tell Mission Control exactly what they saw. The controllers in Houston soon confirmed what the men already suspected. The Moon landing was off. The job now was bringing three men home alive.

The lunar module, Aquarius, became the lifeboat. It had been built to support two men on a short trip to the surface, for about two days. Now it would have to keep three men alive for roughly four days, and the crew moved into its cramped cabin. The command module, Odyssey, was shut down to save its remaining batteries for reentry. Inside Aquarius, the temperature fell, condensation dripped from the walls, and water was rationed sharply. The astronauts wore their bulky suits to stay warm and conserved every bit of power they could. Flight controllers worked through the consequences of each lost system, and one problem soon rose above the rest. Carbon dioxide exhaled by the crew was building up in the cabin, and the lithium hydroxide canisters that scrubbed it were running out of capacity.

The lunar module's canisters had been sized for two men over a shorter stay. The spare canisters on board were stored in the command module, but they were square, and Aquarius's receptacles were round. In Houston, a team of engineers was given the problem and an inventory of what was aboard the spacecraft. They devised an adapter from plastic bags, cardboard, duct tape and a hose, and they walked the crew through building it step by step. The astronauts assembled the rig, and the carbon dioxide levels came down. The episode became a famous example of improvisation under pressure, and it shows how much of the flight depended on people who had never expected to need such a solution.

Getting home was its own challenge. Apollo had been designed with an emergency option: because the spacecraft was already on a free-return trajectory, the Moon's gravity could swing it around the far side and send it back toward Earth. But that path had to be corrected and the timing set precisely. On April 14, a burn using Aquarius's descent engine shortened the return and set a better path for reentry. The next day the spacecraft passed behind the Moon, coming about 140 miles above the surface at its closest. For a while the Moon blocked the radio signal, and the crew was out of contact with Earth. When they reappeared on the homeward leg, the controllers and the crew were both relieved to see the ship still on course. The mission would not land on the Moon, but it was now on a path that could bring it home.

The return took almost three more days. Before reentry, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module and photographed it as it drifted away, its side panels torn open. Mission planners had calculated the reentry angle carefully. If the angle was too steep, the crew would endure crushing deceleration. If it was too shallow, the capsule could skip off the atmosphere and be lost in space. On April 17, Odyssey plunged back through the atmosphere with its heat shield intact, and its parachutes opened over the South Pacific. The capsule splashed down shortly after 1 p.m. Eastern time, a few miles from the recovery ship, USS Iwo Jima. Lovell, Haise and Swigert climbed out into the recovery crew's hands. The world, which had followed the flight through days of anxious silence, broke into relief.

The flight never reached the Moon, but it became one of the most celebrated failures in the history of the space program. Flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers had worked through the disaster with a steadiness that later became legend. The line most people associate with the mission, "Failure is not an option," was written for the 1995 film Apollo 13 and does not appear in the mission transcripts. The real story is less polished and more interesting. It was a story of an oxygen tank that failed, a crew that kept its head, engineers who solved problems with the materials at hand, and a spacecraft that had been designed with enough margin to survive a disaster no one had planned for. Three men came home because many people refused to give up on the problem in front of them.
