**How two strangers can agree on a secret in public**

**The problem**

Suppose you and a stranger want to share a secret code, perhaps to lock a message that only the two of you can read. The only way to talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Everything you say, the eavesdropper hears.

At first this sounds impossible. If every word is overheard, how can anything stay secret? Before the 1970s, everyone assumed it was impossible. To share a secret code, people had to meet first, or send a courier with a codebook. Then a few mathematicians showed there is a way. The idea is called public-key cryptography, and it protects almost everything you do online, from banking to messaging.

**The paint analogy**

Start with a picture before any numbers. Alice and Bob each have a tub of paint. They agree, out loud, on a shared starting colour, say yellow. The eavesdropper, Eve, hears this too.

Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red, and mixes it into the yellow. Bob secretly picks his own, say blue, and mixes it into his yellow. They then swap the mixed tubs. Eve sees both tubs go past, but she sees only the mixtures, not the private colours inside.

Now Alice takes Bob's tub and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's tub and adds his secret blue. Both tubs now contain yellow, red and blue, so they end up the same shade. That shade is the shared secret.

Eve has the yellow and both half-mixed tubs. To get the final colour she would need to add red or blue, and she has neither. Worse, mixing paint is easy but unmixing it is almost impossible. You cannot look at orange-ish brown and work out exactly which reds went in.

The trick is a process that is easy to do and very hard to undo. Mathematics has such processes, and the one we need uses clock arithmetic.

**Clock arithmetic**

Think of a clock with 12 hours. If it is 10 o'clock and 5 hours pass, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. We wrap around. Mathematicians call this "working modulo 12", and the answer is simply the remainder after dividing by 12. So 15 becomes 3, because 15 divided by 12 leaves 3.

We can use any size of clock. A clock with 23 hours works the same way: 30 becomes 7, because 30 divided by 23 leaves 7.

Now take powers. Raising a number to a power just means multiplying it by itself repeatedly. Written out, 5 to the power 3 is 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. On our 23-hour clock, 125 wraps around to 10, since 23 × 5 = 115 and 125 − 115 = 10.

Here is the key observation. Going forward is easy: given 5, a power, and a clock size, you can find the result. Going backward is hard: if I tell you that 5 raised to some secret power lands on 8 on the 23-hour clock, finding that power means trying many possibilities, because the wrap-around scrambles the numbers so they follow no obvious pattern. With a tiny clock like 23, you can just try them all. With a clock of several hundred digits, even the fastest computers cannot.

**The exchange, step by step**

Here is the whole thing with small numbers. Real systems use enormous ones, but the steps are identical.

1. **Agree publicly.** Alice and Bob announce two numbers: a clock size of 23, and a base number of 5. Eve hears both. These are the "yellow paint".

2. **Choose private numbers.** Alice secretly picks 6. Bob secretly picks 15. They tell no one. These are the "red" and "blue".

3. **Compute and swap.** Alice calculates 5 to the power 6 on the 23-hour clock. That is 15,625, which wraps around to 8. She sends 8 to Bob. Bob calculates 5 to the power 15 on the same clock, which comes to 19, and sends 19 to Alice. These are the half-mixed tubs. Eve hears 8 and 19.

4. **Finish privately.** Alice takes Bob's number, 19, and raises it to her secret power 6. On the 23-hour clock the result is 2. Bob takes Alice's number, 8, and raises it to his secret power 15. His result is also 2.

They have both arrived at 2, and neither ever said it aloud.

**Why the answers match**

This is not luck. Alice's final number is Bob's public number raised to her secret power. But Bob's public number was 5 raised to his secret power. So Alice has really computed 5 raised to Bob's power, raised to her power. Bob has computed 5 raised to Alice's power, raised to his power.

Both amount to 5 multiplied by itself (6 × 15) = 90 times, with the clock wrapping as it goes. The order in which you apply the powers does not matter, just as 2 × 3 and 3 × 2 are both 6. That is why the two routes land on the same number. It is the maths version of mixing yellow, red and blue in either order.

**What Eve is left with**

Eve knows the clock size 23, the base 5, and the two swapped numbers 8 and 19. To find the secret, she needs to discover either Alice's power or Bob's. Our example is small enough that she could just test powers one by one. Try 5 to the power 1, 2, 3, and so on, until one lands on 8. She finds 6 quickly, and then she can compute the shared secret herself.

Real systems make this fail by choosing a clock with hundreds of digits and secret powers just as long. Computing the forward direction still takes a computer a fraction of a second, because there are clever shortcuts for large powers. But trying every possible secret would take longer than the age of the universe. This lopsided difficulty is the whole point.

One caution. Nobody has proved that the backward direction is truly impossible. It is simply that after decades of effort by many clever people, no fast method has been found. The security rests on that long record of failure, not on a mathematical guarantee.

**Why it is called public-key**

The numbers 8 and 19 are called public keys: they can be shouted from the rooftops with no harm done. The secret powers 6 and 15 are private keys, and must never be shared. The name captures the surprising idea at the centre of all this: a lock that anyone can see, whose key only you hold.

A related invention, known as RSA, uses the same spirit for a slightly different job. You publish a number that anyone can use to lock a message, but only you hold the private number that unlocks it. Its difficulty rests on a different hard problem: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but taking the result and recovering the two primes is brutally hard. Both methods share the same heart, an easy road one way and a nearly impassable road back.

**Where you meet it**

When your browser shows a padlock, a version of this exchange has just happened between your device and a website you have never visited before. Your devices agree a secret in a fraction of a second, over a line that anyone could be listening to, and then use that secret to scramble everything that follows.

The remarkable thing is how little is needed: some clock arithmetic, a few numbers said out loud, and two private choices that never leave their owners' heads.
