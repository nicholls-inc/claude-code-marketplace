Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon.

Start with what a parking mandate actually is. It is a rule that tells a developer, a church, a restaurant or a small landlord how much storage for private cars they must build, regardless of whether they think anyone will use it. The number usually comes from a manual compiled decades ago from surveys of suburban sites with free parking, and then gets copied from one municipal code to the next. Nobody chose it by weighing costs against benefits. It is a guess that hardened into law, and it governs what gets built on nearly every lot in the country.

The costs are large and land on people who never see them itemized. A surface space costs a few thousand dollars to build and takes up roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles. A structured space can run tens of thousands, and an underground one more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, home prices and menu prices, and everyone pays them, including households that own no car. Researchers who have studied this, most famously Donald Shoup, have shown that bundling parking into every building quietly transfers money from people who drive less to people who drive more. A mandate that sounds neutral is a regressive tax.

Then there is the land. Every required space is ground that could have held a bedroom, a shop, a clinic or a patch of grass. In many American downtowns, more area is devoted to parking than to the buildings it serves. That sprawl makes places thinner and more spread out, which makes walking, cycling and transit less practical, which in turn makes people feel they need a car, which seems to justify the mandate. The policy manufactures the demand it claims to be responding to.

It also kills projects outright. Plenty of small infill sites, such as a narrow lot beside a corner store or an old building one could convert to apartments, cannot physically fit the required spaces. The proposal fails on arithmetic before anyone argues about design. Older neighborhoods that were built before these rules, the ones people pay a premium to live in, would often be illegal to build today. Mandates favor big sites at the edge of town and penalize the incremental, human-scale development that cities say they want.

The usual objection is that without mandates, new buildings will flood the street with cars and ruin parking for everyone. That fear gets the situation backwards. Removing a minimum does not forbid parking. It lets the builder decide. A developer who thinks tenants want a space will still build one, because it makes the units easier to rent. Lenders and buyers will insist on it where it matters. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision rather than a bureaucratic one, and the result varies sensibly: more in car-dependent suburbs, less near stations and main streets. Where cities have tried this, the evidence is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its requirements citywide in 2017, and developers there have mostly continued to include parking where the market calls for it while a number of projects that were previously infeasible became possible. Minneapolis, Seattle, parts of California and others have moved the same way, and the sky has not fallen.

Spillover onto streets is a real concern, but it is a pricing problem with a pricing solution. If curb parking is free and scarce, it will always fill up. Charging for it, using residential permits where needed, and returning the revenue to the neighborhood addresses the problem directly, and it does so without making every new building pay for spaces it may not need. Mandates treat a symptom of underpriced curbs by overbuilding private lots.

Removing minimums also fits the goals cities already announce. Municipalities say they want more housing, lower rents, lower emissions and more vibrant streets. Parking requirements work against each one. They raise construction costs, they lock in car dependence, and they flatten the mix of uses that makes a block interesting. It is odd to adopt an ambitious housing target and leave in place a rule that quietly deletes a share of the homes that could meet it.

To be fair, repeal is not a magic wand. Some places will see poorly planned buildings, and transitional frictions are real. Cities should pair reform with better curb management, accessible parking guarantees for people with disabilities, and good transit investment. But those are arguments for doing the job well, not for keeping a rule whose original justification was a rough guess and whose side effects are now well documented.

The deeper point is about who should make the decision. A city planner cannot know how many cars a particular building's residents will own in ten years. The people putting up the money, and the people choosing where to live, have far better information. Cities should set the rules that protect safety and the public realm and leave the question of how many parking spaces to build to the people who will live with the answer. Scrap the minimums.
