**The Leap Second, and Why Time's Keepers Are Abandoning It**

A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to the world's official clocks to keep them in step with the spinning Earth. Since 1972, it has been inserted 27 times, always at the end of June or December, so that a minute occasionally runs to 61 seconds.

The need arises because there are two ways of measuring time, and they do not agree. The first is astronomical: a day is one turn of the Earth, and noon is when the Sun is highest. The second is atomic. Since 1967, the second has been defined by the vibrations of caesium atoms, which tick with extraordinary regularity. Atomic clocks keep time far better than the planet does.

The Earth is a poor timekeeper. Tides drag on its rotation and slow it by tiny amounts, while earthquakes, ice melt and the churning of its molten core nudge it unpredictably. For most of the past half-century the average day has run slightly longer than 86,400 atomic seconds, so civil time, known as Coordinated Universal Time, has needed an occasional second added to stay within 0.9 of a second of the Sun-based reading.

The system worked well enough when clocks were mostly mechanical. It works badly now. Computers, telecommunications networks, financial exchanges and satellite navigation all depend on precisely synchronised time, and a minute with 61 seconds is something most software does not expect. Because leap seconds are announced only a few months ahead, since Earth's rotation cannot be forecast far in advance, systems cannot be prepared long beforehand. Different companies have handled the extra second in different ways, with some "smearing" it across a day. Such differences have caused outages and data errors, and engineers have long complained that every leap second is a gamble.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that governs units of measurement, voted to stop. By 2035, the gap between atomic and Earth-based time will be allowed to grow well beyond a second, with the precise new limit to be settled later. Leap seconds will effectively be abandoned, probably for a century or so, until the drift becomes large enough to require a different fix, such as adjusting by a minute at once.

Ordinary people will notice nothing. Our clocks will not suddenly be out of kilter with daylight; the drift amounts to roughly a minute every few decades. A further wrinkle is that Earth's spin has recently been running slightly fast, so a negative leap second, the first ever, might have been needed. That prospect made the case for scrapping the practice even stronger.

The decision reflects a modern priority: it is better for time to be smooth and dependable for machines than perfectly aligned with the Sun.
