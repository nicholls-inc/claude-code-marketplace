## The problem

Imagine you want to send a private message to someone you have never met. You can't whisper, because you're not in the same room. Everything you say travels along a line that anyone can tap, and you have no password you both already know. It sounds impossible. How can two people agree on a secret when every word they exchange is overheard?

For most of history, the answer was that they couldn't. Secret codes needed a shared key, such as a codebook or a password, and that key had to be handed over in person first. In the 1970s a few mathematicians found a way around this. The result is called public-key cryptography, and it protects almost everything you do online, from banking to messaging.

The trick rests on one idea. Some mathematical jobs are easy to do and nearly impossible to undo. Once you see that, the rest follows.

## Easy one way, hard the other

Here is a small example. Take two large prime numbers, say 7,919 and 7,927, and multiply them. With a calculator, you get 62,771,... well, a definite answer, in a second. Now suppose I give you only the answer and ask which two primes produced it. You would have to guess and test, and keep going until you got lucky. Multiplying is quick. Un-multiplying is slow, and the gap widens enormously as the numbers grow.

Mathematicians call this a one-way function. The cryptography we'll look at uses a close cousin, built on clock arithmetic.

## Clock arithmetic

On a clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, not 15. When you pass 12, you wrap around to the start. Mathematicians call this "modular arithmetic" and write it as "mod". On a 12-hour clock, 15 mod 12 = 3.

We can use any clock size. Let's use a clock with 23 positions. Then 30 mod 23 = 7, because 30 is one full lap of 23 plus 7 more.

Now consider powers. 5 to the power of 3 means 5 x 5 x 5 = 125. On our 23-position clock, 125 mod 23 = 10, since 23 x 5 = 115 and 125 - 115 = 10.

Going forward is simple: pick a power, work out the answer. But going backward is hard. If I tell you that 5 raised to some secret power gives 8 on a 23-clock, can you find the power? With a clock this small, yes, you can try them all in a minute. But when the clock has hundreds of digits, the answers jump around so unpredictably that no known shortcut exists. Trying every possibility would take longer than the age of the universe.

## The paint analogy

Before the numbers, here is a picture. Suppose Alice and Bob each have a private colour, and they also share one public colour, yellow, which everyone can see.

Alice mixes her private colour (red) with the yellow and sends the result across the line. Bob mixes his private colour (blue) with the yellow and sends that across. A spy sees both mixtures and the yellow.

The spy can't get there, because un-mixing paint to recover the separate private colours is practically impossible. The spy would need red or blue, and neither was ever sent.

That is the whole idea. The numbers just make it precise.

## The same idea with numbers

This method is called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, after two of its inventors. We'll use small numbers so you can check the arithmetic yourself.

They have both arrived at 2. Neither ever transmitted it.

## The lock-and-key version

Diffie-Hellman agrees a secret between two people. A second approach, called RSA after its inventors, works more like a padlock.

Picture Bob making thousands of identical open padlocks and leaving them in a public place. Only Bob has the key that opens them. Alice takes one, puts her message in a box, snaps the padlock shut, and sends the box. Even Alice cannot reopen it. Nor can the spy. Only Bob, with his private key, can.

In mathematical terms, the padlock is Bob's public key and the key is his private key. They're a matched pair, built from those two large primes we met earlier. Anyone can use the public half to lock a message. Only the holder of the private half, who knows the primes, can unlock it. The whole scheme depends on the fact that multiplying primes is easy, and splitting the product back into primes is not.

## An honest caveat

There is one real weakness, and it's worth being straight about. Public-key methods guarantee that nobody listening in can read the secret. They don't, on their own, prove who you are talking to. If a spy sat in the middle, pretending to be Bob to Alice and pretending to be Alice to Bob, each would agree a secret with the spy rather than with each other.

The fix is for Bob's public key to be vouched for by someone trusted, in a certificate signed by an authority that your browser already knows. That is what the padlock symbol in your address bar represents.
