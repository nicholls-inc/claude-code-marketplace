A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). It works in two stages, and the second stage explains why the last stretch of charging is slow.

In the first stage, a charger forces a fixed current into the cell, typically between 0.5C and 1C. A 1C rate would fill the nominal capacity in one hour. Cell terminal voltage rises steadily during this stage. The voltage reflects the electrochemical potential difference between the two electrodes plus the voltage drop across the cell's internal resistance. For a common graphite and layered-oxide cell, the voltage climbs from about 3.0 V toward 4.2 V. Charging moves lithium ions out of the cathode, through the electrolyte, and into the graphite anode, where they sit between the carbon layers. Electrons travel through the external circuit.

When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, usually 4.2 V, the charger stops holding current constant and holds voltage constant instead. That limit exists for safety and longevity. Above it, the electrolyte oxidises at the cathode, the cathode structure degrades, and lithium metal can plate onto the anode, which is dangerous. The charger therefore cannot push the voltage higher, whatever the cell would otherwise accept.

The slowdown follows from basic circuit behaviour. The terminal voltage equals the open-circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance. As the cell fills, its open-circuit voltage rises toward the 4.2 V limit. With the terminal voltage pinned, the gap available to drive current shrinks. The current must therefore fall. A rough analogy is filling a pressurised tank from a regulated supply. As tank pressure approaches supply pressure, the flow decays toward zero. The current decays roughly exponentially, and the charger ends the process when it drops to a cutoff, often 0.05C to 0.1C.

A second effect reinforces this. Lithium diffuses slowly through solid electrode particles. At high states of charge, the outer layer of each particle becomes saturated while the core is still filling. This builds a concentration gradient and raises the effective resistance, so the same voltage drives less current. The anode surface potential also falls toward that of lithium metal near full charge. Holding the current high there would risk plating, so the lower current in the constant voltage stage is protective.

The constant voltage stage commonly delivers the final 10 to 20 percent of capacity and can take as long as the first stage. Fast-charging designs shorten it by tapering current in steps, managing temperature, and using electrodes and electrolytes that reduce resistance.
