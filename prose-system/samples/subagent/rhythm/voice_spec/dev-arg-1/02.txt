Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. The case rests on a plain question about who decides. A minimum tells a developer how many spaces to build, whatever the developer, the lender or the future tenants think is needed. Removing it does not ban parking. It returns the decision to the people who will pay for the spaces and live with them.

Most minimums were written in the middle of the twentieth century, when planners wanted to stop new buildings from pushing cars onto the street. Many of the numbers came from the Institute of Transportation Engineers, whose Parking Generation manual reports peak parking use at surveyed sites, a large share of them in suburbs with free parking and little public transport. A rule such as one space per flat, or one for every 300 square feet of restaurant floor, is therefore based on how much parking people use when it costs them nothing. The rule then keeps it costing nothing at the point of use, because the space is paid for through the rent, the bill or the price of the goods. Donald Shoup, the planner at UCLA whose 2005 book The High Cost of Free Parking made this argument, called the result a subsidy for driving that is paid by everyone, including people who do not drive.

The costs are real and can be stated in numbers. Estimates for a space in a multi-storey garage commonly run from about $25,000 to $50,000, and underground spaces cost more. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once the aisle is counted, which is more than the floor area of many small flats. When a city requires two spaces for a three-bedroom home, the builder adds that cost to the price of the home. A household with no car pays for parking it will not use, and in practice households with lower incomes are more likely to own fewer cars. On small or awkward lots the requirement can make a project impossible, so corner shops, conversions of old buildings and small apartment blocks are often never proposed.

Several places have tried the change. Auckland abolished its minimums in 2016, Buffalo, New York did so citywide in 2017, and Minneapolis followed in 2021. England dropped national minimum standards in 2011 and left the matter to local authorities. Early studies of cities that loosened their rules, Seattle among them, found developers building fewer spaces than the old requirements would have forced on them, but not none. Most new buildings still include parking where buyers and tenants want it, and many lenders still ask for it. What changes is the margin. A developer can build ten flats on a small lot with four spaces, or none if the site is near a rail station, and a restaurant can reuse an old building without first buying the neighbouring plot for a car park. Those are the projects the old rules tended to prevent.

The strongest objection is that cars from new buildings will spill onto neighbouring streets and make parking harder for existing residents. This is a fair concern and it should be answered directly. In my view the answer is to manage the street, not to regulate the building. A kerb that is free and unlimited will fill up whatever the rules say about private lots. A residential permit scheme, or a meter set at a price that leaves one or two spaces empty on each block, keeps the street usable and gives the city revenue it can spend on the neighbourhood. Shoup has argued for returning that revenue to the streets where it is collected, so that residents see a benefit from the charge. Cities that remove minimums without touching their kerb policy will get less from the reform than those that do both.

A second objection is that cities with poor public transport cannot do without minimums, because most people there must drive. The premise is partly right and the conclusion does not follow. Where most people drive, most developers will build parking, since a block of flats with no spaces would be hard to let. The rule is not protecting anyone in that case. Where it does bind is in the exceptions: the site near a bus corridor, the building meant for older tenants who have given up driving, the small business that would be fine with five spaces and is told it needs twelve. A flexible rule lets those cases be judged on their merits, and a fixed rule does not.

There is also a question of what a planning system is for. Zoning exists to limit harms that one owner can impose on neighbours, such as noise, fire risk or a building that blocks light. An empty parking space is not a harm of that kind. A shortage of spaces falls mainly on the developer and the tenants, who have every reason to avoid it. When a city sets the number by formula, it replaces local knowledge with an average drawn from other places, and the average is usually wrong for the site in front of it.

Removing minimums will not make a city affordable by itself, and it will not reduce car use overnight. Housing costs depend on land, labour, interest rates and many other rules. But it is a cheap reform, one that needs no new spending and no new agency, and it takes away a requirement whose costs are measurable and whose benefits are mostly assumed. Cities that want more homes, more small businesses and fewer subsidies for a single way of travelling should start by deleting the clause.
