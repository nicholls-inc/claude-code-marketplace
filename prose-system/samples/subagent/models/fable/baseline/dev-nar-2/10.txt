Apollo 13: The Flight That Came Back

Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, bound for the hills of the Moon's Fra Mauro region. It was to be the third lunar landing, and by then the public had begun to treat Moon flights as routine. The commander, Jim Lovell, was the most experienced astronaut in the world, on his fourth spaceflight. Fred Haise would fly the lunar module with him to the surface. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only days before launch, replacing Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by the flight surgeons.

For two days the flight was quiet. The spacecraft was really two vehicles docked nose to nose. The command module, Odyssey, was the crew's home and the only part that could survive re-entry. Attached behind it was the service module, which carried the main engine, the oxygen and the fuel cells that made electricity and water. The lunar module, Aquarius, was a fragile lander built to carry two men to the Moon's surface and back.

On the evening of April 13, nearly 56 hours into the flight and about 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew finished a television broadcast that none of the major networks carried live. Minutes later, mission control asked Swigert to perform a routine chore: switching on fans to stir the supercooled oxygen in the service module's tanks. He flipped the switches.

Inside oxygen tank 2, damaged wiring sparked. The insulation burned, the pressure soared, and the tank burst. The crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights came on across the instrument panel. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Asked to repeat, Lovell said it again: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first, controllers in Houston suspected faulty instruments, because so many readings had gone wrong at once. Then Lovell looked out a window and reported that the ship was venting something into space. It was oxygen. The explosion had destroyed one tank and damaged the other, and the remaining supply was bleeding away. Without oxygen the fuel cells would die, and with them Odyssey's power, water and air.

The landing was abandoned within the hour. The question now was whether the crew could be brought home alive.

The answer was Aquarius. The lander had its own oxygen, batteries and engine, and it would have to serve as a lifeboat. As Odyssey's last fuel cell faded, Lovell and Haise hurried through the tunnel to power up the lunar module, while Swigert shut down the command module completely to save its small re-entry batteries for the final hours of the flight. In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his fellow flight directors organized their teams around a single aim: stretching a vehicle designed to keep two men alive for about two days so that it could keep three alive for four.

The spacecraft was still heading away from Earth. Turning around directly would have required the service module's main engine, and nobody knew whether the explosion had damaged it. Instead, the controllers chose to let the ship continue around the Moon and use lunar gravity to swing it back. A short firing of the lander's descent engine put Apollo 13 on a path that would return it to Earth. The crew passed behind the Moon, out of radio contact, about 158 miles above the surface they had trained to walk on. At that point they were farther from Earth than any humans had ever been. Two hours after rounding the Moon, they fired the lander's engine again, for more than four minutes, to shorten the trip home by about ten hours and aim for a landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Then came the long coast. To save electricity, nearly everything in Aquarius was switched off, including the guidance computer and the heaters. The temperature in the cabin fell to near freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men, dressed in thin flight suits, slept badly or not at all. Water was needed to cool the lander's electronics, so each man's ration was cut to about six ounces a day. All three became dehydrated, and Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever.

A more urgent danger was building in the air they breathed. The lander's chemical canisters, which absorbed the carbon dioxide the men exhaled, were being used up, and the carbon dioxide level was climbing toward a point that would cause confusion and eventually death. Odyssey had plenty of fresh canisters, but they were square, and the sockets in Aquarius were round. Engineers in Houston worked out a fix using only items aboard the spacecraft: plastic bags, the cardboard cover of a flight manual, a hose from a spacesuit and gray duct tape. They read the instructions up to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the contraption, which they called the mailbox, and the carbon dioxide level dropped within the hour.

The spacecraft also drifted slightly off course, and its path needed correcting. With the guidance computer shut down, Lovell steered by eye, holding the Earth in the lander's window and keeping the line between its day and night sides aligned with the crosshairs, while Swigert timed the 14-second burn with his wristwatch. It worked.

One problem remained. Odyssey had been shut down cold for days, something never planned for and never tested. It had to be brought back to life on batteries with very little power to spare, and its electronics were damp with condensation that might cause a short circuit. In Houston, a team that included Ken Mattingly, the grounded astronaut, spent days in the simulators working out a sequence of switch settings that would stay within the power budget. They finished the procedure and read it to an exhausted Swigert, who copied it down by hand.

On April 17, as Earth filled the windows, the crew cast off the service module and saw the damage for the first time. An entire side panel, running the length of the module, had been blown away. They powered up Odyssey, and it came on without a spark. Then they climbed in, sealed the hatch and released Aquarius. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," mission control radioed.

Nobody knew whether the explosion had cracked the heat shield or whether the parachutes would still work. Re-entry always cut off radio contact for several minutes as hot gas surrounded the capsule. This time the silence ran more than a minute longer than expected, while hundreds of millions of people watched on television. Then Swigert's voice came through, and the cameras found three parachutes over the South Pacific. Odyssey splashed down near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, just under six days after launch.

Investigators later traced the explosion to a chain of small mistakes made years earlier. The tank had been jarred during handling, and its internal thermostat switches had never been upgraded to match the higher voltage of the launch pad's ground equipment. During a test before launch, the switches fused shut, the heaters ran for hours, and the wiring insulation inside the tank was baked. It had been waiting to fail ever since.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon. NASA called it a successful failure: the mission was lost, but the three men who flew it came home.
