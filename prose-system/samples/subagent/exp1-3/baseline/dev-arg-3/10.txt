**Keep the Loops: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years the argument returns, usually after a story about a country that has abandoned cursive. Finland dropped compulsory joined-up writing from its national curriculum in 2016, and American states have flip-flopped on the question for a decade. The case against is easy to state. Children type more than they write, adults rarely join their letters, and the hours spent on looped ascenders could go to coding or critical thinking. It sounds like common sense, but I think it is wrong. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and teach it properly. The reasons are practical, not nostalgic.

**What the argument is really about**

First, a clarification. Nobody serious is proposing that children stop learning to write by hand. Print letters, taught early, are safe in every version of this debate. The question is narrower: should children, usually around seven or eight, be taught to connect their letters in a flowing script? That is a modest request. A school day can easily hold ten minutes of focused practice, and the benefits arrive early and last.

**Speed and fluency**

The strongest argument is speed. Handwriting is still the main way children show what they know. Exams, classroom tasks and note-taking all depend on it, and they will for the foreseeable future. A child who prints every letter lifts the pen after each one. Each lift is a small interruption: the hand stops, repositions, and starts again. A joined script removes many of those interruptions. Writing is faster and the hand tires less, so more of the child's attention stays on what they want to say.

This matters because handwriting is not only a motor skill. Research on writing development, including work by Virginia Berninger and colleagues, has repeatedly found that when letter formation is automatic, children write longer, more fluent and better organised texts. Working memory is limited. If a child has to think about how to form each letter, there is less capacity left for spelling, grammar and ideas. Automaticity frees the mind. A fluent, joined hand is one reliable route to that automaticity, and the data on secondary students tells the same story: legible, quick writers do better under timed conditions, because exams reward the person who can get thoughts onto paper before time runs out.

Critics point out that print can be quick too, and for some adults it is. That is true. But adults who print quickly have usually spent years building the fluency, and the route most schools use to build it is the one I am defending. Teaching joining does not forbid print. It gives children a faster tool and lets them decide later how to use it.

**Spelling and the motor memory of words**

Joined writing also helps with spelling. When a child writes a word in a single flowing movement, the word becomes a pattern the hand remembers. Common letter strings such as "-ing", "-tion" and "-ough" turn into familiar shapes, not a sequence of separate decisions. Teachers who use cursive often report that children who struggle with spelling find joined writing helpful, partly because the movement reinforces the order of the letters. The evidence here is not overwhelming, and I do not want to oversell it. But there is a plausible mechanism and a good deal of classroom experience behind it, and the cost of trying it is close to zero.

It also seems to help some children with specific difficulties. Dyslexic children often reverse letters such as b and d. In a joined script, those letters begin and end differently, so the confusion shrinks. Occupational therapists and specialist teachers have long recommended it for this reason. A policy that drops joined writing for everyone removes a useful support for the children who most need one.

**The brain on paper**

Then there is the research on handwriting and learning more broadly. Studies using brain imaging, such as work by Karin James and others, suggest that when young children form letters by hand, they activate reading-related circuits more strongly than when they type or trace. Mueller and Oppenheimer's much-cited study found that students taking notes by hand tended to process material more deeply than those typing, though later replications have found the effect smaller than first reported. I would not claim that any of this proves cursive is uniquely valuable. Most of the evidence concerns handwriting in general, not joined handwriting in particular.

But that is exactly the point. If handwriting matters, and the evidence says it does, then the sensible aim is to make children good at it. Good at it means fast, legible and comfortable. A child who writes laboriously in print, lifting the pen eighty times in a sentence, will avoid writing and write less. A child with a fluent joined hand will do it without strain. The best case for cursive is that it is the most efficient way most children can reach that goal.

**Reading the world**

A final practical argument concerns reading. Joined writing is everywhere that matters to a child's life outside school: birthday cards from grandparents, handwritten recipes, old letters, historical documents, the notes teachers write in margins. A literate adult should be able to read these. Archives around the world are full of handwritten records, and people who cannot read cursive are cut off from their own family histories. Children who learn to join their letters also learn to read other people's joined letters, and that takes far less time than teaching the skill later to an adult who never encountered it.

There is a small but real civic dimension too. A signature is still a legal act. Learning to sign your name fluently, with a mark that is recognisably yours, is a modest form of identity that digital life has not replaced.

**The strongest objections**

The most serious objection is opportunity cost. School time is finite, and typing is a skill children definitely need. I agree that keyboarding must be taught, and early. But the two do not compete as much as the objection assumes. Joined handwriting needs short, regular practice, not long lessons, and most of the benefit comes in the first year or two of teaching. Typing is learned later and more quickly, because older children already understand letters, words and sentences. The time cost of cursive is small compared with the time spent on many other things schools do.

A second objection is that adults do not use it. Surveys suggest many adults write in a hybrid style, part print and part joined. But that is the argument for teaching it. Those adults developed a personal fast hand because they were taught the joins at school. Their hybrid is built on the foundation. Drop the foundation and you do not get a creative new style; you get children who never developed fluency at all. The evidence from places that reduced cursive teaching, including some US districts, is that handwriting standards slipped and teachers complained about illegible work.

A third objection says the style is outdated, with its elaborate capital letters and loops that no one needs. This has some force against old-fashioned scripts. The answer is not to abolish joined writing but to modernise it. Many schools now teach a simple, largely unloopy joined style with plain capitals and minimal flourishes. That keeps the speed and flow while discarding the fussiness. Teaching it well means choosing a sensible script and not forcing children to copy Victorian copperplate.

**What a good policy looks like**

I would argue for a clear but modest approach. Teach print first, in the reception years, alongside early reading. Introduce joining around age seven, once letter shapes are secure. Use a simple, consistent script across the school. Give short daily practice, not lengthy copying exercises. Teach keyboarding from about age eight or nine, as a complementary skill. And let children develop their own personal style in secondary school, as most do, once the fundamentals are in place.

This approach respects both sides. It does not treat cursive as sacred, and it does not pretend that screens have made the pen obsolete. It treats handwriting as a tool whose purpose is fluent, legible communication, and it picks the method that most reliably gets children there.

**Conclusion**

The case for abandoning joined-up handwriting rests on the idea that it is a relic. But a skill is not a relic if it still does real work. Joined writing makes children faster, supports spelling, helps some of the children who struggle most, and opens the door to a world of handwritten material. The costs are small and the evidence, while not conclusive, points mostly one way. Schools should keep teaching it, teach it in a modern form, and teach it with the same seriousness they give to any other basic skill. Children will be typing for the rest of their lives. They will also, every day, still need to pick up a pen. They should be able to use it well.
