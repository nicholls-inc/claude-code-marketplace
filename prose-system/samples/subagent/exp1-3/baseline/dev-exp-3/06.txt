# How Two Strangers Can Agree on a Secret in Public

## The puzzle

Suppose you and I have never met. We cannot meet in person, and every message between us passes through a line that anyone can listen to. We want to end up knowing the same secret number, a number nobody else knows, so that we can use it as the key to a lock for our later messages.

At first this sounds impossible. If every word I say is overheard, how could anything stay hidden? Surely whatever I tell you, the eavesdropper hears too.

The way out is a piece of cleverness from the 1970s. It rests on a simple observation: some jobs are easy to do and almost impossible to undo. Public-key cryptography is built from such jobs.

## Easy one way, hard the other way

You already know several one-way jobs from everyday life.

Stirring cream into coffee takes a second. Getting the cream back out is hopeless. Breaking an egg is easy; unbreaking it is not.

Maths has such jobs too. Multiply 101 by 103 and you get 10,403 in a few seconds with pencil and paper. Now go the other way. Someone hands you 10,403 and asks which two whole numbers multiplied together make it. You would have to try dividing by 2, 3, 5, 7 and so on until something fits. For small numbers that is tolerable. For numbers hundreds of digits long, even the world's fastest computers would need longer than the age of the universe, using the best methods anyone has found.

The idea is to build a conversation out of steps that are easy for us and hard for the eavesdropper.

## The paint-mixing picture

Here is the whole trick with paint, before any numbers.

You and I agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow. Everyone hears this, and it is fine.

Each of us privately picks a secret colour. You pick red. I pick blue. We never tell anyone.

You mix your secret red into the yellow and send me the resulting orange-ish mixture in a jar. I mix my secret blue into the yellow and send you my greenish mixture. The eavesdropper sees both jars go by and can copy them.

Now you take my jar and add your secret red to it. I take your jar and add my secret blue to it. We both end up with yellow plus red plus blue, the same muddy brown, because the order of mixing does not matter.

The eavesdropper holds the yellow, your jar and my jar. To get the brown, they would need to add a secret colour, and they do not have either of ours. They cannot unmix a jar to pull the red or the blue back out. Mixing is easy and unmixing is not, so the brown is a secret only we share.

Now we replace paint with numbers.

## The numbers version

We need a one-way job that works on numbers. The one used is **clock arithmetic**, which you may have met at school.

On a 12-hour clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, because after reaching 12 you start counting again from 0. We write this as "15 mod 12 = 3", which just means "the remainder when you divide 15 by 12". We can use a clock of any size. A "mod 23" clock has the numbers 0 to 22 and wraps round after that.

The one-way job is this. Pick a base number, say 5. Raise it to a power, then take the remainder on the clock.

- 5 to the power 6 is 15,625. Divide by 23: 23 goes into it 679 times (that is 15,617), leaving remainder **8**.

Going forwards is mechanical. Going backwards is the problem. If I tell you "5 to some power, on a mod-23 clock, gives 8" and ask you for the power, you have no shortcut. You have to try powers one by one: 5 to the 1, to the 2, to the 3, and so on, until you hit 8. With a clock of 23 that takes seconds. With a clock whose size has hundreds of digits, the wrapping scrambles the answers so thoroughly that nobody knows a way to search faster than trying a truly astronomical number of possibilities.

## A full worked example

Here is the real exchange, with small numbers so you can check the sums yourself. Call the two people Alice and Bob.

**Step 1: agree publicly.** Alice and Bob announce two numbers for all to hear: the clock size **23** and the base **5**. These are the "yellow paint".

**Step 2: each picks a secret.** Alice secretly chooses **6**. Bob secretly chooses **15**. These are never sent.

**Step 3: each mixes and sends.**

- Alice computes 5 to the power 6 on the mod-23 clock. As above, that is **8**. She sends 8 to Bob.
- Bob computes 5 to the power 15 on the mod-23 clock. The answer is **19**. He sends 19 to Alice.

The eavesdropper now knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. That is everything that crossed the line.

**Step 4: each mixes in their own secret.**

- Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power 6, on the clock. 19 to the 6 comes to **2**.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power 15, on the clock. 8 to the 15 also comes to **2**.

Both arrive at **2**. That is their shared secret.

## Why do both get the same answer?

This is the part that feels like magic, but it is just a fact about powers. Alice's final number is 5 raised to (15 then 6) and Bob's is 5 raised to (6 then 15). In both cases the base 5 is raised to the power 6 × 15 = 90, and 6 × 15 is the same as 15 × 6. The order does not matter, which is exactly why the paint colours came out the same however we stirred them. The clock wrapping does not disturb this, because remainders behave consistently when you multiply and re-multiply.

You can check it directly: 5 to the 90th power, on a mod-23 clock, is 2.

## What the eavesdropper would have to do

To get the secret 2, the eavesdropper needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. They know 8 is "5 to some power" and 19 is "5 to some power", but recovering the power is the hard backwards job. With a clock of 23 they could simply try everything, and they would find 6 in moments. That is why real systems use clock sizes with hundreds of digits. The sums for Alice and Bob stay quick, because raising to a power can be done by repeated squaring in a few hundred steps. The eavesdropper's job becomes impossible in practice.

So the security never depended on hiding what we said. It depends on the gap between a job that takes us moments and a job that would take them longer than the lifetime of the Sun.

## Where the name "public key" comes from

In this scheme, the numbers that cross the line (8 and 19) are called **public keys**. They can be shown to the whole world. The secret numbers (6 and 15) are the **private keys**, and they never leave their owner.

Other schemes use the same split differently. The well-known RSA method uses the multiplying-versus-factoring puzzle from earlier. Someone publishes a big number made by multiplying two secret primes. Anyone can use that public number to lock a message. Only the person who knows the two secret primes can unlock it. Again the lock is public and easy to close, and the key to open it is private.

## What this does and does not give you

This exchange keeps a secret from a listener. It does not by itself prove you are talking to the right person. Someone could stand in the middle, run one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, and relay the messages between them. Real systems solve this by adding a way to check identity, for example certificates that vouch for who owns a public key. That is a separate problem from the one solved here.

Once the shared secret exists, the two people use it as the key to an ordinary fast lock for their actual conversation. This combination protects most of what you do online, from banking to messaging.

## The takeaway

Two strangers can agree on a secret in public because they never send the secret. Each sends only the result of an easy job, and each finishes the job privately with something only they hold. The listener sees the middle of the process and cannot run it backwards.
