Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). The logic is simple: current pushes lithium ions from the cathode, through the electrolyte, and into the graphite anode, but the cell tolerates only so much of that push before something degrades.

In the first stage, the charger holds current fixed, typically between 0.5C and 1C. (C-rate is current relative to capacity, so 1C charges a 3 Ah cell at 3 A.) The charger lets cell voltage float upward as it must. For most of this stage the terminal voltage rises gently from about 3.4 V to 4.2 V, and the cell absorbs roughly 70 to 80 percent of its capacity. Progress is fast and nearly linear.

Then the voltage ceiling is reached. For a typical cobalt-based cell, 4.2 V is a hard limit. Go higher and the electrolyte oxidizes at the cathode, the cathode structure strains, and lithium metal can plate on the anode, which shortens life and can cause internal shorts. So the charger switches modes. It now holds voltage at 4.2 V and lets the current fall to whatever the cell will accept. This is the constant-voltage stage. Charging ends when current has decayed to a cutoff, commonly 0.05C to 0.1C.

Why must the current fall? Think of terminal voltage as the cell's open-circuit voltage plus the drop across its internal resistance. Open-circuit voltage climbs as the cell fills, because the electrodes' chemical potential depends on state of charge. With terminal voltage pinned, the headroom left to drive current shrinks, and current is roughly that headroom divided by internal resistance. As the cell approaches full, headroom approaches zero, so current decays in a roughly exponential tail, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor.

A second effect compounds this. Ions must diffuse into the graphite particles, and near full charge the outer layers are already crowded with lithium. The concentration gradient driving further diffusion flattens, so the interior fills slowly. The surface can reach its limit while the core still has room.

The practical consequence is that the last 20 percent of charge takes about as long as the first 80. Fast chargers therefore stop early, often near 80 percent, because the tail yields little energy for a lot of time, and extra time at high voltage accelerates aging. Temperature matters as well. Cold cells have higher internal resistance and a greater plating risk, so the charger derates current accordingly.
