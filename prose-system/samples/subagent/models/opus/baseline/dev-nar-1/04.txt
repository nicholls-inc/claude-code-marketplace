The Broad Street Pump

In the last days of August 1854, the people of Soho in London began to die at a rate nobody alive had seen before. The neighbourhood was crowded and poor. Families shared single rooms in tall, sagging houses, and tailors, shoemakers and small workshops crammed the narrow streets around Golden Square. On the night of 31 August, cholera arrived. It was a disease Londoners knew and dreaded. It began with violent diarrhoea and vomiting, and the body lost water so fast that a healthy adult could be dead within a day. Within three days, more than a hundred people living within a few hundred yards of each other were dead. By the end of the first week, most of those who could leave had fled.

Most doctors and officials of the time thought they knew the cause. The accepted theory said that diseases like cholera spread through "miasma", a poisonous bad air that rose from rotting rubbish, sewage and filth. In a city that stank as badly as Victorian London, the idea seemed obvious. If the air smelled foul, the air must be what made people ill.

John Snow was not convinced. He was a London doctor, a quiet, careful man who did not drink alcohol, and he was best known for his skill with the new anaesthetic, chloroform. He had given it to Queen Victoria the year before, when she gave birth to Prince Leopold. He had also spent years thinking about cholera. He noticed that the disease attacked the gut, not the lungs. If it were caught by breathing bad air, he reasoned, it ought to start in the chest. To him it looked more like something swallowed, and the most likely thing people swallowed together was water. In 1849 he had published this idea, and few people had taken it seriously.

Soho gave him the chance to test it. Snow lived nearby, and as the deaths mounted he went to the General Register Office and got the list of everyone who had died, with their addresses. Then he walked the streets and knocked on doors. He asked the families of the dead one question again and again: where did you get your water?

London at that time had no taps in most homes. People carried water from public pumps in the street, each drawing from a well beneath it. As Snow plotted the deaths, a pattern appeared. They clustered thickly around one pump, on Broad Street. The further a house stood from that pump, the fewer deaths it had. And when he found deaths that sat closer to a different pump, he would often learn that the family preferred the taste of the Broad Street water and walked the extra distance for it, or that the children went to school near Broad Street and drank there.

The exceptions told him as much as the cases did. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street held more than five hundred people, yet hardly any of them died. It had its own well. A brewery stood on Broad Street itself, and none of its seventy or so workers died either. The owner explained that the men were given an allowance of beer and rarely drank water at all. These were people breathing the same air as their dying neighbours. What set them apart was what they drank.

The most striking case came from miles away. A widow in Hampstead, in north London, died of cholera, although there was no outbreak where she lived. Snow learned that she had once lived in Broad Street and liked its water so much that she had a cart bring her a large bottle of it every day. Her niece, who visited her and drank from the same bottle, also died after going home to Islington. No bad air from Soho had travelled to Hampstead. The water had.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow went before the local parish officials who were responsible for the pump and presented his evidence. They were sceptical, but they agreed to act. The next day, the handle of the Broad Street pump was taken off, so nobody could draw water from it.

The outbreak was already easing by then, partly because so many people had left and partly because the disease had run through many of those most exposed. More than six hundred people died in all. Snow himself admitted that removing the handle did not end the epidemic on its own. What it did was show where the danger had been.

The final piece came from an unlikely ally. Henry Whitehead, a young local clergyman, set out to prove Snow wrong and ended up proving him right. Talking to his parishioners, Whitehead found what was probably the first case. A baby girl at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill just before the outbreak, and her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspit at the front of the house. When the cesspit was dug up, it turned out to be leaking into the well a few feet away.

Officials did not accept Snow's explanation at the time, and the pump handle was soon put back. Snow died in 1858, before his idea was widely believed. Only decades later did scientists confirm that cholera is caused by a tiny organism carried in contaminated water. Today the Broad Street episode is remembered as one of the founding stories of public health. Its lesson is that patient counting, mapping and asking ordinary people simple questions can find a killer before anyone can see it.
