On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, a startling jump from the deep freeze of the previous days. Just after noon, workers, children and horses went about their business near the harbor. Towering over them stood a steel tank, fifty feet high and ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, which was used in munitions and, with Prohibition just days from becoming law, in plenty of other things besides. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply. It was never properly tested. Locals knew it leaked, and the company's answer was to paint it brown so the drips would be harder to see. Children collected the seeping syrup in cups.

At about 12:30 p.m., the tank failed.

Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rapid popping of rivets shooting loose, followed by a deep rumble. Then the steel split open, and a wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high rushed into the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It was heavy, dense and cold, and nothing could outrun it.

The wave crushed buildings. It lifted a freight car off the elevated railway tracks, and it bent the steel supports of the structure like straw. It pinned horses and men alike. A firehouse was shoved off its foundation, trapping firefighters inside as floors collapsed around them. Some people were swept along, and others were buried where they stood. The molasses poured into every gap and cooled quickly in the winter air, so that those caught in it found themselves held fast, as if in setting glue.

Rescuers arrived within minutes: police, sailors from a nearby ship, Red Cross workers, and soldiers and Boy Scouts who waded in. They struggled in knee-deep syrup. Doctors found that the molasses clogged the lungs and made treatment difficult. Searching for the missing went on for days, and some bodies were so thoroughly coated that they were hard to recognize.

In the end, twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Among the dead were children and city workers who had been eating lunch nearby. The smell lingered for decades, and on hot summer days, residents claimed they could still catch it.

The aftermath was as important as the disaster. Purity Distilling blamed anarchists, claiming the tank had been bombed. But a lengthy civil trial, one of the first major class-action suits in Massachusetts, took years of testimony from experts and eyewitnesses. In 1925, an auditor ruled that the tank was structurally unsound. The company had to pay damages, around $600,000 in total, which was a large sum then but a modest one for the families involved.

The consequences reached well beyond Boston. The disaster contributed to stricter building codes and to the requirement that engineers sign off on large structures. It helped establish the idea that companies must be held responsible for the safety of what they build.

Today, a small plaque marks the spot in the North End. The event has become a curiosity, a punchline almost, which makes it easy to forget that it was a genuine catastrophe.
