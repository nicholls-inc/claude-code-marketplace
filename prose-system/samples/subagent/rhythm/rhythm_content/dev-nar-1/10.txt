In the last days of August 1854, cholera struck the crowded streets of Soho in London with a violence that even a city accustomed to the disease found shocking, and within roughly ten days around five hundred people living near the junction of Broad Street and Cambridge Street had died. Cholera in that period was a terror of a particular kind, because it killed with startling speed: a healthy person could be seized by violent vomiting and diarrhoea, lose so much fluid that the skin turned blue and shrivelled, and be dead within a day. Nobody knew what caused it, and the explanation most respected by doctors and officials was that it arose from miasma, a poisonous foulness in the air given off by rotting refuse, sewers and the damp ground, which was why the stench of Victorian London seemed to many people a plausible culprit.

John Snow, a physician living a short walk from the stricken streets, did not believe it. He was best known at the time as a pioneer of anaesthesia, a careful man who had worked out how to give ether and chloroform in measured doses, and he had approached cholera with the same insistence on evidence. Six years earlier, during an earlier epidemic, he had published an argument that the disease was caused by something swallowed rather than breathed, a poison that multiplied in the gut and passed out in the victim's waste, and that it spread when this waste found its way into drinking water. Because cholera attacked the bowels and not the lungs, and because it tended to follow the courses of rivers and the habits of shared water supplies, this seemed to him far more natural than a vapour drifting through the air, yet few of his colleagues were persuaded, since he could not show them the poison itself.

What he could do was count. Snow went door to door through the affected streets, and he also obtained the register of deaths from the local office, and he began marking each death on a map of the neighbourhood, one small bar at the address where it had occurred. The bars piled up thickly around the public water pump on Broad Street and thinned with distance, and when he asked the families what they had drunk, a large majority of them said they had fetched water from that pump, whose supply was prized for tasting cleaner and fresher than the water from other wells. This alone proved little, since a cluster of deaths around a landmark might have a dozen explanations, and so Snow turned his attention to the exceptions, which is where his argument became difficult to dismiss.

The workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed more than five hundred poor inmates, crowded together in exactly the conditions where cholera should have flourished, yet only five of them died, and Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well and never needed the Broad Street pump. The same was true of the workers at the Lion Brewery on Broad Street, none of whom seemed to have fallen ill; they were given a daily allowance of beer and had a well of their own, so they had little reason to drink from the street. Meanwhile, far away in Hampstead, a widow and her niece both died of cholera after the widow, who had once lived in Soho and preferred the taste of the Broad Street water, had a bottle of it carried to her house each day, and no one else in her neighbourhood was ill.

On the evening of 7 September Snow laid his findings before the Board of Guardians of the parish of St James, and the next day, in the face of considerable scepticism, they ordered the handle removed from the pump so that no one could draw water from it. The epidemic was already declining by then, probably because so many residents had died or fled, which means the removal of the handle cannot be said to have ended it, although the gesture has since become the most celebrated image in the history of public health. Snow's work did not end there either. The local clergyman Henry Whitehead, who had begun as a sceptic and knew the neighbourhood and its people far better than any outside physician, continued the inquiry and eventually traced the outbreak to an infant at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill with cholera in the days before the epidemic, whose mother had washed the soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspool that, as an examination of the well later showed, lay only a few feet from the pump's source and was leaking into it.

An official inquiry nonetheless concluded that the evidence did not warrant abandoning the miasma theory, and the authorities soon allowed the pump to be used again. Snow kept refining his case, comparing the death rates among customers of two water companies that supplied the same streets of south London, one of which drew its water from a stretch of the Thames far above the city's sewage, and finding that the households supplied from the filthier source died at many times the rate of the others. He died in 1858, at only forty-five, still without having convinced most of his profession, and it took the discovery of the cholera bacterium by Robert Koch in the 1880s, along with the sanitary reforms that were already reshaping London, before his reasoning was widely accepted as correct.
