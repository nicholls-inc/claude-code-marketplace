Apollo 13 left Kennedy Space Center on 11 April 1970 at 2.13 in the afternoon, Florida time. It was the third mission planned to land men on the Moon. The commander was James Lovell, who had already flown to the Moon on Apollo 8, and the lunar module pilot was Fred Haise. The command module pilot, John Swigert, joined the crew only three days before launch. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was judged at risk of falling ill during the flight. The plan was to land in the Fra Mauro highlands, a hilly region that scientists wanted to sample.

The launch had one fault. The centre engine of the second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the four remaining engines burned longer to make up the speed. The crew reached Earth orbit as planned and set off for the Moon. They named their command and service module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius. For two days the flight was routine. The crew made a television broadcast on the evening of 12 April, though the networks did not carry it.

On the evening of 13 April, about 56 hours into the flight and some 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked Swigert to switch on the fans in the cryogenic tanks. The fans stirred the liquid oxygen and hydrogen so that the gauges would read correctly. Swigert threw the switch for oxygen tank 2. A few seconds later the crew heard a bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert reported the problem, and Lovell repeated it with the words that became well known: "Houston, we've had a problem."

The tank had ruptured. The cause was found later and went back several years. In 1965 the tank had been dropped about two inches while being moved at the factory, which probably damaged an internal fill tube. During ground testing before launch, the tank would not empty normally, and technicians boiled off the oxygen with heaters on a 65 volt supply. The thermostat switches were built for 28 volts and welded shut. That went unnoticed, and the heaters ran for hours, damaging the insulation on the wiring inside the tank. When Swigert switched on the fan, a spark ignited the damaged insulation. The pressure rose until the tank failed and blew a panel off the side of the service module.

Within minutes the crew could see oxygen venting into space from the second tank, and the pressure in the first was falling. The service module supplied the command module with oxygen, water and most of its electricity through fuel cells, and the fuel cells were failing. Odyssey had a battery that was meant for re-entry only. Controllers in Houston concluded that the Moon landing was impossible and that the aim was now to bring the men home. The lunar module was undamaged and had its own power, oxygen and water. It was built to support two men for about two days, including a landing on the Moon. The crew moved into it, shut down most of the command module, and used Aquarius as a lifeboat for three men for almost four days.

The trajectory was the next problem. The spacecraft was not on a path that would return it to Earth without a burn. Using the lunar module's descent engine, which had been meant for landing, the crew made a burn about five hours after the explosion to put the ship on a path that would swing around the Moon and back. After that they passed behind the Moon, and at their closest they were about 158 miles above the far side. At the point of greatest distance from Earth they were about 248,000 miles away, farther than any humans before or since. About two hours after passing the Moon they fired the descent engine again, for over four minutes, to speed the return and shorten it by roughly ten hours. The engineers did this to bring the splashdown into the Pacific, where the recovery ships were.

The cabin conditions then became the main difficulty. With the electrical systems off, the temperature inside fell to about 3 degrees Celsius, and water condensed on the walls. The crew had little to drink. Each man received around six ounces of water a day, and Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. Lovell, Swigert and Haise all lost weight, Haise about 14 pounds.

The most urgent problem was carbon dioxide. Lithium hydroxide canisters remove it from the air. The lunar module had enough for two men for two days, and the command module's canisters were a different shape and did not fit the lunar module's system. Engineers on the ground worked out a fitting made only from items on board. It used a plastic bag, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, hoses from a spacesuit and duct tape. They read the procedure to the crew over the radio, and the crew built it. The carbon dioxide level, which had risen towards a dangerous figure, came back down.

Meanwhile Mattingly and a team of engineers worked in a simulator on the sequence for restarting Odyssey, which had never been designed to be powered up after being shut down for so long. They had to do it with a tight limit on the electricity available. The procedure that came out of this was read to the crew on the last day.

The ship also needed course corrections. Without its normal guidance, the crew could not rely on the computer in the cold lunar module, and one correction was made by hand. Lovell lined the spacecraft up by keeping the day and night line of the Earth in a window, and they fired the engine for about 14 seconds. The ship was then on the correct path to the re-entry corridor, which is only a few degrees wide.

Before re-entry the crew separated from the service module. As it fell away they saw that a whole panel was missing and that equipment inside was exposed and damaged. They photographed it. Then they went into Odyssey, powered it up, and released Aquarius, which burned up over the Pacific. Re-entry radio blackout lasted for about a minute and a half longer than expected, since the path was slightly shallow, and Houston had no contact during that time. The silence ended when Swigert's voice came through. The parachutes opened, and the capsule came down in the South Pacific near Samoa at about 1.07 in the afternoon, Houston time, on 17 April. The flight had lasted 142 hours and 54 minutes. The recovery ship USS Iwo Jima picked up the crew within an hour.

A review board led by Edgar Cortright examined the accident. It identified the damaged tank, the testing procedure and the thermostat design as the causes, and NASA changed the oxygen tanks, adding a third and removing the fans. Apollo 14 flew in January 1971 and landed at Fra Mauro. Lovell never flew again, and Apollo 13 was his fourth space flight. Of the NASA missions, it is usually counted a failure in its aim and a success in how the crew were brought home, which is a judgement more than a fact, but the three men were alive and well at the end of it.
