**Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years, someone declares that handwriting is finished. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, so teachers should stop spending precious hours on loops and ligatures and hand the time to something useful. It is a tidy argument, and it is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it well.

Begin with what the case against actually says. It makes three claims. Typing is the skill children will use as adults. Cursive is a historical relic, like the abacus or the quill. And the curriculum is overcrowded, so something has to give. Each claim has a grain of truth. None survives a closer look.

Take the first. Yes, most adults type far more than they write by hand. But the claim confuses what people do with what helps them learn. Handwriting is not only a way of getting words onto a page. It is a way of building the mind that produces the words. Studies of children who learn letters by drawing them, as opposed to tapping or tracing them on a screen, repeatedly find stronger letter recognition and, later, stronger reading. Brain imaging work from Norway and the United States has shown that handwriting activates wider networks of neural activity than typing does, including areas tied to memory and learning. The findings are not flawless, and researchers argue over how far they stretch. But the direction is consistent. The hand teaches the eye and the eye teaches the hand.

Now notice what this evidence does and does not prove. It supports teaching handwriting. It does not, on its own, prove that the handwriting should be joined. A sceptic can fairly say that print-style letters would deliver the same benefits. So why insist on the join?

Because of speed and flow, and because of what flow does to thinking. A child who prints must lift the pen after every letter. Each lift is a tiny stop, a restart, a fresh decision. Joined writing turns a word into a single movement. The pen travels, and the word arrives as a unit rather than a pile of parts. That matters most for spelling. Children who write words as continuous shapes build a motor memory of the whole sequence, and that memory backs up the visual one. Teachers of children with dyslexia have argued this for decades, and many specialist programmes rely on cursive for exactly this reason. The movement carries the order of the letters in a way that print, with its many separate strokes, does not.

Speed is the second gain. By the age of eleven or twelve, a child who has mastered a fluent joined hand can write faster than one who prints, and can keep it up for longer without the hand cramping. This is not a trivial matter. Secondary school is a world of timed, handwritten examinations. In England and in many other countries, a student must produce several thousand words in a few hours, by hand, under pressure, with the result deciding what comes next in life. A slow, laboured script costs marks. So does an illegible one. Examiners cannot reward what they cannot read. We may wish that exams were all typed, but they are not, and they will not be for a long time. To send a child into that room without a fluent handwriting style is to hand them a handicap and call it modernity.

The second claim, that cursive is a relic, deserves a fair hearing, because there is a version of it that is true. Some of the old copperplate styles were ornate, slow to learn and hard to read. Nobody should teach a child to write like a Victorian clerk. But the choice is not between ornate cursive and nothing. Modern schemes, such as the simple, slanted, lightly joined styles used in many British and European classrooms, are plain, quick and legible. They are closer to what adults actually write than the round, disconnected letters children learn first. The aim is not to preserve a tradition. The aim is to give every child a style they can sustain, speed up and make their own.

There is also a quieter argument about access. Cursive is part of how we read our own past. Wills, diaries, letters, ship logs, census returns, the marginal notes in a grandmother's recipe book: much of the record of ordinary lives sits in handwriting. A generation that cannot read joined script is a generation locked out of its own archives. That sounds like a minor point until you meet a young person who cannot decipher a birthday card from an elderly relative. It is a small loss, but it is real, and it grows with each cohort we fail to teach.

Then comes the matter of crowded timetables. Here the sceptics have a point. Primary teachers are stretched. There is more to cover than there are hours to cover it, and every new demand squeezes something else. Yet handwriting is not a luxury that sits beside the core subjects. It is the vehicle for them. Every piece of writing a child produces, in English, history, science or anything else, passes through the hand. A child who writes slowly and painfully has less attention left over for what they are trying to say. Cognitive scientists call this the cost of low-level skills. When the mechanics of writing are automatic, working memory is free for ideas. When they are not, ideas wait. Time spent making handwriting automatic is therefore not time stolen from composition. It is time invested in it.

Fifteen minutes a day is enough. That is the surprising thing. Short, regular, well-structured practice, with attention to posture, grip and the shape of the strokes, produces fluent writers by the end of the primary years. The cost is small and the return is long.

What about the objection that some children simply struggle? Dyspraxia, motor delay and other difficulties make handwriting hard for a minority, and a rigid insistence on neat joined script can wound them. This is a serious concern, and it should shape how the subject is taught. It argues for patience, for early support, and for sensible adjustments, including keyboards for children who truly need them. It does not argue for dropping the subject for everyone. We do not abolish swimming lessons because some children find swimming difficult. We teach it, and we help those who need more.

A further objection is that the evidence for cursive over print is thinner than its defenders pretend. This is fair. The research on joined writing specifically is smaller and messier than the research on handwriting in general, and honest advocates should say so. But absence of proof is not proof of absence, and the practical case does not rest on a single study. It rests on the convergence of several modest pieces of evidence: fluency, spelling support, speed under exam conditions, legibility, and access to the written record. Taken together they point one way.

There is, finally, something harder to measure. A joined hand is personal. By the age of fourteen, a child's writing is recognisably theirs, as distinctive as a voice. Signatures, notes, cards, lists on the fridge door: these small human exchanges carry a trace of the person who made them. A keyboard flattens that. Every typed message looks the same as every other. I do not claim that this settles anything. Sentiment is not an argument. But a school that sends children into the world able to leave their own mark, in their own hand, has given them something that no font can supply.

So what should schools do? Teach print first, in the early years, when children are learning letter shapes and need clear, distinct forms. Introduce joins gradually from about the age of seven, beginning with the simplest and most common. Keep the style plain and avoid decorative loops. Practise little and often. Move children toward a fluent, personal hand by the end of primary school. Support those who struggle, and permit alternatives where needed. And teach typing alongside all of this, not instead of it. The choice between pen and keyboard is a false one. Adults use both, and children should be able to as well.

The strongest form of the opposing view says that schools must prepare children for the future, and the future is digital. I agree about the future. I disagree about what it takes to prepare for it. A child who can write fluently by hand has not been held back from technology. They have built a foundation of coordination, spelling memory and attention that makes every later skill easier to learn, typing included. Handwriting and keyboarding are not rivals. They are partners, and the older one has the longer track record.

So let the keyboards come. Let every child learn to touch-type, and learn it well. But do not take the pen out of their hands before they have learned what it can do. Teach the join. It takes a few minutes a day, it pays back for decades, and it gives a child one more way of thinking, one more way of remembering, and one more way of being unmistakably themselves.
