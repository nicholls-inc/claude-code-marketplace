Imagine you want to send a friend a secret message, but the only way to reach them is by shouting across a crowded room, where everyone can hear everything you say. How could the two of you possibly end up sharing a secret that nobody else knows? It sounds impossible, and for thousands of years it was believed to be, since anyone who wanted to keep a message private had to meet their partner beforehand and agree on a code, or a key, in person. Then, in the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around the problem. The idea is called public-key cryptography, and it rests on a surprisingly simple observation: some calculations are easy to do in one direction and extremely hard to undo.

Start with an everyday picture of that idea. Suppose you mix a can of yellow paint with a can of blue paint and get green; mixing is easy, but if I hand you a pot of green paint and ask you to separate it back into the exact yellow and blue that went in, you cannot, because the mixing cannot be reversed. Now picture two people, Alice and Bob, who want to share a secret colour while a spy, Eve, watches everything they pass between them. First they publicly agree on a starting colour, say yellow, which Eve sees, and that is fine. Alice then secretly picks her own private colour, say red, and mixes it into the yellow, while Bob secretly picks his own, say blue, and mixes it into the same yellow. They swap the mixed pots in full view, and Alice adds her private red to Bob's pot while Bob adds his private blue to Alice's pot. Both pots now contain yellow, red and blue, in whatever order, so both are the same final colour. Eve has seen the starting yellow and the two mixed pots, but she never held either private colour, and to reach the final shade she would need to un-mix a pot, which she cannot do. Alice and Bob now share a colour that only they can make.

Real cryptography does exactly this, but with numbers instead of paint, and the role of the paint that cannot be unmixed is played by a kind of arithmetic called clock arithmetic. You already know it: if it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, it is three o'clock, not fifteen, because after twelve the clock starts again from the beginning. Mathematicians call this working modulo twelve, and they can do the same with any number, not just twelve. Working modulo seven, for example, you take any ordinary answer and keep only the remainder after dividing by seven, so eight becomes one, and fifteen also becomes one, which is just the clock trick with a different-sized clock.

Now consider raising a number to a power, which means multiplying it by itself a certain number of times, so that three to the power of four is three times three times three times three, which is eighty-one, and in clock arithmetic you would then take the remainder. Here is the crucial fact. If I tell you the starting number, the size of the clock, and the power, you can work out the answer quickly, even when the numbers are huge; but if I tell you only the starting number, the size of the clock and the answer, and ask which power I used, you are stuck. With small numbers you can simply try every possibility, but with numbers hundreds of digits long, there are more possibilities than there are atoms in the universe, and no known shortcut. The forward direction is the mixing of paint, and the backward direction is the un-mixing.

Here is how Alice and Bob use it. Out loud, in front of Eve, they agree on two public numbers, a clock size and a starting number, and we will use small ones so that you can follow along: a clock of size seventeen and a starting number of three. Alice secretly picks a private number, say six, and tells no one, then works out three to the power of six on the seventeen-hour clock. Three to the sixth is seven hundred and twenty-nine, and dividing that by seventeen leaves a remainder of fifteen, so Alice announces fifteen. Bob secretly picks his own private number, say five; three to the fifth is two hundred and forty-three, which leaves a remainder of five on the same clock, so Bob announces five. Everyone in the room now knows seventeen, three, fifteen and five, but nobody knows six or five-as-a-private-choice, because only the results were spoken.

Now comes the clever step. Alice takes the number Bob announced, which is five, and raises it to the power of her own private number, six; five to the sixth is fifteen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, and on the seventeen clock that leaves a remainder of fifteen... wait, let us check it properly: the remainder works out to eight. Bob takes the number Alice announced, fifteen, and raises it to his private power, five, and fifteen to the fifth, taken on the seventeen clock, also comes out to eight. They have both arrived at eight, and that is no accident. Alice effectively calculated three raised to the power of five times six, and Bob calculated three raised to the power of six times five, and because multiplication does not care about order, six times five and five times six are the same thirty. Both of them have really computed three to the power of thirty, but each reached it by a route that only they could follow, using a private number that never travelled across the room.

Eve, listening, has the clock size, the starting number, and both announcements, but to find the secret she would need either Alice's six or Bob's five, which means working backwards from an announcement to the power that produced it. With our tiny numbers she could do that by trial and error in seconds, which is why real systems use enormous numbers, with hundreds of digits, so that the forward calculation is still fast for a computer, taking a fraction of a second, while the backward search would take longer than the age of the universe. The security does not come from hiding the method, which is published openly and studied by experts the world over, but from the sheer difficulty of un-mixing.

Once Alice and Bob share their secret number, they can use it as the key for an ordinary scrambling method, and from then on they can exchange messages that look like gibberish to anyone else. This is what happens, invisibly, every time your phone connects to a bank or a shopping website: two strangers, a machine and a person, agree on a secret in public, using nothing but numbers that are easy to combine and hopelessly hard to pull apart.

A related family of methods uses the same one-way principle to let someone publish a "public key" that anyone can use to lock a message, while only the owner of a matching "private key" can unlock it, much like a padlock that you hand out freely: anyone can snap it shut, but only you hold the key that opens it. The shared-colour story above is the simpler cousin, and it shows the heart of the whole subject. Two people who have never met can agree on a secret in front of everyone, because the difficulty of reversing a calculation does the job that a private meeting used to do.
