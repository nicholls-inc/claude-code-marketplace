On a mild January afternoon in 1919, Boston's North End was busy with ordinary life. It was January 15, a few minutes past noon, and the temperature had climbed unexpectedly to about forty degrees after days of bitter cold. Workers ate lunch, children played near the waterfront, and horses and trucks moved along Commercial Street beneath the elevated railway. Looming over it all was a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which the company planned to turn into industrial alcohol, a trade that was growing fast as Prohibition approached.

The tank had never been trusted by the neighborhood. Built hastily in 1915, it leaked so badly that residents collected the sticky syrup that ran down its sides. Rather than repair it properly, the company reportedly painted it brown to hide the stains. It was never fully tested, and engineers later found the steel too thin and the rivets poorly set. The tank also groaned and shuddered each time it was filled. Only the day before, a fresh shipment had been pumped in, and the sudden warmth likely made the contents expand and ferment.

Then, with a sound witnesses compared to a machine gun, the rivets began to fail. The tank ruptured and a wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged out at around thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a force that tore buildings from their foundations. It flattened the nearby firehouse, knocked a freight train's cars off the elevated tracks, and crushed wagons and warehouses beneath it. People were lifted and hurled through the air. Others were pinned in a thick brown current that grew heavier by the second.

The cold made rescue brutally hard. As the molasses cooled, it thickened into something like wet cement, trapping victims where they lay. Sailors from a nearby ship, police, Red Cross workers, and soldiers dug through the mess for survivors, often wading waist-deep. Hospitals filled with the injured. In the end, twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty were hurt. Among the dead were children and a number of city workers who had been eating lunch by the harbor. Horses also perished, and rescuers had a hard time reaching them. The cleanup took weeks, with crews using salt water from fire boats and spreading sand to cover the streets. The harbor ran brown until summer, and locals claimed that on hot days the North End smelled of molasses for decades.

The lawsuit that followed was among the first big class action cases in Massachusetts. The company argued that anarchists had blown up the tank, a claim tied to the fear of radicals at that time. After years of testimony, the court's auditor found that the tank had been unsafe from the start, and the company paid substantial damages to the families. The case helped push changes in American building law, including requirements that engineers sign off on major structures. What began as a freak accident became a lesson in the cost of cutting corners.
