Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). The charger acts as a controlled source, and the cell's own chemistry dictates how much it can accept at each point.

**Stage 1: constant current.** The charger forces a fixed current into the cell, typically between 0.5C and 1C, where 1C is the current that would fill the nominal capacity in one hour. Lithium ions leave the cathode (for example, a layered metal oxide), cross the electrolyte, and insert into the graphite anode, a process called intercalation. As charge accumulates, the cell's open-circuit voltage rises from roughly 3.0 V toward 4.2 V. The terminal voltage is higher than this open-circuit value because current flowing through internal resistance adds an overpotential, much like a voltage drop across a resistor in series with a battery. The charger holds the current steady and lets the terminal voltage climb until it reaches the upper limit, commonly 4.2 V for standard cells.

**Stage 2: constant voltage.** The upper limit exists for safety and longevity. Above it, the electrolyte oxidizes at the cathode, and lithium can plate as metal on the anode, which degrades capacity and can create short-circuit hazards. So the charger switches to holding the terminal voltage fixed at the limit. Now the current becomes the dependent variable and decays on its own. Charging is typically terminated when current falls to about 0.05C to 0.1C.

**Why it slows.** The decay has three coupled causes. First, the voltage budget: terminal voltage equals open-circuit voltage plus current times resistance. As the cell fills, the open-circuit voltage approaches the fixed limit, leaving less headroom, so the current that can flow while staying at the limit shrinks. Second, concentration gradients: ions must diffuse into the graphite particles, and diffusion in solids is slow. Near the end the particle surfaces are already saturated, so the driving gradient toward the particle interior flattens and the flux falls. Third, the anode's own potential: at high state of charge the graphite potential sits close to that of metallic lithium, so pushing harder risks plating, which makes the voltage cap the binding constraint.

An engineer might picture an RC circuit charging toward a rail. The current decays roughly exponentially with a time constant set by diffusion and resistance, not by the charger's capability. A more powerful charger cannot help in this stage.

**Practical consequences.** The CC stage delivers about 70 to 80 percent of the capacity in well under an hour, while the CV tail takes comparable time for the remaining fraction. Fast-charging systems therefore taper current stepwise based on temperature and state of charge, and many devices stop at 80 percent to save time and reduce wear. Cold temperatures worsen all three effects by raising resistance and slowing diffusion, which is why charge limits tighten in the cold.
