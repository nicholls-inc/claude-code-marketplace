The Earth does not spin at a perfectly steady rate, and a leap second is the small patch that has kept the world's clocks in step with it. Since 1972 there have been twenty-seven of them, each a single extra second added to the end of a June or December day so that clocks read 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. They exist because two kinds of time have drifted apart. Astronomical time is defined by the planet's rotation, which is slowed unpredictably by tides, shifting oceans and the churning of the molten core, so that a day is on average a little longer than it used to be. Atomic time, kept by clocks that count the vibrations of caesium atoms, is far steadier than the planet, and the official civil time that governs phones, banks and airports, known as Coordinated Universal Time, was designed to follow the atoms while staying within a second of the Earth's turning. Whenever the gap threatened to exceed that limit, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service, a body based in Paris and Frankfurt, announced that another second would be inserted.

For ordinary people this was invisible, but for the computer systems that run modern life it has been a recurring headache. Software generally assumes that every minute has sixty seconds and that time only moves forward, and a minute with sixty-one seconds can make machines repeat a timestamp, disagree with one another or simply crash. Outages affecting well-known websites and data centres have been traced to leap seconds, and large technology companies such as Google and Meta have taken to "smearing" the extra second across many hours to avoid a sudden jump, an approach that works but means different systems may briefly disagree about what time it is. Financial markets, satellite navigation, power grids and telecommunications networks, which depend on timestamps accurate to millionths of a second, have all found the practice a growing burden.

The matter might have continued to be managed by workarounds had it not been for a surprising change in the planet itself: in recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than it did a few decades ago, which raises the prospect of a first negative leap second, in which a second would be removed, something that software has never been properly tested to handle. In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of units, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035, and to allow the gap between atomic and astronomical time to grow far beyond one second, probably to about a minute, before any correction is considered, perhaps not for a century or more. The decision is as much a recognition of what clocks are for as a technical fix, since almost no one needs the time on a phone to match the exact angle of the sun, while a great many systems need time that ticks smoothly and without surprises. Astronomers who track the sky will have to apply their own corrections, and the tiny discrepancy will be left to accumulate in the background.
