Apollo 13: The Flight That Came Home

On April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three American astronauts toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, a veteran of two earlier flights, commanded the mission. Fred Haise would pilot the lunar module, and Jack Swigert, who had little spaceflight experience, was the command module pilot. Swigert was a last-minute replacement. Days before launch, the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles, and doctors judged that he might fall ill in flight. Swigert, the backup, went instead.

The goal was a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands, the third lunar landing attempt of the Apollo program. For the first two days the flight went smoothly. On the evening of April 13, about 56 hours after launch and roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew was preparing a short television broadcast when Mission Control asked them to stir the oxygen tanks in the service module. This was a routine procedure that kept the supercold oxygen evenly mixed. Seconds later there was a loud bang, and the spacecraft shuddered.

Swigert radioed what became one of the most famous understatements in spaceflight: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell then reported a drop in electrical bus voltage. Looking out the window, he saw gas streaming into space from the service module. Oxygen tank No. 2 had ruptured, and the blast damaged plumbing that fed tank No. 1, which was also losing oxygen. Investigators later traced the failure to wiring inside the tank. Its insulation had been damaged during ground testing, when heaters were run at a higher voltage than the wiring was designed for. When the stirring fan switched on in flight, the damaged wiring sparked, the insulation caught fire, and the tank burst.

The loss was serious because the service module held three fuel cells that generated electricity from hydrogen and oxygen. They also produced drinking water as a byproduct. With the oxygen gone, the fuel cells failed, and the command module Odyssey was left with almost no power. A lunar landing was no longer possible. The new task was to bring three men home alive.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers worked quickly. The crew moved into the lunar module, Aquarius, which had its own oxygen, power, and life support. Aquarius had been built to support two men on the lunar surface for about two days. Now it had to keep three men alive for roughly four days, the time it would take to return to Earth.

Mission planners chose a free-return trajectory. Instead of slowing into lunar orbit, the spacecraft would loop around the far side of the Moon and let its gravity sling it back toward Earth. On April 14, a short burn of Aquarius's engine corrected the path. On April 15 the spacecraft swung around the Moon about 150 miles above its surface. In the process, the crew travelled farther from Earth than any humans had before, a distance that remains the record.

Life in Aquarius was grim. The lunar module had been designed for two men, and with three breathing inside, carbon dioxide began to build up to dangerous levels. The spacecraft used canisters of lithium hydroxide to scrub carbon dioxide from the air. Aquarius's canisters were round and fit its own system, but the square canisters from Odyssey would not fit. On the ground, a team of engineers nicknamed the "tiger team" set out to solve the problem with only what was on board. They designed an adapter from plastic bags, cardboard from the flight plan checklists, duct tape, and a spare sock. The crew assembled it under detailed instructions from Houston while the carbon dioxide level climbed. It worked.

Conditions were miserable in other ways too. To conserve power, the crew shut down most systems, and the temperature inside fell close to freezing. Water was rationed, so the astronauts drank very little, saving what they had for the return. Condensation dripped from the walls, and the men were often cold and short of sleep.

The return required precision. The spacecraft had to re-enter Earth's atmosphere at a narrow angle. Too steep, and the crew would burn up or be crushed by deceleration. Too shallow, and the capsule would skip off the atmosphere and into space. Ground controllers calculated a course correction, and the crew carried it out. Engineers also worried that Odyssey's batteries, which had been kept in reserve, might not hold enough charge for the final power-up, so every step had to be carefully managed.

On April 17, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module and photographed the gaping wound in its side. Then they moved back into Odyssey, which had been cold and dark for days, and brought its systems back to life. The re-entry was tense, with communications briefly cut off by the heat of re-entry, as expected, until the capsule's parachutes opened. Apollo 13 splashed down in the South Pacific, about four miles from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, roughly 142 hours after launch. The three men were safe.

The crew was greeted as heroes, and the mission became one of NASA's most studied episodes. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tank system and tightened procedures for ground testing, and the Apollo program continued. Apollo 14 landed in the Fra Mauro highlands in February 1971, completing the mission that Apollo 13 had been unable to finish.

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon, but it is remembered as a triumph of improvisation and teamwork. Controllers and astronauts solved problems that no checklist had anticipated, and they did it under extreme pressure. The phrase "Failure is not an option," often linked to Mission Control, became famous through Gene Kranz's memoir and the 1995 film "Apollo 13." It was not a line from the radio transcripts. What the flight really showed was something quieter and harder to dramatize: a crew and a ground team who kept working the problem until the three men were home.
