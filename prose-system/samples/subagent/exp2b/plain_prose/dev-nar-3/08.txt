On a mild winter day in Boston, January 15, 1919, a storage tank at the Purity Distilling Company, on Commercial Street in the crowded North End, burst open. The tank stood about fifty feet tall and was nearly ninety feet across. It held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, a thick syrup that the company turned into industrial alcohol, a trade that was in a hurry as Prohibition approached and munitions makers wanted the product.

It was a little after half past noon. Workers were eating lunch, children were playing near the waterfront, and the nearby streets were busy with horses, wagons and trains. Witnesses later described a low rumbling and a sound like machine-gun fire, which was probably the rivets shooting out of the steel plates. Then the tank gave way.

A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, swept out across the neighborhood. Accounts put its speed at around thirty-five miles an hour. It was heavy and sticky, and nothing could outrun it for long. It knocked buildings off their foundations and tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports. A firehouse was shoved apart, and a truck was carried into the harbor. Horses and people were caught and held fast, dragged along and then trapped as the syrup began to cool in the winter air.

Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Rescuers waded in as quickly as they could, but they found the work slow and miserable. The molasses clung to everything, and some victims were so thoroughly coated that they were hard to identify. Soldiers and sailors from nearby posts joined police and Red Cross workers in the search. It took days to recover everyone, and cleanup went on for weeks, with salt water from fireboats hosed over the streets and sand strewn over the worst of it. Locals said the harbor ran brown until summer, and for decades people claimed that on hot days the North End still smelled of sweetness.

The question of blame went to court. The company's parent, United States Industrial Alcohol, insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, a claim that fit the fears of the day. But the lawsuit that followed was one of the first big class actions in Massachusetts, and its auditor, Hugh Ogden, spent years hearing testimony from hundreds of witnesses. The evidence told another story. The tank had been built hastily, with thin walls and poor testing. It had leaked from the start, and the company had simply painted it brown to hide the seepage. An engineer's calculations were never checked, and no one with the right training had signed off.

In 1925 the court found the company responsible, and it paid out settlements that families received in modest sums. The disaster's legacy was larger than the payments. It helped push Massachusetts and other states toward requiring that engineers and architects stamp plans for large structures, and it helped create a public expectation that companies, not victims, should prove their buildings are safe.
