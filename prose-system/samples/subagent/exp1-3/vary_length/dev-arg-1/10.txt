**Let the Lot Go: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet law shaping the ground beneath your feet. It says that every new restaurant, apartment building, and dentist's office must provide a fixed number of parking spaces, whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these minimum parking requirements. They are a subsidy for driving that nobody voted for, and they make housing, businesses, and streets worse.

Start with the origin. Most requirements date to the mid-twentieth century, when planners reached for a number to keep curbs clear. The numbers were rarely grounded in careful study. Many came from a handbook that averaged a few suburban sites, then hardened into code. A city might demand one space per 200 square feet of restaurant, or two per apartment, simply because the neighboring city did.

The result is a rule built on a guess and enforced as if it were physics.

Now consider what a parking space costs. A surface spot takes roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles and ramps. In a dense district, that is land that could hold a bedroom, a shop, or a tree. A spot in a structure can run tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spots cost more still. Developers do not absorb that bill. They pass it along, so tenants who own no car end up paying for spaces they never use. Researchers who have studied the effect find that requirements can raise the price of an apartment by a meaningful margin. In a housing crisis, that is a strange thing to mandate.

Supporters offer a fair objection. If a building provides too little parking, they say, drivers will crowd the neighborhood and circle the block. This worry is real, and I do not wave it away. But it confuses two different questions. Whether parking should exist is one question. Whether the government should dictate how much each building supplies is another. Repealing a minimum does not ban a single space. It lets the person who knows the site best, the developer, decide, in consultation with lenders who will not finance a building tenants cannot use.

And the developers do keep building parking. After Buffalo scrapped its requirements citywide in 2017, many projects still included it. They simply stopped including more than they needed. Where demand is high, the market supplies it. Where demand is low, the lot stays a lot of dirt, or becomes something better.

Spillover, meanwhile, has a better remedy than a building code. Price the curb. A city that charges a sensible rate for on-street spaces, and returns some of the revenue to the neighborhood for sidewalks and lighting, ends the free-for-all that makes parking feel scarce. Free things get overused. Charge for a scarce thing, and a space opens up in front of the shop you want to visit.

There is also a fairness argument that deserves more attention than it gets. Roughly one in ten American households owns no car at all, and the share is far higher in many dense city neighborhoods. Requirements force these households to subsidize their neighbors' vehicles through higher rents and higher prices at every store. It is a quiet transfer from people with less to people with more, and it falls hardest on the young, the old, and the poor.

Then there is the shape of the city itself. Parking minimums are why so many commercial streets look the same: a low box set back behind an ocean of asphalt. They make walking unpleasant and distances long, which in turn makes driving feel mandatory. The rule creates the very demand it claims to serve. Small businesses suffer most. A corner café in a century-old building often cannot reopen under a new owner, because the old structure could never meet today's parking count. The law freezes neighborhoods in place and forbids exactly the incremental change that made them lovable.

Environmental costs follow too. Cheap, abundant parking tells every resident that driving is the default, so people drive. More driving means more emissions, more congestion, and more road deaths. A city serious about its climate goals cannot keep a rule that guarantees a parking space for every trip.

What should a city do in practice? Repeal the minimums outright rather than trimming them. Piecemeal reductions leave the framework intact and keep planners arguing over numbers no one can defend. Let builders choose. Keep accessibility spaces, which serve a clear legal purpose. Manage the curb with prices and permits. And watch what happens, because cities from Minneapolis to Auckland have already run this experiment, and the sky has not fallen.

Nothing here requires hostility to cars. People will keep driving, and many should. The point is that a city should not force every project to build for the car-heavy past. A parking space is a private good with a real price. It belongs in the market, not in the code.

Take the mandate out of the rulebook. Let the lot go. The housing, the shops, and the sidewalks that fill the space will do more for a city than another empty stall ever could.
