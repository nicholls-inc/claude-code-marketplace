Scrap the Parking Mandate

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet rule shaping the ground beneath your feet. Before a developer can build an apartment block, a corner cafe or a clinic, the city usually requires a fixed number of parking spaces. Two per home. One for every 300 square feet of retail. One for every four restaurant seats. These minimum parking requirements look like technical housekeeping. They are in fact one of the most expensive and least examined policies in urban planning, and cities should remove them.

Begin with the origin of the numbers. Many were never carefully derived. Planners often copied figures from other towns or relied on a handbook that summarized parking demand at a handful of suburban sites, usually at peak times and with free parking on offer. The result was a rule built on the assumption that every visitor arrives alone in a car and expects to park at no charge. Cities then wrote that assumption into law and applied it to every neighborhood, whether it sat beside a subway stop or at the edge of a highway.

The cost of that assumption is large and it lands on people who never asked for it. A single surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you include the lanes needed to reach it. A structured space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one far more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, home prices and menu prices. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the garage. Researchers who have studied this, most famously the late Donald Shoup, have estimated that required parking can raise the cost of a new apartment by a substantial share. In a housing shortage, a rule that makes every unit pricier is hard to defend.

Parking mandates also quietly kill projects. On a small urban lot, the space required for the cars can leave no room for the building. A developer who might have put twelve apartments on a plot ends up with six, or abandons the idea. Historic main streets suffer most. Many of the charming old blocks people love could not legally be built today, because their shops and flats sit shoulder to shoulder with no off-street spaces at all. We have made our best urban forms illegal and then wondered why new construction feels so bland.

Then there is the land itself. Cities that demand abundant parking end up as seas of asphalt, with buildings pushed far apart and destinations too distant to walk between. That spreading-out makes driving more necessary, which seems to justify yet more parking, and the loop feeds itself. It also eats tax base. A paved lot generates a sliver of the property tax that a well-used building on the same ground would deliver, so cities that mandate parking often starve their own budgets.

The strongest objection is practical. If the mandate vanishes, will streets flood with cars and will neighbors lose their spots? This worry deserves a direct answer. Removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it wherever customers want it, because a building with no parking in a car-dependent area will struggle to find tenants. Lenders and retailers understand this very well. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision tied to real demand rather than a number picked decades ago. A grocery store in a suburb will provide ample spaces. A studio apartment block near a train station may provide few, and charge lower rent as a result.

Where the spillover is a genuine concern, there are better tools than a blanket mandate. Cities can price curb parking so that a space is usually available, and they can return the revenue to the neighborhood for sidewalks and trees. Residential permit programs can protect streets near busy districts. These approaches deal with the real problem, which is scarcity at the curb, rather than forcing every builder to guess at it in advance.

The evidence from places that have tried reform is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017 and found that developers continued to include parking where it made sense while new projects that had been stalled began to move. Minneapolis, Seattle and a growing list of other cities have followed, some by statute and some through state law. The sky has not fallen. Rents did not collapse and traffic did not choke, but projects that were once impossible became feasible.

Nothing here requires anyone to give up their car. It asks only that cities stop deciding on behalf of builders, tenants and shoppers how much parking they must buy. People who want to drive will still be able to, and the market will supply spaces where there is a market for them. People who would rather spend their money on rent, or who cannot afford a car at all, will no longer be taxed by the zoning code to subsidize someone else's.

A city that wants more homes, livelier streets and a healthier budget should start with the cheapest reform available. Delete the minimums. Let people build what the street actually needs.
