Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and who can only talk to each other across a crowded room where everyone can hear every word. They want to end up sharing a secret number, perhaps to use as the key for scrambling their later messages, and they want to do it without any private meeting beforehand. At first this sounds impossible, because anything either of them says out loud is also heard by the eavesdropper. Yet it can be done, and the trick, discovered in the 1970s, is one of the quiet foundations of modern life, since it is what protects your card details whenever you shop online.

The easiest way to see the idea is with paint rather than numbers. Suppose Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common starting colour, say yellow. Each of them privately chooses a secret colour of their own, which they never reveal. Alice mixes her secret colour into a bucket of the yellow and sends the resulting mixture across the room to Bob, while Bob does the same and sends his mixture to Alice. Everyone can see these two mixed buckets, but nobody can easily un-mix paint to find out which secret colour went in. Alice now takes Bob's mixture and adds her own secret colour to it, and Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his secret colour. Both end up with exactly the same final shade, because each bucket now contains yellow plus Alice's colour plus Bob's colour, and the order in which paints are added does not change the result. The eavesdropper holds only the plain yellow and the two half-mixed buckets, and cannot reach the final shade without one of the secret colours.

Paint is only a picture, so we need something in mathematics that behaves in the same way: easy to do in one direction, practically impossible to undo, and indifferent to the order of the steps. The ingredient that provides this is clock arithmetic. On a clock with twelve hours, if it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, it is three o'clock, because you wrap around after twelve. Mathematicians call this working modulo twelve, and you can do it with any size of clock. If we use a clock with twenty-three positions, then any number you calculate is replaced by the remainder you get after dividing by twenty-three. So thirty becomes seven, and fifty becomes four, because fifty is two lots of twenty-three plus four.

The one-way step in our scheme is raising a number to a power on such a clock. Raising five to the power six simply means multiplying five by itself six times, which gives fifteen thousand, six hundred and twenty-five, and on the twenty-three-position clock that wraps around to eight. Working forwards like this is quick and mechanical. Working backwards is the hard part. If I tell you that some number raised to a power landed on eight, and I do not tell you the power, you cannot read it off. The wrapping scrambles the answers so thoroughly that they jump around with no visible pattern, and the only general method is to try the powers one after another until you hit the right one. With a small clock that is trivial, but with a clock of enormous size it becomes hopeless even for the fastest computers.

Now let us run the real scheme with the small numbers. Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers, which are the clock size of twenty-three and a starting number of five. These play the role of the yellow paint, and anyone is welcome to know them. Alice secretly picks six and Bob secretly picks fifteen. Alice calculates five to the power six on the clock, which we just saw is eight, and she announces eight across the room. Bob calculates five to the power fifteen on the clock, which turns out to be nineteen, and he announces nineteen. These two announced numbers are the half-mixed buckets of paint.

Next comes the step that makes the two of them agree. Alice takes Bob's announced nineteen and raises it to her own secret power of six, which on the clock gives two. Bob takes Alice's announced eight and raises it to his secret power of fifteen, which also gives two. They have arrived at the same number without ever speaking it aloud. The reason is that both calculations amount to multiplying five by itself six times fifteen times, which is ninety times in all, and it does not matter whether you do the six first or the fifteen first. This is the same freedom of order that let the two paint mixtures end up as one colour.

Consider what the eavesdropper knows at this point. She heard the clock size of twenty-three, the starting number five, and the two announcements of eight and nineteen. To find the shared secret she would need either Alice's six or Bob's fifteen. To get one of those she must work backwards from the announcement, asking which power of five gives eight on this clock. With numbers this small she could simply try them all and succeed within seconds, which is why the example is only a demonstration. The security comes entirely from scaling up. In practice the clock has hundreds of digits, and the secret powers are similarly huge, so the forward calculation still takes a computer a fraction of a second while the backward search would take longer than the age of the universe.

It is worth being clear about what has and has not been achieved. The two people have agreed on a secret that was never transmitted, which is the surprising part, and which nobody listening can reconstruct. They can then use that shared number as the key for a fast ordinary scrambling method, so that everything they say afterwards is unreadable to the room. What this basic scheme does not do is prove who is on the other end. If a mischievous person stood between Alice and Bob, pretending to be Bob to Alice and pretending to be Alice to Bob, she could run two separate agreements and read everything in the middle. Real systems therefore add a second ingredient, in which each party can sign a message in a way that only the genuine owner could produce, so that Alice can be confident that the announcement really came from Bob.

The name public-key cryptography comes from this division into a public part and a private part. The announced numbers, such as eight and nineteen, are public and can be shouted across any room, while the secret powers, such as six and fifteen, stay private and never leave their owner's head. A closely related family of methods uses the same kind of lopsided arithmetic to let anyone lock a message with a freely published key that only the owner of a matching private key can open. The most famous of these relies on the difficulty of splitting a huge number back into the two large prime numbers that were multiplied together to make it.

What unites all of these methods is a single, simple observation about arithmetic, which is that some operations are easy to perform but fiendishly hard to reverse. Two strangers can lean on that imbalance to build trust out of nothing, each keeping a private ingredient, sharing only the scrambled result, and combining what they hear with what they hold to reach an identical answer. Everything the eavesdropper hears is true and public, and none of it is enough.
