On a mild January day in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with the noise of ordinary life. Workers hauled cargo along the waterfront, children played in the streets, and firemen relaxed at their station. Overlooking it all stood a huge steel tank on Commercial Street, owned by the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses was not meant for the breakfast table. It was bound for distilleries, where it would be turned into industrial alcohol and, with Prohibition about to take effect, into rum and munitions. The tank was only a few years old and had been built quickly. It leaked so badly that neighbors collected the sticky drips in cups. Rather than repair it, the company painted it brown so the stains would not show. Nobody had properly tested whether it could bear its load.

On January 15, 1919, the temperature had climbed above forty degrees after several bitterly cold days. Just after noon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a rumbling sound and a sharp noise like machine gun fire, which was probably the sound of rivets shooting loose. Then the tank burst open.

A wave of molasses roughly twenty-five feet high at its crest surged out across the neighborhood. It moved at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It tore a section of the elevated railway off its supports, crushed buildings, and knocked a firehouse from its foundation. Men inside were thrown into the wreckage. Horses, wagons and people were swept up in a brown flood that smothered everything it touched.

The cruelty of molasses lay in its nature. In the cold air it thickened quickly, trapping victims in a heavy slurry that was nearly impossible to swim through or escape. Rescuers waded in to find people stuck fast, and the sticky mass slowed every attempt to reach them. Sailors from a nearby ship, police, Red Cross workers and soldiers all joined the effort. Some people were pulled free alive. Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Many of the dead were crushed by debris or drowned in the syrup.

Cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed salt water and sand on the streets, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer. Residents claimed for decades that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled faintly sweet.

The aftermath proved just as significant as the disaster. The company blamed anarchists and claimed the tank had been bombed. Survivors and families sued, and the case became one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. After years of testimony, an auditor concluded that the tank had been poorly built and that the company was responsible. It paid out settlements to the victims' families.

The flood also changed how America built things. The tank's design had never been reviewed by a qualified engineer, and its plans carried no architect's signature. The scandal helped push states to require licensed professionals to approve and sign off on major construction. It was a hard lesson, paid for by the people of the North End, that a structure's safety cannot simply be assumed.
