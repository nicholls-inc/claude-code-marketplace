In England a child is expected to start joining letters in Year 2, when most are six or seven, and to write in a fluent joined hand by the end of primary school. The national curriculum has said so since 2014. Other countries have gone the other way. Finland removed compulsory cursive from its national core curriculum in 2016 and gave the time to typing. In the United States the Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out, and since then many states have passed laws to put it back; California, for one, now requires handwriting instruction in grades one to six. The question is a live one, and it is reasonable to ask whether a skill that most adults use for a shopping list and a birthday card is worth classroom hours. My view is that it is, and that primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, though with more modest aims than they sometimes set.

Joined-up handwriting, also called cursive or running writing, is a way of forming letters so that the pen stays on the paper from the start of a word to the end, or close to it. Children normally learn print letters first, in Reception and Year 1, and meet joins once their letter shapes are steady. Teaching takes a few short sessions a week, often ten or fifteen minutes each, and the aim is a legible and reasonably quick hand by the age of eleven. Over four years that comes to something like forty or fifty hours, which is a small share of a school career. The case against it has to show that this time could be better spent, and I do not think it can.

The strongest argument for handwriting in general comes from research on how children learn. A study by Virginia Berninger and colleagues, published in 2009, compared pupils in grades two, four and six writing with a pen and on a keyboard, and reported that the pen produced longer texts, written faster, with more ideas. In 2012 Karin James and Laura Engelhardt scanned the brains of children who could not yet read. Those who had practised writing letters by hand showed more activity in reading-related regions than those who had typed or traced them. In 2024 a Norwegian team led by Audrey van der Meer recorded brain activity in thirty-six university students and found wider patterns of connection when they wrote by hand than when they typed. None of this is conclusive. The samples are small, and the finding that note-taking by hand beats laptop note-taking, first published by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer in 2014, has been only partly replicated. Most of these studies also compare handwriting with typing, not joined writing with print. They show that forming letters by hand does something useful. They do not show that joining is the part that matters. That is the weakest point in my own side's case, so I will rest the argument on other grounds as well.

The second ground is fluency. Writing by hand is a motor task that competes with the thinking a child is trying to do. A seven-year-old who has to decide how to shape each letter has less attention left for spelling, for the next sentence, and for what the sentence is meant to say. Joining reduces the number of times the pen leaves the page, and it fixes the order and direction of the strokes, so the movements become habitual sooner. The evidence that a joined hand is faster than a print hand is modest, and some studies find little difference once both have been practised. My view is that the larger gain comes from having one settled way of forming every letter, and joining is a reasonable way to get it. Speed matters because so much assessed work is still handwritten. GCSE and A-level examinations in England are written by hand, and a candidate may write for several hours in a single day in the summer series. A pupil whose hand tires or turns illegible after thirty minutes has a problem that a keyboard in Year 3 does nothing to remove.

The third ground is reading. A great deal of the written world is in joined hand, and a child who has never been taught it cannot be expected to read it. That includes the comments teachers write in margins, a note left on a fridge, a card from a grandparent, and the papers of any family or town. Archivists in several countries have said that fewer young people can read handwritten historical documents. I take that as a reasonable worry and not as a measured fact. Even so, reading a joined hand is easier for a child who has made the shapes with a pen than for one who has only seen them, and a few weeks of practice at writing them is a cheap way to learn.

The fourth ground is cost and fairness. A pencil and a lined page cost almost nothing and work in every classroom. Devices, charging, software and typing lessons cost money, and the gap between homes that have them and homes that do not is real. Handwriting taught well at school is a skill that every child leaves with, whatever the household has. Some teachers also report that joined writing helps children who reverse letters such as b and d, because the entry and exit strokes make the shapes harder to confuse. This is claimed more often than it is demonstrated, and I treat it as a teachers' view and not as a finding. It is still worth noting that no one has produced evidence that joining makes such difficulties worse for most children.

The best objection is that time is limited and typing is the skill children will use most. Finland's decision rested on that reasoning, and no one has yet shown that the change helped or harmed its pupils. Most adults type far more than they write. If a school had to choose between teaching joins and teaching touch typing, there would be a fair argument for typing. But the choice is not forced. Joined handwriting takes a small number of short lessons across about four years. Many teachers prefer to begin touch typing at around eight or nine, when hands are larger and spelling is more secure, and the two can run side by side. The English curriculum already leaves room for this, since it sets out outcomes for handwriting and for computing without tying either to a fixed number of hours.

A second objection is that adults rarely write in the neat joined style taught at school. Most develop a personal mix of print and joins, and many write mainly in capitals or print. This is true, and it argues for a particular kind of teaching and not for none. A child needs a model, and a model is different from a mandate. The aim should be a clear, steady hand that the child then adapts. In practice that means teachers should not mark down a letter shape that is legible, should allow print for pupils with motor difficulties such as dyspraxia, and should not ask for joins before the letter forms are secure. A school that insists on identical looped letters from every nine-year-old has misunderstood the purpose, and it gives the opponents of handwriting their best examples.

There is also a point about what primary schools are for. Part of their job is to teach skills that are tedious to learn, that make later work easier, and that cannot be picked up later with the same ease. Times tables are one case. Handwriting is another. A child who reaches secondary school with a slow, cramped or illegible hand will carry the cost into every subject and every test, and the teachers there seldom have the time to repair it. Fixing it in Year 3 or Year 4 is far cheaper than fixing it at fourteen. I regard that as the practical heart of the matter. The benefits are small in any one week and large over a decade.

The position I would defend is therefore limited. Primary schools should keep teaching joined handwriting, beginning in Year 2 once print is secure, with short regular practice and a clear expectation of a legible, fluent hand by the age of eleven. They should treat it as a base skill, in the way they treat number facts. They should pair it with early keyboard teaching, and they should be flexible with the children for whom it is hard. What they should not do is drop it because it seems old. Age is not an argument. Handwriting is cheap, it is used in examinations, it gives access to a great deal of written material, and there is some evidence that it helps children learn to read and to compose. Against that stands a modest share of the timetable. I think the balance clearly favours keeping it.
