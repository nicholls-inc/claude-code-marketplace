Free the Curb: Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet rule shaping what gets built. It does not say how tall a building may be or what it may look like. It says how much car storage must come with it. These minimum parking requirements, written into zoning codes across the country, tell developers to provide a set number of spaces for every apartment, restaurant seat, or square foot of office. Cities should repeal them. Not tweak them, not lower them a little, but remove them from the rulebook entirely.

Start with the problem the rules claim to solve. Planners set minimums to make sure nobody circles the block hunting for a spot. That sounds reasonable until you ask where the numbers come from. Most were borrowed from manuals that surveyed a handful of suburban sites at peak demand, then treated the busiest hour as the standard for every hour. The result is a lot sized for the day after Thanksgiving that sits mostly empty the rest of the year. Anyone who has seen a half vacant lot ringing a pharmacy has seen the evidence.

Now consider what those empty spaces cost. A surface space takes roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles. A space in a garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and far more underground. Someone pays for that. Because the requirement bundles parking into every unit, the expense lands in the rent or purchase price whether or not the tenant owns a car. A household without a vehicle ends up subsidizing storage for the neighbors who have two. Housing is already scarce and expensive in most growing cities, and forcing builders to add costly concrete they did not choose is a strange way to make homes affordable.

The rules also kill projects before they start. A small lot downtown might comfortably hold eight apartments, but if the code demands twelve parking spaces, the math collapses and the lot stays vacant or becomes a parking lot itself. Older buildings often could not be legally built today because they lack the required spaces. Corner shops, small apartment blocks, and mixed use buildings that make neighborhoods lively are the first casualties. Cities spend years pleading for infill and density while their own code makes it illegal.

Then there is the land. Parking requirements spread everything apart. When every business needs a moat of asphalt, destinations drift farther from each other, walking becomes unpleasant, and driving becomes the only sensible choice. That is a self reinforcing loop. The code mandates parking, the parking pushes buildings apart, the distance makes cars necessary, and the necessity is then cited as proof that we need more parking. Cities also lose tax revenue, since an acre of dense mixed use building typically yields far more property tax than an acre of parking.

The strongest objection is that without mandates, spillover will clog neighborhood streets. It is a fair worry, but it misreads what repeal does. Removing a minimum does not ban parking. It lets builders and lenders decide how much to provide based on the real market. A new apartment building aimed at commuters with cars will still include spaces, because tenants will demand them and banks will insist. What disappears is the one size fits all number that ignores context. A building next to a rail station can build fewer spaces. A suburban shopping center can build more. Judgment replaces a formula.

Where street parking does get tight, the answer is to price it, not to mandate oversupply on private lots. Cities can charge fair rates for curb space, issue permits for residents, and use the revenue to improve the neighborhood. Economists have argued for decades that free curb parking is the real source of the scramble, because anything underpriced gets overused. Fix the price and the circling stops.

The evidence from places that have tried repeal is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and developers there have since proposed projects that previously would have been impossible, often with parking set by what the market wanted. Minneapolis, Seattle, and a growing list of cities and even entire states have followed with full or partial repeals. Builders have not abandoned parking. They have simply started to fit it to the site. Residents have not been buried under cars. Meanwhile, more housing has penciled out on lots that once sat idle.

Some will say this is an attack on drivers. It is the opposite. People who drive benefit when cities stop pretending that every trip must begin and end in a private space beside the door. Drivers also benefit from shorter distances, more choices, and cheaper homes near jobs. Those who cannot or choose not to drive benefit even more, since they stop paying for something they do not use.

A city that wants more housing, livelier streets, and a fairer cost of living should not hard code a parking quota into its law. Let the people who build and finance and live in these buildings decide how many spaces make sense. The curb will not collapse. It will finally be free to serve the neighborhood rather than the formula.
