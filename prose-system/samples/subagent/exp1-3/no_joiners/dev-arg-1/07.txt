Free the Curb: Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet law shaping everything around you. It is the minimum parking requirement, and it tells builders how many spaces they must provide before anyone will let them put up a home, a shop or a restaurant. A small apartment building might need two spaces per unit. A restaurant might need one space for every hundred square feet of dining room. These numbers feel like technical details, but they are among the most powerful planning decisions a city makes, and most of them were never carefully justified. Cities should repeal them.

Start with where the numbers come from. Many requirements were copied from manuals that drew on a handful of suburban sites, often counting cars at peak demand in places where parking was free and plentiful. Planners then adopted the figures as if they were laws of nature. The urban planning scholar Donald Shoup spent years documenting how thin this evidence was. Cities were not measuring what people needed. They were guessing high, and builders paid for the guess.

And they pay dearly. A single surface space can cost thousands of dollars to build, while a spot in an underground garage can run to tens of thousands. That cost does not vanish. It lands in the rent, the price of a coffee and the price of a haircut. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the space the law forced the landlord to build. Bundling parking into every unit is a hidden tax on people who can least afford it, and it falls hardest on the households that drive the least.

The requirements also quietly kill housing that would otherwise exist. Spaces take up room. On a small lot, the parking mandate can decide whether a project fits at all, and a developer who cannot fit the required spaces will build fewer homes or none. In a country short of housing, this is an absurd way to ration. We say we want more apartments near jobs and transit, then pass rules that make them physically impossible to build. Old main streets with narrow lots, the kind of places people love to visit, could never be built today because the parking math forbids them.

Defenders of the mandate raise a fair worry. If a new building offers too little parking, residents and customers will spill onto neighbouring streets and clog them. That is a real concern, but it is an argument for managing street parking well, not for forcing every private owner to oversupply it. Cities can price curb space, issue permits to residents and let the market find a sensible level. Removing a minimum does not ban parking. It simply lets the person who knows the most about the site decide how much is worth building. A developer who expects tenants to bring cars has every reason to provide spaces, because tenants will pay for them. Plenty of buildings will still come with generous parking. What changes is that the number reflects demand instead of a formula.

Evidence from places that have tried reform is encouraging. Buffalo scrapped its citywide requirements in 2017, and the sky did not fall. Developers kept building parking where it made sense and skipped it where it did not, and some small projects that had been stuck became viable. Minneapolis, Anchorage and a growing list of other cities have followed. Several states have gone further and limited mandates near transit. None of these places has reported the gridlock that critics predicted. The pattern is simple. When the rule goes away, buildings get cheaper and more varied, and drivers adjust.

Consider too what the mandates do to the shape of a city. Acres of asphalt sit empty most hours of the day, separating shops from the homes of the people who might walk to them. Every required space pushes destinations farther apart, which makes driving more necessary, which seems to justify still more parking. It is a loop that cities built for themselves. Breaking it frees land for homes, trees, patios and small businesses that actually pay taxes. A parking lot produces very little public revenue per acre compared with almost anything else you could put there.

There is a fairness argument as well. Free or cheap parking is often defended as a service to ordinary people, yet it is paid for by everyone, including those who walk, cycle or ride the bus. Since lower income households are less likely to own cars, they subsidise the habits of wealthier ones. Repealing the mandate does not take anything away from drivers who value a space. It just stops forcing the rest of us to buy it for them.

Nothing here requires cities to go to extremes. A sensible reform can keep accessible spaces for people with disabilities, require bike parking and let cities set maximums in the densest districts. It can phase in changes and pair them with real curb management. But the central step is the same. Take the number out of the zoning code and let people decide.

Cities spend enormous energy debating how to make housing affordable, streets lively and transport cleaner. Here is a reform that costs the treasury almost nothing and helps with all three. The parking minimum was an experiment that never produced the results promised. It is time to end it.
