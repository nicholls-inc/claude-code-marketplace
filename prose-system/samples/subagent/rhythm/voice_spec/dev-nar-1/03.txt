John Snow was a physician who lived in Soho, in central London, in 1854. He was forty-one and was known chiefly for his work in anaesthesia, having given chloroform to Queen Victoria during two of her confinements. He also held a view about cholera that most of his colleagues did not share. Cholera is an infection of the gut that causes severe diarrhoea and can kill a healthy adult within a day or two. It had reached Britain in the 1830s and returned in waves. The usual explanation was that it spread through foul air, known as miasma, which rose from rotting matter and drifted through poor districts. Snow thought the cause was something swallowed, and that it travelled mainly in drinking water.

He had already published this argument in 1849, but he lacked proof that would persuade many people. In 1854 the outbreak that began in his own neighbourhood gave him a chance to test it. In the last days of August, people living around Broad Street began to fall ill. By 2 September, 127 people had died. Within about ten days several hundred had died, and many of the survivors left the area. Snow said later that it was the worst outbreak of cholera he had seen in his own neighbourhood.

Snow did not start from a theory about the air. He started from a list of names and addresses. The General Register Office gave him the records of deaths, and he went through the streets to find out where those people had lived and what they had drunk. Most of the households were close to a public water pump in Broad Street, near the corner with what is now Lexington Street. Many of the deaths were in houses nearer to that pump than to any other. The water in the pump had a reputation for being clean and pleasant to drink, and people carried it home from some distance.

Snow then looked at the cases that did not fit. There were few deaths among the men working at a brewery on Broad Street. The brewery had its own well, and the workers were also given malt liquor to drink, so they had little reason to use the pump. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed about 535 people and recorded only five deaths. It also had its own supply of water. In the other direction, a widow living in Hampstead died of cholera after her family had arranged to have bottles of water from the Broad Street pump delivered to her. She had moved away from Soho years before but liked the taste. A niece who drank the same water also died. No one else in her neighbourhood was ill, so the pump was the only link Snow could find.

On 7 September Snow took his findings to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, which was responsible for the pump. He asked them to remove the handle so that no one could draw water from it. The Board agreed, and the handle was taken off the next day. Snow was open about the limits of this evidence. The number of new cases had already been falling before the handle was removed, partly because many residents had fled and partly because the outbreak may have been running its course. The removal of the handle did not by itself prove his case.

Snow continued to collect evidence, and he recorded the deaths on a map of the district. Each death was shown as a short black bar at the address where it occurred, and the bars were thickest around the pump. This map was published in 1855 in the second edition of his book on the communication of cholera. It was a clear way to show how the cases clustered, and Snow used it for that purpose, though it did not settle the question alone.

Another man added to the case. The Reverend Henry Whitehead, a curate at St Luke's church, did not believe Snow's theory at first. He knew the district and its people well, and he carried out his own inquiries. He found that the first case seemed to be a baby girl at 40 Broad Street, who had fallen ill in late August and died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the child's soiled napkins and emptied the water into a cesspool a few feet from the well. When the well was examined, it was found that its brick lining was cracked, and waste could have seeped in. Whitehead came to agree with Snow.

The official response was less favourable. A committee reporting to the Board of Health considered the Broad Street outbreak and did not accept that the water was the cause. Snow died in 1858 at the age of forty-five without seeing his view generally accepted. The organism that causes cholera was identified by Robert Koch in 1883. Snow's work is now regarded as a founding example of epidemiology, because he reached his conclusion by counting cases and checking where each one occurred.
