**Keep the Loop: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years, someone declares handwriting dead. Keyboards are faster, screens are everywhere, and the argument goes that children should spend their limited classroom hours on coding, typing, or critical thinking rather than on looping the tail of a lowercase "y". Several education systems have taken this seriously. Finland removed compulsory cursive from its national curriculum in 2016, and a number of American states dropped it after the Common Core standards left it out. The logic seems tidy: why teach a skill that adults increasingly never use?

I think this is a mistake. Primary schools should continue to teach joined-up handwriting, and teach it deliberately, not as an optional extra. My case rests on four claims. Handwriting builds thinking, not just penmanship. Joined writing is, for most children, the more efficient and more fluent form. Writing by hand remains a basic literacy skill that cannot be replaced by typing. And the usual arguments for abandoning it are weaker than they look.

**Handwriting is a cognitive act, not a clerical one**

The strongest reason to keep teaching handwriting is what it does to the learner. When a child forms a letter by hand, she has to retrieve its shape from memory, plan a sequence of movements, and coordinate eye and hand. Pressing a key requires none of this: every letter is the same action in a different place.

Research has repeatedly pointed to a difference. Studies using brain imaging, such as work by Karin James and colleagues at Indiana University, have found that young children who practise letters by hand show richer activation in reading-related brain regions than children who type or trace the same letters. A frequently cited study by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer found that students who took notes longhand did better on conceptual questions than laptop note-takers, apparently because the slowness of handwriting forces them to summarise rather than transcribe. That particular result has been debated, and later replications have been mixed, so I would not rest the entire case on it. But the broader picture is consistent enough: handwriting recruits more of the brain than typing does, and in the early years, when a child is learning what letters and words are, that matters.

Notice that this is an argument for handwriting in general, not joined-up writing in particular. A sceptic could reasonably say: fine, teach children to print, and stop there. This is where the second claim comes in.

**Joined writing is the faster, smoother destination**

Printing letter by letter is a perfectly good way to start. But it has a ceiling. Every letter requires the pen to lift and land again, and every lift is a small interruption. Joined writing reduces those interruptions. A child who has mastered a clean, simple joined script can write faster and with less fatigue, and fluency matters more than it might seem.

Here is why. A child who has to concentrate on forming each letter has less attention left for what she is trying to say. This is the idea behind much writing research: transcription skills, meaning handwriting and spelling, compete for the same limited working memory as composition. Steve Graham and others have shown that when handwriting becomes automatic, children write longer, more coherent texts of higher quality. Automaticity frees the mind. Joined writing, taught well, is simply the most automatic form for most writers, which is why most adults who handwrite at all drift towards some joined or semi-joined hand anyway.

There is a reasonable counterpoint. Some studies suggest that printed and joined scripts do not differ much in speed, and some schools have adopted a hybrid style that joins only the easy pairs. I accept that. A rigid insistence on looped, ornate cursive from age five would be a poor idea. But that is a point about how to teach joining, not whether to. A simple, lightly joined italic style, introduced once letter formation is secure, gives children a route from careful printing to quick, legible adult handwriting. Taking that route away leaves them to invent their own, usually with less guidance and worse results.

**Handwriting is still a basic literacy skill**

The practical objection is that adults type now. True, but it overstates the case. Children still write by hand constantly: in exams, in class, on forms, in notebooks, on whiteboards, on birthday cards. In England, written examinations are still handwritten, and a pupil who writes slowly or illegibly is penalised however good her ideas are. A child who cannot write fluently by hand at sixteen is at a real disadvantage, and the foundations for that are laid in primary school.

There is also a fairness issue. Children from homes with plenty of devices and quiet practice will learn to type regardless of what school does. Children without those advantages depend on school to teach the fundamentals. If schools stop teaching handwriting explicitly, the gap widens, because the families who can afford to compensate will do so and the rest cannot. Handwriting is cheap: it needs a pencil, paper, and a teacher who knows how to show a child how to hold a pen. It is one of the few skills where the playing field can be levelled almost for free.

And consider the children for whom handwriting is hardest. For some pupils with dyslexia or dyspraxia, a continuous flowing movement can actually help, because joined writing reduces reversals such as b and d and makes word boundaries clearer. Teachers of such pupils have long reported this, though the evidence base is modest. Whatever the precise mechanism, the fact that a joined script helps some of the children who struggle most is a reason to keep it available, not to remove it.

Finally, there is the plain matter of reading. A child who never learns joined writing will still meet it, in her grandmother's letters, in historical documents, in a teacher's marking, in a handwritten recipe. Being unable to read cursive is a small but real exclusion from a shared written culture. It takes only a few weeks of lessons to prevent it.

**Answering the objections**

The most common objection is opportunity cost: the school day is finite, and every minute on handwriting is a minute not spent on something else. This is fair, and it deserves a direct answer. Handwriting practice need not be long. Ten or fifteen minutes a few times a week, built into existing English lessons, is enough for most children to reach fluency. That is a small price for a skill that supports every other piece of written work. And the alternative is not free either: teaching keyboard skills properly takes time too, and typing instruction is a valuable addition, not a replacement. Children should learn both.

A second objection is that handwriting is a relic, and that insisting on it is sentimental. I do not find this persuasive. Sentiment is not my argument. I am not asking for copperplate or for marks deducted for a wobbly loop. I am saying that a skill which builds fine motor control, supports composition, helps struggling writers, and remains in everyday use is worth a modest slice of timetable. If the evidence changed, if it showed that joined writing offered nothing over printing or typing, I would change my view. The honest position today is that the evidence favours handwriting in general and is more cautious about joining in particular. That is a reason for care in how we teach it, not for dropping it.

A third objection is that the technology will only improve, and that voice input and AI will make writing by hand pointless. Perhaps. But children will not stop needing to think on paper, to annotate, to sketch ideas, to take quick notes without a screen, or to sit exams. And if anything, a world full of generated text makes personal, handwritten expression more valuable, not less. A handwritten note carries a trace of the person that a typed one does not.

**What good teaching looks like**

If we keep joined-up handwriting, we should do it sensibly. Start with secure letter formation in print during the first years, with attention to grip, posture, and pencil control. Introduce joins gradually, beginning with the simplest, most common ones, once children are confident. Choose a plain script with few flourishes, so there is little to unlearn and few awkward joins. Practise little and often, rather than in rare, long, dispiriting sessions. And be relaxed about the end result: the goal is legible, comfortable, reasonably quick writing, not a uniform house style. Children should be allowed to settle into a personal hand, as every adult does.

Alongside this, teach typing. Nothing in this argument is hostile to keyboards. The point is that handwriting and typing are complementary, and primary school is the right time to lay the foundations of both.

**Conclusion**

The case for abandoning joined-up handwriting depends on treating it as a decorative leftover from the age of quills. It is better understood as a tool: a way of thinking, a route to fluent writing, a safeguard for children who find literacy hard, and a skill still demanded in classrooms and examination halls. The research is not perfectly settled, and a fair-minded teacher should say so. But where the evidence is uncertain, the costs are also lopsided. Teaching handwriting costs a few minutes a week. Not teaching it risks leaving children slower, less fluent, and less equal. On that balance, we should keep the loop.
