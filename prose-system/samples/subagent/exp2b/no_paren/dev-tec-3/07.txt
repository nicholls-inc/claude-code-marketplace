A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current followed by constant voltage, usually shortened to CC-CV. The charger acts as a controlled source that first limits current and then limits voltage, and the slowdown near full charge follows from that switch.

Start with what the cell is. Two porous electrodes sit in an electrolyte, separated by a membrane. The negative electrode is typically graphite and the positive is a lithium metal oxide. Discharging moves lithium ions from graphite to the oxide, and charging forces them back. The cell voltage is the difference between the two electrode potentials, and each potential rises or falls with how full of lithium that electrode is. So voltage is a proxy for state of charge, though a loaded cell reads differently from a resting one.

In the constant current phase, the charger holds current at a set rate, often between 0.5 and 1 times the cell capacity per hour. Terminal voltage climbs steadily from around 3.0 V toward the upper limit, typically 4.2 V for common chemistries. This phase delivers roughly 70 to 80 percent of the capacity and is fast because the voltage margin above the open circuit voltage is large.

The terminal voltage is not the open circuit voltage. It is the open circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance, plus polarization terms from slow processes inside the cell. Those processes are charge transfer at the electrode surfaces, ion transport through the electrolyte, and solid-state diffusion of lithium inside the particles. As the open circuit voltage rises with charge, the terminal voltage reaches the 4.2 V ceiling. Pushing past it risks lithium plating on the graphite and oxidation of the electrolyte, both of which damage the cell and can be unsafe.

At that point the charger switches to constant voltage and holds 4.2 V. Now the quantity that can change is current. The driving margin is the fixed ceiling minus the rising open circuit voltage, so as the cell fills, that margin shrinks. Current must fall to keep the voltage drop across internal resistance within the margin. Solid-state diffusion makes this worse. Lithium arriving at particle surfaces must migrate inward, and surfaces become crowded while interiors lag. A crowded surface sits at a higher potential than the particle average, so the ceiling is reached sooner than the average state of charge suggests. The result is an approximately exponential decay of current, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor, though with a nonlinear open circuit voltage.

The charger ends the cycle when current falls to a cutoff, commonly 0.05 to 0.1 times the capacity rate. Waiting for zero current would take very long and add little capacity while keeping the cell at high voltage, which accelerates ageing.

In short, the slowdown is a deliberate response to a voltage limit set by safety and lifespan, combined with resistance and diffusion that make the cell's internal state lag its terminals.
