**Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Cursive**

Every few years, someone declares joined-up handwriting dead. The argument is always tidy. Children type on tablets from the age of three, adults rarely write more than a shopping list, and the hours spent looping an "e" into an "l" could go to coding, or to reading, or to anything else that sounds more like the future. I understand the appeal. I think the argument is wrong, and I think it is wrong in a way that matters most for the children it claims to help.

Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. They should teach it early, teach it properly, and treat it as a core skill rather than a decorative leftover.

Start with what the case against actually claims. It rests on three ideas: that handwriting is obsolete, that cursive specifically is slower to learn than print and no better once learned, and that the curriculum is crowded enough already. The first idea is the weakest. Handwriting is not obsolete; it has simply stopped being the only way to write. Children still sit exams by hand in most countries. They still fill in forms, take notes when a laptop is banned or flat, label diagrams, sign their names, and write birthday cards to grandparents who will keep them for decades. A skill does not die because a faster alternative exists for some tasks. We still teach children to swim in a world of bridges and ferries.

The second idea deserves more care, because it contains a grain of truth. Cursive is not magic. Plenty of adults write a fast, legible, unjoined hand, and plenty of cursive writers produce an unreadable scrawl. If joined-up writing were merely a stylistic preference, the case for it would be thin.

But it is not merely a style. It is a motor system, and that is where the evidence gets interesting.

When a child writes a word in a continuous flow, the pen stays on the page. The hand learns a single movement for the whole word, rather than a stop-start sequence of separate letters. That matters for speed, but it matters even more for what speed frees up. A child who has to think about every pen lift is spending attention on the mechanics. A child whose hand knows the shape of "ing" or "the" can spend that attention on what to say. Researchers who study writing have long described this as the link between transcription fluency and composition quality: when the physical act becomes automatic, the ideas get more room. Studies of young writers have repeatedly found that children who write more fluently produce longer and better pieces, not because the pen is magical but because working memory is finite and handwriting that is still effortful eats into it.

There is also a spelling argument that rarely gets the attention it deserves. Joined writing treats the word as a unit. Letters that always travel together, like "tion" or "ough", become patterns the hand remembers. Teachers who use joined script from the start often report that it helps with spelling, and the logic is plausible: a misspelled word feels wrong under the pen in a way that it does not when each letter is a separate stamp. I would not claim this is settled science. I would say the direction of the evidence is consistent, and that the cost of testing it in classrooms is small.

Then there is the question of what happens when children are not taught a joined style at all. They do not stay with neat print. They invent something. By the age of ten or eleven, a child who was never shown how to join letters will develop a private hybrid, half print and half improvised links, and it will be whatever happened to be quickest on the day. Some of those hybrids are lovely. Many are hard to read. The choice, in practice, is not between cursive and a clean alternative. It is between a taught system and an untaught one, and teaching has a good record of beating improvisation.

Dyslexia and other learning differences offer a further reason, and here the argument runs against what many people assume. A common view holds that cursive is harder for struggling writers, so we should spare them. Several specialist teaching traditions argue the reverse. Because every letter in a joined script starts from the baseline and flows rightward, there are fewer chances to reverse "b" and "d", and the continuous movement can reinforce the sequence of sounds in a word. Not every child benefits, and nobody should be forced into a method that plainly is not working for them. Still, a blanket claim that joined writing hurts the children who find literacy hardest does not survive contact with the specialists who teach them.

Now to the crowded curriculum, which is the objection that carries the most practical weight. It is true that schools are asked to do too much. But look at the actual cost. Handwriting practice is not a separate subject that must displace maths or reading; it is the vehicle through which children do most of their written work. Ten focused minutes a day in the early years, folded into spelling or phonics, is not a heavy burden. And the alternative is not free either. If we drop handwriting, we must teach keyboarding instead, which also takes time, and which children generally pick up faster at home than they ever pick up the pen.

That last point is the heart of the matter. Children will learn to type whether or not schools help them. Keyboards are everywhere, and the incentives to master them are overwhelming. Handwriting is different. It is a skill that exists only if somebody deliberately teaches it, and for most children school is the only place that will. If schools stop, the skill does not move to the home. It simply fades.

And something is lost when it fades. Experiments comparing note-taking by hand with note-taking on laptops have produced mixed and sometimes contested results, so I will not lean on any single study. But the broad pattern is hard to dismiss. Writing by hand slows a person down, and that slowness forces selection: you cannot transcribe everything, so you must decide what matters. Typists tend to capture more words and process them less. Brain-imaging work with children has suggested that drawing letters by hand engages the reading circuits more strongly than pressing keys does, which is an intriguing finding even if the field is young. The safest summary is that the pen and the keyboard are not interchangeable, and that a child who has only ever met one of them has a thinner toolkit than one who knows both.

There is a human argument too, and it is not sentimental. Handwriting is how most of us first make a mark that is recognisably our own. A child who can write her name in a confident, flowing line has done something that a font cannot do for her. She has put a piece of herself on the page. Older people will remember being able to recognise a parent's handwriting across a room, and the pang of finding it again in an old recipe book. We should be wary of dismissing that as nostalgia. A society that can read its own history, from letters, diaries and annotated margins, needs at least some of its citizens to read joined script. Teaching children to write it is the surest way to teach them to read it.

So what would good teaching look like? Not the grim drill of a century ago, with rulers and rapped knuckles. Not endless worksheets either. It would start with large, relaxed movements, patterns in sand and air and on whiteboards, before the pencil gets small. It would introduce the joins gradually, once letter formation is secure, and it would pick one clear script and stick to it, so that children are not asked to relearn their alphabet every September. It would watch for grip and posture, since a child in pain will not stay long at the table. And it would be honest about its aim: legible, fluent, comfortable writing, not copperplate.

It would also be flexible about the child who struggles. If a pupil has a motor difficulty, give her a keyboard and give her time. But make that a deliberate accommodation, not a default for everyone. The existence of exceptions is an argument for support, not for abolition.

The strongest form of the opposing view says that schools should prepare children for the world they will inhabit, and that world is typed. I agree with the premise and reject the conclusion. Preparing children for the world means giving them every tool a literate adult might need, and the pen remains one of them. It is cheap, it never runs out of battery, it works in an exam hall and a hospital ward and a muddy field, and it demands a kind of attention that screens seldom reward. Teaching it costs little. Not teaching it costs something we may not notice until it is gone.

So keep the joins. Teach them well, teach them kindly, and then let each child make the hand her own.
