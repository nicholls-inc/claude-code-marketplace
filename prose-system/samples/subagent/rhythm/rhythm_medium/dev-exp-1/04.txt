A heat pump does not create heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists from one place to another, and that is why it can warm your house even when the air outside feels bitterly cold. The key idea is that "cold" is only a relative term. Air at minus five degrees Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy, because absolute zero is far lower, at about minus 273 degrees. A heat pump is a machine for collecting that scattered energy and concentrating it indoors.

It does this using the same principle as your fridge, but run in the opposite direction. A sealed loop contains a refrigerant, which is a fluid that boils at a very low temperature, well below freezing. Outside the house, the refrigerant passes through a coil with a fan blowing air across it. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, heat flows into it, and this is enough to make it evaporate into a gas. So even on a frosty morning, the outdoor unit is quietly absorbing warmth from the air around it.

The next step is what makes the whole thing work. A compressor squeezes that gas, and compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, in the same way a bicycle pump gets warm when you inflate a tyre. The gas leaves the compressor much hotter than your radiators or indoor air need to be, perhaps 50 to 60 degrees. It then flows through a second coil inside the house, where it gives up its heat to your heating water or room air and condenses back into a liquid. Finally, an expansion valve drops the pressure, which cools the refrigerant again, and the cycle repeats.

The compressor needs electricity, but the amount of heat delivered is far larger than the electricity consumed. Engineers describe this with a figure called the coefficient of performance. A typical modern heat pump achieves around three on a mild day and perhaps two and a half on a very cold one, meaning every unit of electricity brings two and a half to three units of heat into your home. A gas boiler, by comparison, can never do better than turning one unit of gas into slightly less than one unit of heat. Even electric resistance heaters only manage one for one.

For a homeowner weighing up a boiler replacement, several practical points matter. Efficiency falls as the outdoor temperature drops and as the water temperature you ask for rises, so heat pumps work best when they run gently for long periods and deliver water at a lower temperature than a boiler does. That makes your existing radicators an important question. Many homes need some larger radiators, or better insulation, so that lower flow temperatures still keep rooms comfortable. A heat loss survey from an installer should tell you what your particular house needs.

Cold-climate models are designed to keep working at minus 15 or even lower, so a harsh winter alone should not rule one out. Expect the outdoor unit to be noticeably larger than a boiler flue and to produce a steady hum, so location and neighbours deserve some thought. Costs are the other big factor. The upfront price is usually higher than for a boiler, although grants or subsidies may narrow the gap, and running costs depend heavily on the price of electricity relative to gas where you live. A heat pump becomes especially attractive if you also have solar panels, a time-of-use tariff, or a wish to cut household carbon emissions, since the electricity powering it can become cleaner over time.

In short, a heat pump heats a house on a cold day by relocating outdoor heat rather than generating it. Whether it suits your home depends mainly on insulation, radiator sizing and local energy prices, so a proper survey is the sensible next step.
