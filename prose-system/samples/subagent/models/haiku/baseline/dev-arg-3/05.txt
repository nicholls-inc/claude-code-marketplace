Keep the Joined-Up Hand

Every autumn, in classrooms across England, Australia and the United States, a teacher opens a handwriting scheme and wonders whether it still earns its place. Keyboards sit on every desk, speech-to-text runs on every phone, and many children will do almost all of their writing on a screen. The case for dropping joined-up writing looks tidy: why spend time teaching a skill that most people will use less each year? But the tidy case is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, because it makes children faster and more fluent writers, it supports spelling and reading, and it gives them access to a written record that a keyboard will never replace.

Start with fluency, since that is the strongest argument. Writing has two jobs. One is to decide what to say: choosing words, building sentences, holding an argument in mind. The other is to get the words onto the page. For a young child, the second job can swallow almost all of the available attention. A pupil who has to lift the pen after every letter, pause to recall how a capital R begins, and then check whether the next letter sits on the line has little room left for the sentence she is trying to build. Joined script changes the physics of the task. Once the letters are linked, a word becomes one continuous movement with its own rhythm. The hand carries the pattern, and the mind is free to compose.

This is not a fanciful claim. Researchers including Virginia Berninger and her colleagues have found that handwriting automaticity, the ease with which children produce letters without conscious effort, is linked to the quality and length of their written compositions. Meta-analyses of handwriting instruction have reported gains in speed and legibility, with some evidence of improved composition when handwriting is taught well. The evidence is not perfect, and most studies are small, but the direction is consistent: when transcription becomes automatic, writing improves. Joined script is one of the most efficient ways to make that happen, because it removes the stops and starts that slow a print-based hand.

The second argument concerns spelling and reading. When a child writes a word in joined script, the movements form a chain. Common letter combinations become familiar shapes, and the hand learns patterns that the eye also comes to recognise. Research led by Karin James and colleagues has shown that children who form letters by hand show greater activation in brain regions associated with reading than children who type the same letters. That study concerned printing rather than joined writing, so it should not be overstated. Still, it points to a general principle: the motor act of forming letters helps fix them in memory, and the more a child practises forming a word as a whole, the more securely she tends to hold its spelling. Teachers who have watched children stumble over "could" and "would" will recognise the value of a movement that carries the word from start to finish.

Third, handwriting matters for thinking, not only for transcription. In a much-discussed 2014 study, Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer found that students who took notes longhand performed better on conceptual questions than those who typed their notes. Later replications have been mixed, and the effect may depend on how notes are taken and reviewed. But the core observation holds up: writing by hand forces a slower, more selective process. A student cannot transcribe a lecture word for word, so she must decide what matters. Joined writing, which is fluent enough to keep pace with thought, allows that selection to happen without the friction of constant pen-lifting. A child who writes quickly and legibly is a child who can think on paper.

Fourth, consider the practical world. In England, GCSE and A-level examinations are still handwritten. Many university exams, professional assessments and job application forms are too. In these settings, a candidate who writes fluently under time pressure has an advantage that has nothing to do with the content of her knowledge. Print can be perfectly legible, and some adults print all their lives, but most people write more quickly in a joined hand, and speed matters when the clock is running. Schools that stop teaching joining are quietly making that speed harder to reach.

Fifth, and perhaps most overlooked, a child who cannot read cursive is cut off from a large part of the written past. Letters from grandparents, birthday cards, recipe books, family diaries, parish registers, census records and wartime correspondence are written in joined hands. A pupil who learned only print will see those documents as a puzzle to be decoded rather than text to be read. Handwriting is a form of cultural literacy, and a school that drops it is deciding, on behalf of its pupils, that they will not be able to read much of their own history without help.

The objections deserve a fair hearing. The first is that print is more legible, and that children with messy joined handwriting are worse off than children with neat print. This is a real concern, but it argues for better teaching, not for abandoning joining. A well-taught joined script is at least as legible as print, and many children find it easier to keep letters evenly sized and spaced once the joins give them a consistent rhythm. The problem is usually that handwriting is taught in short, unsupervised bursts with little feedback. Fix the teaching and the legibility problem largely disappears.

The second objection concerns children with dysgraphia or other motor difficulties. For some of them, joined script is genuinely hard, and forcing it can cause distress without benefit. The answer is accommodation, not abolition. Children with a diagnosed difficulty should be allowed to print, to use a keyboard or to receive other support, and their assessments should be designed with that in mind. But the existence of a minority who need alternatives is no reason to deny the majority the benefits of a skill most of them can learn.

The third objection is about time. The curriculum is crowded, and every hour spent on handwriting is an hour not spent on something else. This is a fair point, but the numbers are small. Ten or fifteen minutes a day of focused handwriting practice, sustained over the primary years, is a modest investment for a skill that underpins the rest of a child's writing. Keyboard skills also need teaching, and the two are not in serious competition. A school can teach both, and most should.

None of this means primary schools should demand copperplate, penalise every blot, or spend lessons on decorative flourishes. The aim is a clear, fast, personal joined hand that a child can use for the rest of her life, taught with patience and realistic expectations. Schools should judge handwriting by whether it helps pupils write and read, not by whether it looks impressive on a display board.

The keyboard has earned its place in the modern classroom, and children should learn to type well. But typing is not a substitute for handwriting. It is a different tool, used for different purposes, and it does not give the same boost to fluency, spelling or the slow selective thinking that handwriting encourages. A child who can join her letters has a faster hand, a surer memory for words, a better chance in timed assessments and a way into the written history of her own family. Primary schools that teach joined-up writing are not clinging to the past. They are giving children a skill that the future still needs.
