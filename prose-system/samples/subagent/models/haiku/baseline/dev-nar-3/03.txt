On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, Boston's North End was going about its business. The day was unusually mild for January, and the air along the waterfront felt almost springlike. Horses pulled carts along Commercial Street, and workers moved through the warehouses and rail yards near the harbor.

Looming over the street was a steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, a thick, dark syrup the company was converting into industrial alcohol. The tank had been built in a hurry, and its thin, widely spaced rivets were not suited to the weight of its contents. It had leaked before. Neighbors had complained for months about sticky seepage on the sidewalks and a sweet smell in the air. The company reportedly dismissed the concerns, and the tank had never been properly tested before it was filled.

Around 12:30 p.m., the tank gave way. Witnesses heard a sharp report like artillery fire, then a deep, rising roar. The steel plates tore apart, and a wave of molasses estimated at 25 to 35 feet high burst into the streets. Moving at perhaps 35 miles per hour, it swept over everything in its path. It tore buildings off their foundations, twisted the supports of the elevated railway, and knocked a firehouse off its base. Horses and people were thrown through the air or pinned under debris. Those who could not escape were trapped in a flood so heavy that even running was nearly impossible.

When the wave slowed and spread, it covered several blocks. Rescue crews, sailors from a nearby Navy ship, and local residents waded through knee-deep molasses to pull out survivors and recover the dead. Twenty-one people died, including children and workers, and about 150 were injured. Crews spread sand and used seawater and salt to clean up, but molasses soaked into the streets and harbor, staining the water brown for months. Residents later recalled that the sweet, sticky smell lingered in the neighborhood for years.

The disaster quickly became a legal battle. Dozens of lawsuits were filed against Purity and its parent company. Investigators concluded that the tank was poorly built and had not been properly tested. In 1925, a judge found the company liable, and the settlements eventually totaled roughly $628,000. The case is still cited in engineering ethics courses as a lesson in what happens when corporate shortcuts meet public safety.

Today, the Boston molasses flood is remembered as one of the strangest disasters in American history: a day when a city was flooded with syrup, and a company's negligence left its neighbors to pay the price.
