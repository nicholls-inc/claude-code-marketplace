Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. The case rests on a plain observation: a rule that tells a builder how many car spaces to provide is a rule about private business decisions, and it is usually set without any evidence of what a particular building needs.

Start with how the numbers get chosen. Many American codes ask for ten or more spaces per 1,000 square feet of restaurant floor, or two spaces for every flat. Donald Shoup, the planning professor whose 2005 book The High Cost of Free Parking made the main academic case against these rules, found that the figures often trace back to a handbook of surveys taken at suburban sites where parking was free. A site surveyed at its busiest hour, with no price on the space, tells you how many cars arrive when parking costs nothing. It does not tell you how many a city should oblige people to build.

The cost of the requirement is then paid by someone. A space in a multi-storey car park typically costs a developer in the region of $25,000 to $35,000 to build, and an underground space costs more. That cost is folded into rents, sale prices and the price of a meal. A household without a car pays part of it anyway. In a city with high land values the effect is larger still, because each space also uses floor area that could have been a flat or a shop. Small sites are often undevelopable under these rules, since the required spaces will not fit on the lot.

The requirement also shapes what gets built. An old building in the centre of a town may have no parking and may be perfectly useful, but if a new owner wants to change its use, the code can demand spaces that cannot be added. Los Angeles recognised this in 1999 when it exempted conversions of old downtown buildings to housing from its parking rules, and the downtown housing stock grew considerably afterwards. Many such conversions were legally impossible before the exemption.

Cities that have gone further show what happens when the minimum is removed. Auckland abolished minimum requirements across the whole city in 2016, and Buffalo, New York did so in 2017. In neither place did parking vanish. Developers kept building it where they judged that tenants and customers would want it, and left it out where they did not. That is the point of the reform. It moves the decision to the person who has to find the money and who bears the loss if they get it wrong. A developer who builds too few spaces loses tenants, and one who builds too many has paid for something that earns nothing. Both have a reason to look at the actual site, which a uniform ratio in a code does not.

The usual objection is that without a minimum, new buildings will push their cars onto the surrounding streets. This is a real problem, but it is a problem of how the kerb is managed. Free, unmetered street parking invites overflow in a way that priced parking does not. Residential permit schemes and meters set at a level that keeps one or two spaces free on each block deal with spillover directly, and the revenue can be spent on the same neighbourhood. A city that is worried about overflow should price its streets. Forcing every new building to carry a surplus of spaces, whatever the street rules are, treats the symptom at much greater expense.

A second objection is that some people really do need to drive, including disabled people, tradespeople and families in places with poor transit. That is true, and removing a minimum does not stop anyone providing parking. Accessible spaces can stay as a separate requirement, since they serve a specific need, and most developers will continue to include ordinary spaces where the demand is clear. What changes is that a corner shop in a walkable district is no longer forced to behave like a supermarket on a ring road.

There is also a point about fairness. Minimum requirements treat car ownership as the default and everything else as an exception. Someone who walks, cycles or takes the bus to a restaurant is paying for parking they do not use, through higher prices, and nobody asked them. A rule that lowers the cost of building homes and shops where people can already get around without a car is, in my view, one of the cheaper ways a city can encourage more of them.

None of this means a city should ban parking or set a maximum. Some places will still want generous provision, and the market will supply it. The argument is narrower. A fixed number written into law, applied to every site regardless of its location or its likely users, is a poor tool, and the evidence from places that have dropped it is that the buildings still get their parking where it is needed. Cities that retain minimums are making a costly choice on behalf of others, and they should stop.
