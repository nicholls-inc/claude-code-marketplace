On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, a sharp rise from the bitter cold of the previous days. Around 12:30 in the afternoon, people were eating lunch, children were playing near the waterfront, and workers were going about their business along Commercial Street.

Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly 90 feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. The molasses was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions during the First World War, and soon after, in the rum that Prohibition was about to make scarce. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply in 1915. It leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups, and the company's response was to paint the tank brown to hide the stains. No one had ever properly tested whether it was safe. The tank was filled to the brim only a day earlier with a fresh delivery, and the warm weather expanded the already fermenting liquid inside.

Then, with a rumble that witnesses compared to a machine gun's rattle, the rivets began to fail. The tank ruptured, and a wave of molasses estimated at up to 25 feet high surged into the streets at around 35 miles per hour. It carried a force that crushed buildings and knocked a section of the elevated railway off its supports. Freight cars were swept aside. Houses were torn from their foundations, and the firehouse near the tank was wrecked, burying firefighters beneath its collapsed walls.

The sweet flood killed 21 people and injured about 150. Among the dead were children, a number of dock workers, and horses that had been standing in the street. Many victims drowned in the thick liquid, while others were crushed under debris. Rescuers struggled through knee-deep syrup that clung to everything it touched. The cold air soon thickened the molasses, trapping people in a sticky mass that made rescue slow and desperate. Sailors from a nearby ship, along with police, Red Cross workers, and soldiers, waded in to dig out survivors. It took days to recover all the bodies, and the harbor ran brown with molasses until summer. For decades, residents said that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled sweet.

The disaster led to one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company claimed anarchists had blown up the tank, but a court-appointed auditor, after hearing from more than 3,000 witnesses and experts, concluded that the structure was simply too weak. Purity Distilling was held responsible and eventually paid roughly 628,000 dollars in settlements, with families of victims receiving about 7,000 dollars each.

The case helped change how America builds. It strengthened demands for engineering oversight, and Massachusetts soon required that architects and engineers stamp their plans to certify safety. The molasses flood remains a strange but sobering reminder of what happens when profit and haste are allowed to outrun caution.
