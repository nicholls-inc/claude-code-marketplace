Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and they should do so with confidence rather than apology. The case against it has become fashionable in recent years, usually in the form of a shrug: children will type for the rest of their lives, the argument goes, so the hours spent looping an "e" into an "l" would be better spent on coding, or on nothing in particular. That view mistakes what handwriting is for. It is not a museum skill kept alive for sentimental reasons. It is a foundational tool for thinking, for reading, for physical coordination and for fairness in the classroom, and the evidence for dropping it is much thinner than its confident advocates suggest.

Start with the most practical point, which is that children still have to write by hand, and they have to do it a great deal. Pupils sit exams with pen and paper. They take notes on worksheets, fill in forms, label diagrams and jot down answers in lessons where no device is open. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly is handicapped in all of these situations, and the handicap compounds over time. Joined-up writing exists because it solves a real problem: it lets the pen stay on the page, so that a whole word flows from a single movement instead of being assembled from separate marks. For most children who master it, cursive is simply faster than print, and speed matters when a ten-year-old is trying to get a thought down before it escapes. The child who prints every letter separately will often hit a ceiling in fluency that holds back the quality of their written work, because so much of their attention is consumed by the mechanics of forming letters.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is where the research is most interesting. Writing is a demanding task for young children precisely because several things must happen at once. They have to decide what to say, find the words, spell them, and then physically produce them. Working memory is limited, and anything that takes up capacity for the physical act leaves less for composition. Studies of children in the early years of school have repeatedly found that handwriting fluency predicts the length and quality of their compositions, even after other factors are accounted for. When the hand becomes automatic, the mind is freed to attend to meaning. A fluent joined hand is therefore not a decorative extra; it is part of the machinery that lets a child become a better writer in the first place.

There is also a body of work suggesting that writing by hand helps children learn to read. Forming letters with a pen engages the motor system in a way that tapping a key does not, and some neuroimaging studies have found that handwriting activates reading-related brain areas more strongly than typing does, particularly in young children who are still learning to recognise letters. Pupils who draw letters by hand appear to remember them better than those who type or trace them. Cursive in particular has a useful side effect, since the continuous movement helps children perceive a word as a single unit with a shape and a rhythm, which supports spelling. A child who has joined the letters of "because" a hundred times has felt the word in their fingers as well as seen it on the page, and that kind of embodied repetition is a quiet but real aid to memory. None of this proves that cursive is uniquely powerful, and honest researchers say so. But the direction of the evidence is consistent enough that abandoning handwriting instruction would be a gamble with children's literacy as the stake.

The same applies to higher education and the habits of learning. Several well-known experiments have compared students who take notes by longhand with those who type them, and a number have found that the longhand writers retain conceptual material better. The usual explanation is that typists can transcribe almost word for word, which requires little thought, while handwriters cannot keep up and are forced to summarise, which requires them to understand. The details of these studies have been debated and some replications have been mixed, so it would be foolish to lean too heavily on any single result. Still, the broad lesson is that the effort of writing by hand is not wasted effort. A small amount of friction in the act of recording ideas can turn out to be the very thing that makes them stick. A child who has a comfortable, quick handwriting style is more likely to use it for this purpose throughout school, whereas a child for whom writing is slow and painful will avoid it and lose the benefit.

Then there is the question of equity, which opponents of handwriting tend to overlook. It is easy to say that everyone will type, but that assumes every child has reliable access to a keyboard, at school and at home, and the settled quiet in which to use one. Many do not. Pen and paper are cheap and universal. A handwriting curriculum asks nothing of a family beyond a pencil, which makes it one of the most democratic skills a school can teach. Moreover, children with certain difficulties often benefit from structured handwriting instruction rather than being excused from it. For pupils with dyslexia, a joined style can reduce the letter reversals that plague print, since a "b" and a "d" are formed with entirely different movements when they are written in cursive. Teachers working with children who have dyspraxia often find that a consistent flowing pattern is easier to learn and automate than a series of stops and starts. To abandon joined writing would be to remove a tool that helps precisely those children who are most at risk of falling behind.

It is worth being fair to the other side. Critics are right that some of the old ways of teaching cursive were rigid and joyless, with children made to copy rows of identical loops while teachers marked them down for slanting the wrong way. They are right that there is limited time in the primary day and that keyboard skills deserve a place in it. They are also right that adult handwriting varies enormously, and that plenty of successful people write in a messy semi-print that bears little resemblance to what they were taught. These points are worth taking seriously, but none of them amounts to an argument against teaching joined-up writing. They are arguments for teaching it well, briefly and sensibly. A few short sessions each day in the early years, focused on posture, grip and fluent movement, is a modest investment. And the fact that adults eventually develop personal styles is exactly what we should expect: a taught foundation gives a person something to adapt, whereas a person who was never taught has nothing to build on.

Another objection holds that cursive is outdated because the world no longer requires it. This sounds persuasive until one looks at how skills actually work. Very little in a primary curriculum is taught because adults will use it in exactly the same form. Children learn long division even though calculators exist, and they learn to spell even though software corrects their mistakes. We teach these things because the underlying competence supports further learning and because a person who understands the process is better placed to judge the machine. Handwriting belongs in that category. It trains fine motor control, builds the link between thought and symbol, and provides a fallback when the technology fails, as it always eventually does. A society in which people can only communicate through a device is more fragile than one in which they can also pick up a pen.

There is a cultural argument too, though it should not be the main one. Joined-up writing is the script in which a vast amount of the written record exists, from family letters to historical documents and the margins of old books. A child who cannot read cursive is cut off from that record, and has to ask someone else to translate the handwriting of their own grandparents. Teaching children to read and write it is a small act of continuity between generations, and it costs very little. Sentiment alone would not justify the practice, but it is a pleasant bonus alongside the cognitive and practical ones.

What, then, should a sensible approach look like? Children should begin with print, as they do in most schools, and move to a joined style once their letter formation is secure, usually around the age of seven. The goal should be legibility and fluency rather than perfection, with no marks lost for a slanting hand. Teachers should keep the sessions short and frequent, and should integrate them with spelling and composition rather than treating them as a separate drill. Keyboard skills should be taught alongside, not instead. The two abilities complement one another, and a child who can do both has more choices than a child who can do only one.

The deepest reason to keep teaching joined-up handwriting is that it is a modest, well-understood way of helping children become competent writers and readers. It costs little, harms no one and supports those who struggle most. The arguments against it rest on a picture of the future in which writing by hand has vanished, but that future has been predicted for decades and has not arrived. Until it does, we owe children the skill that lets them put their own thoughts on paper quickly, clearly and without needing anything but a pencil. Schools should teach it, and teach it well.
