In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell on Soho, a crowded, working-class district of London. The disease was cholera. It begins with sudden vomiting and violent diarrhea, and it can drain a healthy person of so much fluid that they die within a day or two. Between the last days of August and the middle of September, hundreds of people in a few streets around Broad Street were killed. Entire families were wiped out. Many residents fled, and shops closed.

Almost nobody understood what caused it. The dominant belief was the miasma theory, the idea that disease spread through foul, poisonous air rising from rotting waste and filthy ground. Cholera, by this reasoning, was a kind of bad smell made deadly. London certainly stank. Its streets and cellars were full of sewage, and the Thames was an open drain. The theory seemed to fit what people could see and smell, and most respected authorities accepted it.

One man did not. John Snow was a physician in his early forties, a quiet, teetotal man from a modest background in York. He had made his name as a pioneer of anaesthesia, and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He had also been thinking about cholera for years. In 1849, during an earlier epidemic, he had published an argument that the disease was not breathed in but swallowed. It was something in contaminated water or food, he suggested, that attacked the gut directly. Few people were persuaded. Doctors noted that cholera victims suffered in the bowels rather than the lungs, which Snow thought was strong evidence for his view, but it was not proof.

When the Broad Street outbreak erupted, Snow lived only a short walk away. He saw a chance to test his idea in a small, well-defined place. His method was simple and stubborn. He went door to door, asking who had fallen ill, who had died, and where they got their drinking water. He obtained death records from the local registrar and compared them with what he learned on the streets.

A pattern began to appear. Most of the dead lived close to a public water pump on Broad Street, where residents filled their buckets. Snow drew a map of the area and marked each death with a black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars clustered thickly around the pump. The map showed visually what the figures suggested: whatever was killing people seemed to be centered there.

Snow was careful, though, to look at the exceptions, because exceptions are where a theory is truly tested. A workhouse nearby, home to hundreds of poor residents, had very few deaths. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped lightly. Its workers were given malt liquor to drink and had access to their own supply of water. By contrast, a widow living far away in Hampstead died of cholera. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her every day. A niece who drank from the same bottle also died.

Convinced, Snow took his evidence to the local Board of Guardians on 7 September. He asked them to remove the pump handle, which would force people to find water elsewhere. The officials were skeptical, but they agreed. The handle was taken off the next day. The epidemic had already been fading, as many residents had fled or died, so it is hard to say how many lives the removal saved. Later critics have used this point to question how decisive it was. But the act itself became famous, a symbol of acting on evidence rather than on assumption.

Snow did not stop there. With the help of a local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who was at first a doubter, he kept investigating. Whitehead knew the neighborhood and its people far better than Snow did. He found the likely beginning of the outbreak: a baby girl who lived at 40 Broad Street had died of a diarrhea illness days before the epidemic exploded. Her family's cesspool lay very close to the well. Inspection later showed that its brickwork was cracked and that waste could seep into the water. The well, which many had thought sweet and pure, was fouled.

The authorities were not won over. An official inquiry concluded that the evidence did not support the water theory, and the Board of Health continued to favor miasma. Snow died in 1858, still unrecognized, at only forty-five. Yet the work he had done lasted. Over the following decades, as germ theory took hold and the cholera bacterium was identified, his reasoning was recognized as correct. London built its great sewer system, and clean water became a basic public right.

Today Snow is honored as a founder of epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations. His real achievement was not a single discovery but a way of working: gather careful data, map it, hunt for the exceptions, and follow the evidence even when it contradicts what everyone believes.
