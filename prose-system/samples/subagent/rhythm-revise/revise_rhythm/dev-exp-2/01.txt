A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth, and the need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time that do not quite agree. One is astronomical: a day is the time the Earth takes to spin once, and the planet's rotation is the original basis for our hours and minutes. The other is atomic: since the 1950s, clocks that count the vibrations of atoms, such as caesium, have kept time with astonishing steadiness, drifting by less than a second over many millions of years.

The trouble is that the Earth is a far less reliable timekeeper than an atom. Tides, the movement of molten rock in the planet's core, shifting ice and weather all nudge its rotation, and over the long run the spin has tended to slow, so a day is slightly longer than the 86,400 seconds that atomic clocks count out. If nothing were done, official time would slowly pull ahead of the sun in the sky. To prevent this, in 1972 the international community introduced Coordinated Universal Time, which runs on atomic seconds but is kept within 0.9 of a second of Earth-based time. When the gap threatened to grow too large, a leap second was inserted, usually at the end of June or December, so that the last minute of the day lasted 61 seconds; twenty-seven have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016.

For most people this was invisible, but for computers it has been a persistent headache, because software generally assumes every minute has 60 seconds and a minute with 61 breaks that assumption. Leap seconds have been blamed for crashes and outages at websites, airlines and financial systems, and technology companies have devised their own workarounds, such as spreading the extra second across the whole day. Because leap seconds cannot be predicted far ahead, being decided by observation only months in advance, they are especially awkward for systems that need exact timing, including telecommunications, satellite navigation and power grids.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of units, voted to stop using leap seconds by 2035, reasoning that the problems they cause now outweigh the benefit of keeping official time tied to the sun to within a second. Few people could notice such a difference, and the gap would take about a century to grow to a minute. Interestingly, the Earth has recently been spinning slightly faster, which could even call for a rare negative leap second, an untested prospect that worries engineers further. Timekeepers expect to revisit the question of how to reconcile the two clocks in the future, perhaps by permitting a much larger correction, such as a minute, far less often.
