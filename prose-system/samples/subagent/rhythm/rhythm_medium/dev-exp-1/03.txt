A heat pump can warm a house on a cold day because it does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists from one place to another. Heat is not the same thing as warmth: even air at minus five degrees Celsius contains a great deal of thermal energy, because its molecules are still moving, and they only stop moving at absolute zero, which is about minus 273 degrees. A heat pump is built to collect some of that energy from outdoor air and deliver it indoors, where you want it.

The trick that makes this possible is the same one your fridge uses, run in the opposite direction. Inside the outdoor unit is a sealed loop of refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at a very low temperature, far below freezing. A fan draws outdoor air across a coil full of this refrigerant. Because the refrigerant is colder than the air, even on a frosty morning, heat flows into it and it evaporates into a gas. That is the first step, and it works for the simple reason that heat always travels from warmer things to colder ones, and the refrigerant has been made colder than the winter air.

Next comes the compressor, which is the part that uses electricity. It squeezes the gas, and compressing a gas makes it much hotter, in the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. The refrigerant leaves the compressor at perhaps 50 to 70 degrees Celsius, which is well above the temperature of your rooms. This hot gas then passes through a second coil indoors, where it gives up its heat to the water in your radiators or underfloor heating, or to the air in your home, and as it cools it condenses back into a liquid. Finally an expansion valve lets the pressure drop, which chills the refrigerant again so it is ready to go back outside and pick up more heat.

The reason this is so attractive is efficiency. A gas boiler turns the energy in its fuel into heat at roughly 85 to 95 percent efficiency, so you can never get more heat out than the fuel contained. A heat pump only spends electricity on moving heat, not on creating it, so for every unit of electricity it uses it typically delivers about three units of heat across a winter, and sometimes more in milder weather. Engineers call this ratio the coefficient of performance. In practice that can mean running costs close to or below those of gas, depending on how your local electricity and gas prices compare, and it also means lower carbon emissions, particularly as the electricity grid gets cleaner.

There are honest limits you should weigh. Efficiency drops as it gets colder, because the pump has to lift heat across a bigger temperature gap, so on the coldest days it may deliver closer to two units of heat per unit of electricity. Modern units still work well at minus 15 or even lower, but they do so less cheaply. Heat pumps also run best at lower flow temperatures than a boiler, so they like a well-insulated house with generously sized radiators or underfloor heating. A poorly insulated older home may need some upgrades, such as loft insulation, draught proofing or a few larger radiators, before a heat pump performs at its best. Those costs belong in your decision.

Upfront price is the other consideration. A heat pump installation usually costs noticeably more than a new boiler, although government grants in some countries can close much of that gap. You will also need space outdoors for the unit and usually a hot water cylinder indoors.

So the question to ask is not whether a heat pump can cope with a cold day, because it can. The better question is whether your home is well insulated enough, and your energy prices favourable enough, for the savings to repay the higher installation cost. A survey from a certified installer, with a heat loss calculation for your specific house, is the most reliable way to find out.
