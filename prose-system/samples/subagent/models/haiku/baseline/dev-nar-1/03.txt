In the last days of August 1854, the streets around Broad Street in Soho began to empty, and then the houses began to fill with the dead. Cholera had returned to London. Families within a few hundred yards of one water pump were dying within hours, after days of vomiting and a terrible loss of fluid that left the body cold and blue.

At the time, most doctors believed cholera spread through "miasma," poisonous air rising from rotting waste. John Snow did not accept this. He was a physician of forty-one who had already made his name as an anaesthetist and would later give chloroform to Queen Victoria. Years earlier he had argued that cholera was caught by swallowing something, not by breathing bad air. Broad Street gave him a chance to test that idea.

Snow had no microscope that could show the germ, and no laboratory. What he had was a notebook and a method. With the help of Henry Whitehead, the local clergyman, he went door to door and asked families where they got their water, what they ate, and who had been sick. He marked every death on a street map of the district with a small black bar at the house where it happened. The bars bunched thickly around one spot: the pump at the corner of Broad Street.

The map alone was persuasive, but the exceptions made it stronger. The brewery on Broad Street, whose workers drank beer rather than water, recorded almost no deaths. A workhouse in Poland Street, which had its own well, was largely spared. A woman in Hampstead, several miles north, who had grown fond of the taste of Broad Street water and had it sent to her, fell ill and died. Each case fit the pattern. Each one pointed at the water.

Snow took his findings to the local authorities. On 8 September, after the outbreak had already peaked and many residents had fled, the parish removed the handle from the pump. Nobody could say for certain whether this saved lives. The epidemic was already fading, and Snow himself admitted he could not separate the effect of the handle from the effect of people leaving. Still, cases fell sharply afterward, and the removal became the symbol of his argument.

Snow then tested his idea more widely, using an accident of history. In London, two water companies supplied neighbouring houses. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company drew its water from the Thames below the point where sewage flowed into it. The Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream, to cleaner water. Snow compared the death rates in homes served by each company. Households supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall died of cholera at roughly eight times the rate of those supplied by Lambeth. The houses were similar in almost every other way. The water was the main difference.

Snow published his full account in 1855, in a book titled On the Mode of Communication of Cholera. Many doctors remained unconvinced. Some clung to miasma, and others argued that the disease was simply carried by the air or by the sick. Acceptance came slowly. Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, before most of his ideas were fully adopted. The city's great sewer system, built in the following years by Joseph Bazalgette, did more than any single argument to end the disease in London.

Snow's real achievement was method. He did not know what cholera was. He asked where the cases were, looked for what they had in common, checked the exceptions, and tested his explanation against a comparison. Decades later, Robert Koch identified the bacterium that causes cholera, confirming what Snow had reasoned out from a map and a notebook.
