On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was a crowded neighborhood of tenements, stables, warehouses and docks. It was an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about forty degrees after a bitter cold snap, and people were out in the streets enjoying the thaw. Dockworkers ate lunch. Children played near the waterfront. Wagons and trucks rumbled along Commercial Street beneath the tracks of the elevated railway.

Looming over the neighborhood was a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. It stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and would be turned into industrial alcohol, which was used in munitions during the Great War and soon would be needed for liquor as Prohibition approached.

The tank had been built in a hurry in 1915 and was never properly tested. Residents knew it was troubled. It groaned and shuddered when filled, and it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups and buckets. Instead of repairing it, the company painted the tank brown so the stains would not show.

Shortly after noon, a deep rumbling was heard. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun as rivets shot out of the steel. Then the tank burst. A wall of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty five feet high, surged down the streets at roughly thirty five miles an hour. It tore buildings from their foundations. It bent the steel supports of the elevated railway and nearly pulled a train from its track. Wagons, horses and people were swept up in the sticky tide.

The wave was only the beginning of the horror. As the molasses cooled in the winter air it thickened, and rescuers found it terribly hard to wade through. Survivors were trapped in a dark brown sludge that clung to their clothes and filled their mouths. Police, firefighters, sailors from a nearby ship and Red Cross workers rushed in to help. Twenty one people died, among them children, and about a hundred and fifty more were hurt. Horses suffered too. Rescuers worked for days, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer.

The aftermath was nearly as remarkable as the disaster. The company insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, but a lengthy court case followed, one of the earliest big class action suits in Massachusetts. After years of testimony from thousands of witnesses, an auditor ruled that the tank had been poorly built and the company was responsible. Families received settlements, though modest ones, in the range of several thousand dollars for each death.

The disaster changed how Americans built things. It helped push states to require engineers to sign off on construction plans. And for decades, people in the North End said that on hot summer days you could still catch the sweet smell of molasses in the air.
