On 15 January 1919, a Tuesday, the temperature in Boston rose to about 5 degrees Celsius after several days of bitter cold. At the foot of Copp's Hill in the North End stood a steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It was 50 feet tall, 90 feet across, and held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, which was fermented to make industrial alcohol, including for munitions. The tank had been built in 1915 and had leaked from the start. The company's response was to paint it brown, so that the stains would be less obvious. Residents of the neighbourhood collected the molasses that seeped from the seams and took it home in tins. The company never filled the tank with water to test it. The Prohibition amendment had been ratified the day before, and the tank had recently been filled from a ship that had arrived from the Caribbean.

At about 12.30 in the afternoon, the tank failed. Witnesses described a rumbling noise and the sound of rivets popping like machine-gun fire. A wave of molasses, probably between 8 and 15 feet high at its front, moved out from the base at an estimated 35 miles per hour. It carried a mass of about 12,000 tons. The wave broke the supports of the elevated railway on Commercial Street and pushed a train off its track. It crushed a firehouse, whose members were eating lunch, and carried a house off its foundations. Several buildings in the area collapsed or were swept away. A section of the tank itself was thrown into the street.

Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Those killed included children who had been playing or collecting wood near the tank, a city worker, and some of the firefighters. Many victims were caught in the molasses, which was cold and thickened as it spread. It pulled down rescuers as well as the people they were trying to reach. Police, soldiers, sailors from a ship in the harbour, and Red Cross workers searched through the sticky mass for survivors. The last bodies were recovered months later, some from the harbour. One man was not identified for some time.

The clean-up took weeks. Crews used salt water from fire boats to wash the streets, and sand to cover what remained. The molasses was said to have turned the harbour brown until summer. For years afterwards, some people in the North End said that the area smelled of it on hot days.

The company first suggested that anarchists had blown up the tank, since the firm had been a target of such suspicion during the war. A court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, heard testimony for three years, from about 3,000 witnesses, and concluded in 1925 that the tank had been poorly built and not adequately tested. The company was held liable and paid damages, which were about 600,000 dollars in total, or roughly 7,000 dollars for each victim's family. The case is often cited as an early instance of a company being held responsible in a large class action, and it contributed to stricter rules in Massachusetts requiring engineers to sign off on building plans.
