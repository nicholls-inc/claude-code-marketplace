On the evening of April 13, 1970, some fifty-five hours into a mission meant to land on the Moon, the three astronauts of Apollo 13 were finishing a television broadcast to a public that had grown bored with lunar flights. Jim Lovell, the commander, Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, and Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, had shown viewers around their cramped spacecraft and signed off with good wishes. Only a few minutes later, a routine request from Mission Control would begin one of the most celebrated rescues in the history of spaceflight.

The controllers asked Swigert to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the cone-shaped command module where the crew lived. Stirring kept the supercold contents from stratifying and gave accurate gauge readings. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later there was a bang, a shudder, and a cascade of warning lights. Swigert reported the now famous understatement, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it with a slight change, saying "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause lay in a chain of errors that predated the flight. The oxygen tank had been damaged during ground handling years earlier, when it was accidentally dropped a couple of inches. Later, during testing, its heaters were run on a higher voltage than the tank's thermostatic switches were built for, which fused the switches shut and scorched the Teflon insulation on the wires inside. Nobody knew. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark in the bare wiring ignited the insulation in a tank full of liquid oxygen. Pressure surged, the tank ruptured, and the blast blew off a panel of the service module. The second oxygen tank was crippled too, and the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity and water needed oxygen to run.

At first no one on the ground or in the spacecraft understood how bad it was. Lovell, looking out of a window, saw gas streaming into space and told Houston so. That sight settled the matter. The command module's own batteries were meant only for the final hours of reentry, and the oxygen was bleeding away. Within about three hours the crew would lose their primary ship. The Moon landing was gone, and the question was now whether three men could be brought home alive.

The answer was the lunar module, the spidery landing craft named Aquarius, still attached and still healthy. It was designed to support two men for roughly two days on the lunar surface, and now it had to serve as a lifeboat for three, for four days. The crew moved into it, and Swigert and Lovell powered down the command module, which Swigert called "Odyssey", to preserve what little battery life remained. Mission Control, led by flight director Gene Kranz, worked in shifts through the night, treating the crisis as a problem to be solved rather than a disaster to be mourned. Kranz is often credited with the phrase "failure is not an option," although it was actually coined for the 1995 film.

The first decision was how to turn around. The spacecraft was on a path that would swing it around the far side of the Moon, and a free-return trajectory would bring it back toward Earth eventually, but the crew had left that path earlier to aim for their landing site. Controllers chose to fire Aquarius's descent engine, which had been built to land on the Moon, to push the ship back onto a path that led home. Later, after swinging around the Moon, the crew used the engine again for a longer burn to speed the return and bring splashdown forward, to the Pacific Ocean, by many hours. Lovell steered by hand, aligning the craft by sighting the Earth in a window, since the guidance system had been shut down to save power.

Power was one of three great constraints, and the others were water and air. Aquarius carried limited water to cool its electronics, so the crew switched off almost everything that was not essential. The temperature inside fell to near freezing. Condensation beaded on the walls, and the men, in their light flight clothing, slept poorly if at all. Haise developed a serious infection and a fever. All three drank very little, and they lost weight and became dehydrated.

The most urgent crisis was invisible. A lunar module's air was scrubbed of carbon dioxide by canisters of lithium hydroxide, and Aquarius had enough to support two men for two days. With three men breathing for much longer, carbon dioxide levels began to climb toward the danger zone. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square, while the lunar module's receptacles were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the same materials the crew had on board, including cardboard from flight manuals, plastic bags, hoses, spacesuit parts and duct tape, and worked out a way to adapt the square canisters to the round fittings. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the astronauts built the contraption themselves. Within hours the carbon dioxide level fell, and the device became known as the mailbox.

There was one more hurdle, and it concerned the command module itself. Turning it back on after days of cold had never been planned. Controllers and engineers, including a team led by astronaut John Young, wrote a new startup procedure on the fly, one that had to bring each system back using only the battery power and water available. They had to get it right the first time, with a ship that had been frozen for days.

On April 17, as the spacecraft closed in on Earth, the crew released the service module, and for the first time they saw the damage. A whole panel was gone and the interior hung out, a ragged wound along the side. Lovell described it to Houston. Then they moved back into Odyssey, powered it up, and let go of Aquarius, which had saved their lives, to burn up in the atmosphere on its own.

Reentry had one last tension in store. Communications are always lost for a few minutes as a spacecraft plunges through the atmosphere, surrounded by hot plasma, and the blackout was expected to last about three minutes. The minutes passed with no word. Controllers watched the clock, and the world, listening, wondered. After a delay of roughly a minute and a half beyond the expected time, Swigert's voice came through the static. Then the parachutes appeared on television, three bright canopies above the capsule.

Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, on April 17, 1970, safely and within sight of the helicopters sent to meet it. The three men were weak, unshaven and exhausted, but they were alive. A mission that had been dismissed as routine days earlier had held the world's attention for nearly six days.

Apollo 13 never landed, yet it is often called a successful failure. A review board traced the accident to the tank's history and recommended redesigns, and later Apollo missions flew with changes that included a third oxygen tank and improved wiring. The flight showed how much mattered not only the machines but the people behind them, who improvised from what was at hand, thought clearly under pressure and refused to give up on three men a quarter of a million miles from home.
