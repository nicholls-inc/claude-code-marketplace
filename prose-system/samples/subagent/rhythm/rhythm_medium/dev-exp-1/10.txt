A heat pump can warm a house on a cold day because it does not create heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists. Even when the air outside is at freezing, or well below, it still contains a great deal of thermal energy, because air only reaches zero energy at absolute zero, about minus 273 degrees Celsius. A heat pump's job is to gather some of that low-grade energy from outside and deliver it indoors, where it is useful.

The trick is the same one your fridge uses, run in the opposite direction. A sealed loop of refrigerant circulates between an outdoor unit and an indoor heat exchanger. This refrigerant is chosen because it boils at a very low temperature, far below zero. As it passes through the outdoor coil, the outside air, though cold, is still warmer than the refrigerant, so heat flows into the liquid and makes it evaporate into a gas. Heat always flows from warmer to cooler, and the refrigerant is kept colder than the air, so the flow goes the way the machine needs.

Next comes the compressor, which is the part that uses electricity. It squeezes the refrigerant gas into a much smaller volume, and compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, in the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. The gas leaves the compressor much hotter than your living room, typically somewhere between 40 and 70 degrees depending on the system. Now the heat flows the other way. In the indoor heat exchanger, the hot refrigerant gives up its energy to the water in your radiators or underfloor heating, or to the air in your home, and it condenses back into a liquid as it does so.

Finally, the liquid passes through an expansion valve, which drops its pressure suddenly. That makes it very cold again, colder than the outside air, and the cycle starts over. Nothing is used up in the loop. The refrigerant simply keeps carrying heat from outdoors to indoors.

The reason this matters for your bills is efficiency. A gas boiler turns each unit of gas energy into at most about 90 to 95 percent of a unit of heat, because some is always lost in the flue. A heat pump delivers more heat than the electricity it consumes, since most of the energy is collected from the air rather than paid for. Engineers describe this with a coefficient of performance. A figure of 3 means that one kilowatt-hour of electricity brings three kilowatt-hours of heat into the house. Over a whole season, good installations commonly average between 2.5 and 4.

There are honest limits you should weigh. Performance falls as the weather gets colder, because the machine must lift heat across a bigger temperature gap, so on a very cold night the figure may drop towards 2. Modern units still work well in sub-zero conditions, and many are rated down to minus 15 or lower, but they do work harder. The temperature of the water you send to your radiators matters too. Heat pumps are most efficient at lower flow temperatures, so a house with large radiators or underfloor heating suits them better than one with small, old radiators designed for a hot boiler. Good insulation helps for the same reason, because it lets the system run gently and efficiently.

Cost is the other question. Electricity usually costs more per unit than gas, so the efficiency advantage has to outweigh that price gap, and the answer depends on your local tariffs. Installation is also a significant outlay, though grants or subsidies are often available. A sensible next step is to ask installers for a heat loss survey of your home, which will tell you whether your radiators, insulation and budget make the switch worthwhile.
