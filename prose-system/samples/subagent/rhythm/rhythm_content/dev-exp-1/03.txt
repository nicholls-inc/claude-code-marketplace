A heat pump does not make heat by burning something; it moves heat that already exists in the outdoor air into your house, and the reason this works on a cold day is that "cold" is only relative. Air at minus five degrees Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy, because heat only vanishes from a substance at absolute zero, which is about minus 273 degrees. A heat pump is a machine for collecting that scattered energy and delivering it where you want it.

It does this with a sealed loop of refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at very low temperatures. Outside, the refrigerant circulates through a coil with a fan blowing air across it. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, even on a freezing day, heat flows into it and it evaporates into a gas. That gas then passes through a compressor, which squeezes it, and squeezing a gas heats it sharply, much as a bicycle pump warms up when you inflate a tyre. The refrigerant leaves the compressor hotter than your radiators or underfloor circuits need to be, so inside the house it gives up that heat to the water in your heating system and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops its pressure, which chills it far below the outdoor temperature, and it returns outside to collect more heat. The electricity you pay for runs the compressor and fans, but most of the energy delivered to your home was gathered from the air rather than generated.

This is why heat pumps are quoted with a coefficient of performance, usually between about two and four. A figure of three means that each unit of electricity produces three units of heat, so the machine is effectively three times as efficient as an electric heater, and it comfortably beats a gas boiler, whose best condensing models deliver somewhat under one unit of heat per unit of gas. Performance does fall as the weather gets colder, because the compressor has to lift the heat across a bigger temperature gap, but modern units designed for cold climates keep working well below minus ten and are widely used in Scandinavia and Canada.

What matters more for your decision is how the heat pump meets your house, because it is most efficient when it runs at lower water temperatures, typically 35 to 45 degrees against the 60 to 75 a gas boiler often uses. It therefore tends to run for long, steady periods rather than firing hard in bursts. If your home is well insulated and your radiators are large enough to emit enough heat at those lower temperatures, you may need little more than the unit itself. If the house leaks heat or the radiators are small, you may need to upgrade some radiators or add insulation, and that is where much of the real cost and disruption can hide. An installer should carry out a room-by-room heat loss calculation instead of quoting from the size of your existing boiler.

Running costs depend on the price gap between electricity and gas in your area. Electricity usually costs several times as much per unit, so a heat pump with a coefficient of performance of three saves money only if the ratio between the two prices is lower than that figure; where the gap is narrower, or where you have time-of-use tariffs, solar panels or a battery, the case improves considerably. You should also weigh the purchase price, which is generally higher than a new boiler before any grants, the space for an outdoor unit and a hot water cylinder, and the noise of the fan, which is modest but worth checking against your neighbours' windows.

The practical test is therefore whether your home can be made to work at low flow temperatures at a sensible cost, and whether local energy prices and incentives make the running costs competitive. Ask for quotes that state the expected seasonal efficiency, the heat loss figures behind the sizing, and any radiator or insulation work needed, and be wary of anyone who cannot explain those numbers. If your boiler is near the end of its life, that is usually the best moment to switch, since you avoid paying for a like-for-like replacement you will not need.
