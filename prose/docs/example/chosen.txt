A test run in November 2026 looked at why Claude's prose reads differently from human writing, and at whether the usual fixes work. Its finding is that, on current Claude models, the common advice for making AI writing sound human targets the wrong things, and that single style rules make the text worse on measurable features. The test covered 1,848 generated samples on 12 briefs, with 10 samples per brief per condition. Most samples came from Claude Sonnet 5.5, and baselines were also taken on Opus 5.5, Fable 5.2 and Haiku 5.5. The human reference was 133 documents, about 258,000 words, all published before November 2022.

The best-known tell is the em dash, and the baseline did not have it. The Sonnet 5.5 baseline contained no em dash in 120 samples, about 109,000 words, while the human texts used 2.9 dashes per 1,000 words. The differences that did show up were elsewhere. Baseline mean sentence length was 14.6 words, against a human mean of 20.3. In the baseline, 57% of samples used headings and 36% used lists. An instruction to write plain prose with no headings or lists brought mean sentence length to 19.0 words, which is close to the human figure.

Single rules about rhythm did worse. The instruction "Vary your sentence length" raised the share of sentences of four words or fewer from 7.3% to 10.1%. The instruction "Do not use a short sentence to set up a longer one" raised mean sentence length to 39.6 words, about twice the human mean. Banning dashes, semicolons, colons and parentheses cut digits per 100 words from 2.2 to 1.2. A revision pass asked to fix rhythm raised mean sentence length from 19.0 to 27.0 words and raised colons, semicolons and commas.

One pattern held in every model and condition. Short and long sentences alternated more than they do in the human texts. The lag-1 autocorrelation of sentence length was between -0.07 and -0.19 for the models and 0.04 for the human texts. The one method that moved this number toward the human value was to draft five candidates under a full voice description and pick the one closest to the human profile. That took the autocorrelation from -0.14 to -0.05.

The limits of the test are plain. No reader study was run, and one reader judged six pairs in a pilot. All measures are surface features counted by a script, so the results describe how the text is built and say nothing firm about how people receive it.

My own view, which the test does not establish, is that this matters for anyone who drafts documents with a model. A rule such as banning dashes addresses a feature the baseline did not have, and a rule about rhythm can push the text well past the human range. The results point to a fuller description of the voice, several drafts, and a selection step as the approach that moved the measure closest to human writing, and they suggest that stacking more single rules is unlikely to help.
