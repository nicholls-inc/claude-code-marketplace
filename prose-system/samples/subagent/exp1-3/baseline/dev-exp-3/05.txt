# How two strangers can agree on a secret in public

## The problem

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret number, which they can then use as the key to lock their later messages. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Every word they say, an eavesdropper called Eve hears too.

This sounds impossible. If Eve hears everything, how can anything stay secret? For most of history people assumed it was impossible. Secret codes needed a key that both sides already held, and getting that key to the other person meant meeting in private or sending a courier. In the 1970s, researchers showed there is a way round it. The trick rests on a one-way street in mathematics: some things are easy to do and very hard to undo.

## Mixing paint

Here is the idea with paint rather than numbers.

Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common starting colour, say yellow. Everyone, Eve included, knows this.

Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red, and mixes it into a big tub of the yellow. She sends the resulting orange tub to Bob. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, say blue, mixes it into another tub of yellow, and sends the green result to Alice.

Eve sees both tubs travel past: the orange one and the green one. She also knows the starting yellow.

Now Alice takes Bob's green tub and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange tub and adds his secret blue. Both tubs now contain yellow, red and blue, so both end up the same muddy brown. That brown is the shared secret.

Eve cannot get there. She has the orange and the green, but to make the brown she would need either Alice's red or Bob's blue, and those never left their owners. Why can't she just un-mix the orange to find the red? Because separating mixed paint is, for practical purposes, impossible. Mixing is easy and unmixing is not. That is the one-way street.

Mathematics has its own version of mixing paint, and it is a surprisingly ordinary one.

## The mathematical version: clock arithmetic

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You go round the clock and start again once you reach the top. Mathematicians call this working "modulo", and write it as "mod". On a 12-hour clock, 15 mod 12 = 3. Put simply, *mod* means "divide, then keep only the remainder".

We will use a clock with 23 positions, 0 to 22, instead of 12. The number 23 is chosen because it is prime, meaning nothing divides it evenly except 1 and itself. (Real systems use primes that are hundreds of digits long.)

The other ingredient is repeated multiplication, which is just powers. 5^3 means 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. On our 23-position clock, we keep only the remainder after dividing by 23. 125 divided by 23 is 5 with 10 left over, so 5^3 mod 23 = 10.

Now the whole exchange, with small numbers.

**Step 1: public agreement.** Alice and Bob announce two numbers openly: the clock size, 23, and a base number, 5. This is the yellow paint. Eve hears it. It does not matter.

**Step 2: private choices.** Alice secretly picks the number 6. Bob secretly picks 15. These are the red and the blue. They are never spoken aloud.

**Step 3: mix and send.**
- Alice computes 5^6 mod 23. Working it out: 5^2 = 25, which is 2 on our clock. Then 5^6 = (5^2)^3 = 2^3 = 8. She sends **8** to Bob.
- Bob computes 5^15 mod 23, which comes to 19. He sends **19** to Alice.

Eve hears 8 and 19. These are the orange and green tubs.

**Step 4: add your own secret again.**
- Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power: 19^6 mod 23. The answer is **2**.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power: 8^15 mod 23. The answer is also **2**.

They have the same number, 2, and neither ever said it aloud. That is their shared secret.

## Why do they get the same answer?

This is where the maths earns its keep. Alice's number 8 is 5 raised to the power 6. When Bob raises it to the power 15, he gets (5^6)^15, which is 5^(6×15), which is 5^90. Meanwhile, Bob's number 19 is 5 raised to the power 15, and Alice raises it to the power 6, giving (5^15)^6 = 5^(15×6) = 5^90.

Both routes lead to 5^90, because 6 × 15 and 15 × 6 are the same number. Multiplication does not care about order. That is all it takes. Each person effectively applies both secrets, in a different order, and the order does not matter. This matches the paint: red then blue gives the same brown as blue then red.

## Why can't Eve do the same?

Eve knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the secret, she needs one of the private numbers, 6 or 15. For these tiny numbers she can simply try them all: test 5^1, 5^2, 5^3 and so on until she sees 8. She would find that 5^6 gives 8, and so on. A child with a calculator could do it in a minute.

So the real security comes from size. If the clock has hundreds of digits rather than two, the secret powers are also enormous. Working forward is quick: there is a shortcut of repeated squaring, which is how we got 5^6 using 2^3 above, and it handles huge numbers with a modest number of steps. But working backward, from "5 to some power gives this number" to the power itself, has no known shortcut. Powers on a clock jump around with no visible pattern. 5^6 gives 8, 5^7 gives 17, 5^8 gives 16, and there is no way to tell from looking at 8 which power produced it, other than trying. With numbers of that size, trying every possibility would take longer than the age of the universe, even with every computer on Earth working on it.

This is the one-way street again: easy to go forward, effectively impossible to go back.

## Where the "public key" comes in

The method above is usually called key exchange. The name "public-key cryptography" comes from the wider family of ideas it belongs to. In that family, each person holds a pair of numbers that are mathematically linked. One is public and may be shared with anyone, like Alice's 8. The other is private and never leaves its owner, like her 6. Anything locked using the public number can only be unlocked using the matching private one, and the link between the two is a one-way street of exactly this kind. Different schemes use different maths for the street, but the principle is the same: publish something that is easy to make and impossible to reverse, and keep the reversing information to yourself.

## What the method does not do

It is worth being honest about the limits.

First, Alice and Bob cannot be sure who they are talking to. Suppose Eve does more than listen and actually sits in the middle of the line. She could do one exchange with Alice, pretending to be Bob, and another with Bob, pretending to be Alice. Each of them would share a secret with Eve without knowing it. Defending against this needs a way to prove identity, which is why websites carry certificates vouching that a public number really belongs to them.

Second, the secret number is usually just the beginning. Once the pair share it, they use it as the key for a faster, simpler kind of lock that works well on long messages. The slow clock arithmetic is used only for the introduction.

## The summary

Two people announce a base number and a clock size. Each secretly picks a private number, uses it to turn the base into a public number, and sends that across. Each then applies their own private number to the other's public one. Because multiplication does not depend on order, both reach the same result. An eavesdropper sees only the public numbers, and getting from those back to a private number is, for large enough numbers, beyond anything that can be computed. Easy one way, impossible the other: that single imbalance lets strangers build trust from nothing, in full view of everyone.
