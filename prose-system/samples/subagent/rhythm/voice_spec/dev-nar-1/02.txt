Cholera is an infection of the bowel. It causes violent diarrhoea and vomiting, and a person can lose so much water that they die within a day or two of the first symptoms. The disease reached Britain in 1831 and returned in several epidemics over the following decades. Most doctors at the time held that it was carried by miasma, a poisonous air that rose from rotting waste and damp ground. John Snow, a physician in Soho, London, disagreed. He was best known for giving chloroform to patients during surgery and childbirth, but he had studied cholera since the epidemic of 1848 and 1849. He concluded that people caught it by swallowing something that had come from the bodies of the sick, and he set out this argument in a short pamphlet in 1849. Few readers were convinced.

The outbreak that gave him a test began in the last days of August 1854 in the streets around Broad Street, a crowded part of Soho where many families shared a house and most waste went into cesspools under the floors and yards. In the three days after 31 August, 127 people in the area died. Within ten days there had been about 500 deaths, and many residents had left. Snow lived on Sackville Street, a short walk from the centre of the outbreak, and he began to look into it himself.

He obtained the list of deaths from the Registrar General's office and went from house to house asking where each household got its water. Few homes had piped water, and most people drew theirs from public pumps. Snow marked each death on a map of the streets with a short bar at the address, so that the bars stacked up along the roads. They clustered around the pump on Broad Street, near the corner with Cambridge Street. A few of the dead lived nearer to other pumps, and when he asked, he found that several of them had sent for water from the Broad Street pump or had used it on the way to school.

Two groups stood out because they escaped. The workhouse on Poland Street housed 535 people and had five deaths, and it had its own well. A brewery on Broad Street employed about seventy men and none of them died. They were given malt liquor to drink, and the brewery also had its own well. A death in Hampstead, far from Soho, pointed the same way. A widow named Susannah Eley, who had once lived in Broad Street, had water from the pump brought to her each day because she liked the taste. She fell ill after drinking it and died on 2 September, and a niece who had drunk some of it also died. Snow found no other cholera in her part of Hampstead.

On 7 September Snow took his findings to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, which was responsible for the district. The next day the handle was taken off the pump so that no one could draw water from it. Deaths were already falling by then, partly because so many people had fled, and Snow said so himself. The removal of the handle therefore cannot be shown to have ended the outbreak.

Henry Whitehead, the curate of St Luke's, knew the neighbourhood well and at first doubted Snow's idea. He did much of the local work that followed. He traced the first case to a baby girl at 40 Broad Street, who fell ill in late August and died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspool in front of the house. A surveyor who examined the well later found that its brickwork was in poor repair and that the cesspool lay only a few feet away.

Snow had already made a larger comparison in south London during 1853 and 1854. Two companies, Southwark and Vauxhall and Lambeth, supplied water to the same streets, and sometimes to neighbouring houses. Lambeth had moved its intake upstream of the city's sewage in 1852, and Southwark and Vauxhall still drew from the polluted stretch of the Thames. In the epidemic that followed, the death rate was 315 per 10,000 houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall and 37 per 10,000 houses supplied by Lambeth.

The General Board of Health reported in 1855 that it could not accept his view, and Snow died in 1858, aged 45, with the question still open. The bacterium that causes cholera was described by Filippo Pacini in 1854 and identified again by Robert Koch in 1883, and the link to contaminated water became settled medical opinion. Many historians regard the Broad Street study as an early and clear example of epidemiology, because Snow worked from counted deaths, addresses and a map instead of from a theory about air.
