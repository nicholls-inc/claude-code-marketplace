On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston woke to an unseasonably mild day. The temperature had climbed above forty degrees after a bitter cold snap, and the neighborhood's Italian and Irish families, along with the dockworkers and city employees who shared its crowded streets, went about their noon routines. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The tank had been built in a hurry in 1915. Molasses was a valuable commodity in those years. It was shipped up from the Caribbean and fermented into industrial alcohol, which was in high demand for munitions during the Great War. The company's priority was speed and profit, not safety. The builders never consulted an architect or engineer, and the structure was never properly tested. When it was first filled, the walls groaned and leaked so badly that neighbors collected the seeping syrup in pails. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown so the leaks would be harder to see.

Just after half past noon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun as the rivets shot loose, followed by a deep rumble. The steel wall tore open and a wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen feet high at its crest, rushed out at around thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a force that could snap the supports of an elevated railway, and it did, bending the track and nearly toppling a train. The wave crushed buildings as if they were made of matchsticks. A firehouse was knocked off its foundation, and men inside were trapped in the wreckage.

The cold air quickly thickened the syrup. Those caught in it found that every movement made things worse. Rescuers sank to their knees as they waded in. Horses struggled and drowned. Children who had been playing in the street were swept away. Policemen, soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship rushed to help, and Red Cross workers set up a makeshift hospital. They worked through the afternoon and into the night, cutting into the sticky mass in search of survivors. In all, twenty-one people died and about a hundred and fifty more were injured. For days afterward, the harbor ran brown, and the smell of molasses lingered in the neighborhood for decades.

The aftermath produced one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank, a claim meant to shift blame away from its own negligence. After years of testimony, the court appointed an auditor who heard from hundreds of witnesses. He concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound and that the company was responsible. Purity paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements, a sum that was meager for those who had lost family members.

The disaster left a lasting mark on American law. It helped push Massachusetts to require that engineers and architects approve the plans for large structures, and it contributed to a broader culture of building regulation. The flood faded from public view for a while, but it is remembered now as a grim lesson about what can happen when profit outruns caution.
