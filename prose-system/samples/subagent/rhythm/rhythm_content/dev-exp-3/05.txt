Suppose you want to agree on a secret number with someone you have never met, using a telephone line that you know a stranger is recording, so that everything either of you says aloud is also heard by the eavesdropper. The old way to share a secret was to meet beforehand and agree on a code book or a password, and that is useless when no meeting is possible, which is exactly the situation for two computers that connect for the first time across the internet. It seems almost logically impossible, since anything the two of you can say to each other the listener can also hear, and yet there is a method, first published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, that works because some mathematical operations are easy to do in one direction and extraordinarily laborious to undo.

The easiest way to see the shape of the idea is with paint. Imagine that you and a stranger each have a private tin of paint that you never show anyone, and that you both also own a large public tub of yellow paint, the same colour for both of you and known to the eavesdropper as well. You each mix a generous amount of your private colour into a portion of the yellow and send the resulting mixture across the line, so that the listener sees both mixtures and the yellow, but cannot see what went into either. When your stranger's mixture arrives you add your own private paint to it, and the stranger does the same with yours, and since you have both ended up with yellow plus your two private colours, the final shade in your two pots is identical. The eavesdropper holds only the yellow and the two half-mixed pots, and because mixed paint cannot be unmixed to reveal its ingredients, the eavesdropper has no way of reaching that final shade without one of the private colours.

Numbers can play the role of paint if we choose an operation that behaves like mixing, in that it is easy to perform and practically impossible to reverse. The operation that mathematicians use is clock arithmetic, which you have met if you have ever worked out that four hours after ten o'clock it is two o'clock. On a clock with twelve hours, you count round the dial and discard whole laps, and the same can be done with a dial of any size, so that on a dial with 23 positions the number 25 lands on position 2 because one full lap of 23 uses up most of it, and the number 46 lands on position 0 because it is exactly two laps. The remainder after dividing by the size of the dial is what we keep, and it is the only thing that matters, so I will write "mod 23" to mean that the answer has been reduced to a position on that dial.

The second ingredient is repeated multiplication, which is simply the powers you learned at school, such as 5 multiplied by itself six times, written 5 to the power 6. When these powers are reduced onto a dial, the results jump around in a way that has no visible pattern, so that 5 to the power 6 is 15625, which lands on position 8 of the 23-position dial, while neighbouring powers land on positions that bear no obvious relation to it. Working forwards is straightforward, because you can multiply step by step, always reducing to the dial as you go. Working backwards is the hard part, because if I tell you that some power of 5 landed on position 8 and ask which power it was, you have no shortcut and must simply try 1, 2, 3 and so on until the answer appears, and with a dial this small that takes a moment, whereas with a dial of several hundred digits it would take longer than the age of the universe.

Here is the whole exchange with the small numbers. You and the stranger publicly agree on the dial size 23 and on the base number 5, and the eavesdropper hears both. You secretly choose 6 and the stranger secretly chooses 15, and neither number is ever spoken. You compute 5 to the power 6 on the dial, which gives 8, and you announce 8. The stranger computes 5 to the power 15 on the dial, which gives 19, and announces 19. The eavesdropper therefore knows 23, 5, 8 and 19, and nothing else. You now take the stranger's 19 and raise it to your private power 6, which gives 2 on the dial, and the stranger takes your 8 and raises it to the private power 15, which also gives 2, so you both hold the secret number 2 without it ever having crossed the line.

The reason you both land on the same number is that raising a power to a power simply multiplies the exponents, and multiplication does not care about order. The stranger's announcement was 5 raised to 15, and you raised that to 6, which amounts to 5 raised to 15 times 6, while your announcement was 5 raised to 6, and the stranger raised that to 15, which amounts to 5 raised to 6 times 15, and since 15 times 6 and 6 times 15 are both 90, the two calculations are the same one wearing different clothes. Each of you has used your own private exponent, which the other never learns, together with the other person's public announcement, which carries the other's exponent locked inside it, and the lock only opens for the person who already holds one of the two exponents.

The eavesdropper, meanwhile, would need to recover either 6 or 15 from the announcements 8 and 19, and that means working backwards from a position on the dial to the power that produced it, which is precisely the operation we said has no shortcut. With a dial of 23 positions the eavesdropper would find the answer in seconds, since there are only a couple of dozen possibilities to try, which is why a real system uses a dial whose size has hundreds of digits, and private exponents of similar length, so that the number of powers to try is larger than the number of atoms in the planet. Your own computer is not troubled by such numbers, because it can reach a huge power by repeated squaring, multiplying a number by itself and then squaring the result again, so that a few hundred steps are enough where a naive count would need more steps than could ever be performed. The same asymmetry therefore protects you and defeats the listener, since the legitimate parties need only do the cheap forward calculation while the attacker faces the impossible reverse one.

Two cautions keep this from sounding like magic. The first is that no one has proved that the reverse calculation is truly hopeless, only that after decades of effort by some of the best mathematicians in the world nobody has found a fast way to do it on ordinary computers, and so the security rests on a well-tested belief rather than on a theorem. The second is that the method protects against someone who merely listens, but not against someone who can intercept the line and impersonate the stranger, because if an impostor sits in the middle and runs one exchange with you and another with the real person, each of you will faithfully agree a secret with the impostor. For this reason real systems add a way of proving who is on the other end, and this is where the other half of public-key cryptography comes in, with a person holding a private key that only they can use to sign a message and a public key that anyone can use to check the signature, a pair related by the same kind of one-way mathematics, and the guarantee that the stranger is who they claim to be comes from checking that signature against a public key you have reason to trust.

Once the exchange has been completed, the shared number is used as the key to an ordinary and very fast scrambling method that both ends know, which is why the secret you agreed in a few moments with a stranger can then protect a long conversation, a payment or a password as it crosses the open internet.
