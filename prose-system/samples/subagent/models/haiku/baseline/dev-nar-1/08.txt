The Pump on Broad Street

In the late summer of 1854, London had lived with cholera for more than twenty years. The disease came in waves, and when it arrived it killed fast. A healthy adult could be dead within a day, emptied by vomiting and a violent, watery diarrhoea. Almost everyone agreed on the cause: bad air. Doctors called it miasma, the foul vapours said to rise from sewers, rotting refuse and overcrowded graveyards. John Snow, a physician with a busy practice in Soho, did not believe it.

Snow had spent years arguing that cholera spread through what people drank. He could not see the cause. Nobody could, because the bacterium responsible would not be identified until 1883. So he worked the way a detective works. He collected death records, studied where people lived and what they did, and asked why one street suffered while the next one was spared.

At the end of August 1854, cholera broke out in a crowded corner of Soho centred on Broad Street, now called Broadwick Street. Deaths came quickly, dozens a day. Families fled, shops shut, and the undertakers could not keep up. Snow stayed and went from house to house. Wherever someone had died, he made a mark on a street map. He wanted to see where the deaths were, not just how many there were.

The marks clustered around one place: a water pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street. The pump drew water from a well, and Snow noticed that the people who died mostly lived nearer to it than to any other pump. Then he began to examine the exceptions, and they were the most telling. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street, which had its own well, recorded very few deaths. The workers at the Broad Street brewery, who drank beer rather than water, were largely spared. And a woman living in Hampstead, several miles away, had fallen ill and died after her household drank water carried in from Broad Street, where she had a relative who had died. She had preferred the taste of that particular pump.

Snow took these findings to the local authorities, the vestry and Board of Guardians of St James's parish. On 8 September the pump handle was removed, and the local people could no longer draw from that well. The story is often told as a triumph, a clean experiment that proved everything at once. The truth was messier. The epidemic was already fading, partly because so many residents had fled or already fallen ill. Snow himself admitted that this made the timing hard to interpret. What convinced the officials was the combination of evidence, not one dramatic moment.

Some of the best detective work came from a local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, who had been a skeptic of Snow's ideas. He went house by house, asking about habits and water sources, and eventually found that a cesspit near the well had been leaking into the ground. He concluded that the pump was the source of the outbreak, and his findings supported Snow's account.

Snow later published a fuller report, including a version of his map, and he turned his method on the wider city. London's water came from several companies. Households served by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, which drew from the Thames below the city's sewage outflows, suffered far more deaths than households served by the Lambeth Company, which had moved its intake upriver. The pattern repeated itself, and it was hard to explain away.

Still, the miasma theory did not disappear overnight. Many authorities resisted the idea that invisible agents in water could cause a disease, and sanitary reform moved slowly. Snow died in 1858, at only forty-five, without seeing his ideas fully accepted. Only decades later did the microscope confirm what his maps had suggested.

Today Snow is remembered as one of the founders of epidemiology, the study of how diseases spread through populations. His lesson is simple and still useful: when something harms people in clusters, look at the places they share. The pump on Broad Street was not special because it was dangerous. It was special because someone was willing to count, map and ask why.
