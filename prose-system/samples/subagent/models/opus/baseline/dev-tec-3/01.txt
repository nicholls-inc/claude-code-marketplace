**Charging a Lithium-Ion Cell: CC-CV and the Slowdown Near Full**

A lithium-ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions between two host structures. The negative electrode is usually graphite, and the positive electrode is a layered metal oxide such as NMC or LCO. When the cell charges, an external source drives electrons through the circuit into the graphite. At the same time, lithium ions leave the oxide, cross the liquid electrolyte, and slot in between the graphite layers. This process is called intercalation. Discharge runs the same process in reverse. The cell voltage is the difference in electrochemical potential between the two electrodes, and it rises as the graphite fills and the oxide empties. For a typical cell, it goes from about 3.0 V empty to 4.2 V full.

Nearly all chargers follow a constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV) profile. In the constant-current phase, the charger holds the current at a fixed value, often 0.5C to 1C (C being the current that would fill the cell in one hour). The terminal voltage rises during this phase. Once it reaches the upper limit, typically 4.2 V, the charger switches to constant voltage. It holds that voltage, and the current falls off roughly exponentially. Charging ends when the current drops below a cutoff, commonly C/20. The CC phase usually delivers 70-80% of the capacity in well under an hour. The CV phase adds the last 20-30%, but it can take as long again or longer.

The slowdown follows from the cell's internal impedance. The voltage measured at the terminals is the open-circuit voltage (the equilibrium potential set by state of charge) plus the overpotentials needed to push current through the cell. These come from three sources:

- **Ohmic resistance** in the electrolyte, current collectors and contacts, which acts immediately.
- **Charge-transfer resistance** at each electrode surface, where ions shed or regain their solvation shell and cross the interface.
- **Diffusion limits** inside the solid particles. Lithium enters at the particle surface and has to diffuse slowly inward. This produces concentration gradients that behave like a long RC time constant.

At high current these overpotentials are large, so the terminal voltage hits 4.2 V while the open-circuit voltage is still lower and the particle interiors are not yet full. Holding the terminals at 4.2 V lets the current fall to whatever value keeps the total voltage at that limit. As diffusion evens out the concentration gradients and the open-circuit voltage approaches 4.2 V, the voltage margin left to drive current shrinks toward zero, and so does the current. It is the same behavior as charging a capacitor through a resistor to a fixed supply voltage.

The voltage cap is hard because the materials degrade above it. Overcharging the oxide makes its structure unstable and oxidizes the electrolyte. High current into nearly full graphite can also push the graphite surface potential below 0 V versus lithium metal. At that point metallic lithium plates onto the surface instead of intercalating. Plating permanently loses capacity and can grow dendrites that short the cell. Cold temperatures make plating more likely because they slow diffusion. Tapering the current near full is therefore the main protection against both failure modes, not just an efficiency compromise.
