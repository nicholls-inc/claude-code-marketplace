Imagine you want to send a friend a secret message, but the only way to reach them is by shouting across a crowded room where everyone can hear everything you say. How could the two of you possibly agree on a secret that nobody else in the room can work out? It sounds impossible, and for most of history it was, since sharing a secret code meant meeting first to agree on it in private or sending it by a trusted courier. Public-key cryptography, developed in the 1970s, solved the problem with an idea that is surprisingly neat, and you already have almost all the maths you need to follow it.

Start with a simple picture using paint instead of numbers. Suppose you and your friend, whom we will call Bea, agree out loud on a shared starting colour, say yellow, so that everyone in the room hears it and yellow is public. You each then secretly choose a private colour, you picking red and Bea picking blue, and neither of you says so. You each mix your private colour into the yellow and swap the resulting mixtures out loud, you handing over an orange-ish blend and Bea a greenish one, both of which everyone in the room sees. Now you take Bea's greenish mixture and add your own private red to it, while Bea takes your orange-ish mixture and adds her private blue. Both of you end up with exactly the same final colour, because each of you has mixed yellow, red and blue together, just in a different order. Meanwhile, an eavesdropper has only seen the yellow and the two blends, and to reach the final colour they would need to separate a mixture back into its ingredients, which is the hard part, because mixing paint is easy to do but very hard to undo. That one-way quality is the whole secret.

Computers cannot mix paint, but mathematics offers something with the same property: an operation that is easy to do forwards and extremely hard to reverse. The one used in the classic version of this trick is called modular arithmetic, and you have met it every time you read a clock. If it is nine o'clock and you add five hours, you do not get fourteen o'clock but two, because you have wrapped around, and mathematicians say that fourteen and two are the same thing when you count in a loop of twelve, writing it as fourteen mod twelve equals two. All it means is this: divide, and keep only the remainder, so seventeen mod five is two, because seventeen is three fives with two left over. Nothing more is going on than that.

Now consider repeated multiplication. If you take a number and multiply it by itself several times, you get a power, so three to the power of four is three times three times three times three, which is eighty-one, and computing this is easy even for huge numbers because computers have clever shortcuts. But if you do the same thing inside a clock-like loop, something odd happens: take a loop of seventeen, and ask what three to the power of some number leaves as a remainder when divided by seventeen, and the answers jump around in a way that seems to have no pattern at all. Going forwards, from the power to the remainder, is quick, but going backwards is a different story. If I tell you that three to some mystery power leaves a remainder of twelve when divided by seventeen, and ask what the mystery power was, the only general method is to try the powers one at a time until you hit it. With a loop of seventeen that takes a moment, while with a loop that is several hundred digits long it would take longer than the age of the universe, even using every computer on Earth. That is our paint mixing: easy to do, practically impossible to undo.

Here is how you and Bea use it, with small numbers so you can check it by hand. First, you agree publicly on two numbers, a loop size, which we will make seventeen, and a base number, which we will make three; these are the yellow paint, and anyone can know them. Now you each choose a private number, you choosing six and Bea fifteen, and nobody else ever hears these.

Next, you each do the mixing. You calculate three to the power of six, which is seven hundred and twenty-nine, then keep only the remainder after dividing by seventeen: seventeen goes into seven hundred and twenty-nine forty-two times, which is seven hundred and fourteen, leaving fifteen, so your mixture is fifteen and you announce it out loud. Bea calculates three to the power of fifteen, divides by seventeen, and keeps the remainder, which works out to six, and she announces six. Everyone in the room now knows seventeen, three, fifteen and six, which are the two public numbers and the two mixtures.

Now comes the clever finish. You take Bea's announced number, six, and raise it to the power of your own private number, six, which gives forty-six thousand six hundred and fifty-six, and when you divide that by seventeen the remainder is eight. Bea takes your announced number, fifteen, and raises it to the power of her private number, fifteen, which is a very large number, yet the remainder after dividing by seventeen again comes out as eight. You have both arrived at eight without ever saying it aloud, and it works because, underneath, you have both computed three raised to the power of six times fifteen, just by different routes, exactly like mixing the colours in a different order. That shared eight is your secret, and you can now use it as the key to scramble messages between you.

What can the eavesdropper do? They know seventeen, three, fifteen and six, and to find your secret they would have to work out one of your private numbers, which means working out what power of three leaves a remainder of fifteen, or six. With a loop of seventeen they could simply try every possibility, and so can you, which is why real systems never use anything so small; real loops are numbers hundreds of digits long, and then the guessing game becomes hopeless, while the forward calculation stays fast enough for your phone to do in a blink.

It is worth being honest about what is and is not proven. Nobody has mathematically proved that reversing the calculation is impossible, only that people have tried very hard for decades and found no quick method, so the security rests on that stubbornness of the problem. There is also a practical caveat, which is that the method guards against people who only listen: someone who could intercept and replace your messages could pretend to be Bea, so real systems add ways of checking who you are talking to.

Still, the achievement is remarkable, since two strangers shouting across an open room can end up holding the same secret while everyone else holds only clues that lead nowhere. Every time your phone sets up a private connection to a website you have never visited, a version of this paint-mixing trick is quietly happening behind the scenes, using nothing more exotic than remainders on a clock face.
