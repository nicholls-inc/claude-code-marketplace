In the last days of August 1854 the crowded streets of Soho around Broad Street began to lose their residents to cholera at a pace that shocked even a city that had lived with the disease for twenty years, and within about ten days more than five hundred people in an area of a few hundred yards had died, many of them within a day or two of first falling ill, their bodies drained by violent diarrhoea and vomiting until the skin turned blue and cold. Most doctors and officials of the time held that cholera was caused by miasma, a poisonous vapour believed to rise from rotting refuse, damp ground and the general foulness of poor districts, and the remedies that followed from this belief were to scour the streets, burn tar and keep away from bad air. John Snow, a physician who lived a short walk from the afflicted streets, did not accept it. He was best known as a pioneer of anaesthesia who had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during two of her confinements, and he was a sober, teetotal and rather solitary man who had spent years arguing that cholera behaved less like something breathed in than like something swallowed, an illness that attacked the gut and spread through whatever passed from the sick into the water of the healthy.

He had already tested this idea once. During the epidemics of 1848 and 1849 and again in 1853 he had noticed that two water companies supplied houses in the same south London streets, one drawing its water from the Thames where the river was thick with the city's sewage and the other, after 1852, from a cleaner stretch far upstream, and the death registers showed that households on the dirty supply died at many times the rate of their neighbours on the other. Snow regarded this as an experiment that nobody could have designed and nobody could fairly dismiss, yet it had won him few converts, and when the Soho outbreak began he saw a chance to make the case on a smaller and more visible scale.

He obtained the list of the dead from the General Register Office and went from door to door asking the families of the victims where they had fetched their drinking water, and the answers pointed house after house to the pump on Broad Street, a well that people prized for the clear and agreeable taste of its water. Snow marked each death on a map of the district, stacking a short bar at the address of every victim, and the bars piled up around the pump in a pattern that a reader could take in at a glance.

What persuaded him most were the cases that seemed not to fit and then did. A workhouse on Poland Street, hemmed in by the stricken streets, housed more than five hundred inmates and lost only five, and Snow found that it had a well of its own. A brewery nearby, whose men were given beer and drew on their own supply, lost none at all. A widow living in Hampstead, far from the epidemic, died of cholera after a cart had brought her bottles of Broad Street water every day because she had grown fond of its taste. Each of these cases was awkward for a theory of bad air, which should not have spared a brewery or singled out a woman in Hampstead, and easy to explain for a theory of bad water.

On the evening of 7 September Snow laid his findings before the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, and the next day the handle of the pump was removed so that nobody could draw from it. The epidemic was already waning by then, partly because so many residents had fled, and sceptics were quick to say so, but the case did not rest there. Henry Whitehead, a local curate who knew the neighbourhood far better than any outsider and who had begun by thinking Snow wrong, set out to disprove him and ended by supplying the missing piece, tracing the first case to a baby girl at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill with diarrhoea just before the outbreak. The drain beneath that house, when it was dug up, proved to be cracked and leaking within a few feet of the well, so that the washings from her soiled napkins could have seeped straight into the water that half the street was drinking.

The official response was cool, since a committee of inquiry concluded that there was no sufficient reason to believe the pump was to blame, and Snow, who published his map and reasoning in a second edition of his book in 1855, died in 1858 at the age of forty-five without seeing the argument settled. It was settled slowly, by the building of London's great sewers, by the conversion of the statistician William Farr, who had once opposed him, and finally by Robert Koch, who in 1883 identified the bacterium that causes cholera and showed how it travelled in water. Snow never saw the organism and could not have said what it was, but he had worked out from houses, deaths and the taste of a well what it did.
