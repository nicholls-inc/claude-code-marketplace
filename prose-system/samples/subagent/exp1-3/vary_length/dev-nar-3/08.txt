On a mild January day in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy. It was just after noon on the fifteenth, and the temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, a startling warmth after days of bitter cold. Children were playing near the waterfront. Dockworkers ate lunch. Beside them stood a steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly three hundred feet around, owned by the Purity Distilling Company.

It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The sweet, dark syrup was bound for industrial alcohol, and from there, in part, for munitions. The tank had been built fast and cheaply in 1915, its walls thinner than they should have been. Nobody had tested it properly before filling it. Workers noticed it groaned. They saw it leak, brown streaks running down its sides, and neighbors collected the drippings in cups. The company's response was to paint the tank brown, so the stains would not show.

Then, at about 12:30, it failed.

Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rivets popping loose one after another, followed by a deep rumble. The tank split open. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen feet high at its crest, burst outward at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed buildings. It knocked a freight car off its tracks. It tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports, and the girders sagged as a train approached. Firehouse walls collapsed inward. A firefighter was swept out of the station and later found dead.

Cold had made the syrup thick, and thickness made it deadly. People caught in the flood could not swim, and could scarcely move. The molasses clung to their legs and clothes, then slowly set around them. Rescuers waded in, sinking to the knee, and struggled to reach those trapped. Horses thrashed and drowned. Sailors from a nearby ship, the USS Nantucket, rushed in to help, joined by Red Cross workers, police, and soldiers.

Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Some victims were not recovered for days.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats, then covered them with sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown until summer. For decades afterward, residents claimed that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled faintly of molasses.

The lawsuit that followed was one of the earliest large class actions in Massachusetts. Purity Distilling insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank, a plausible claim in an anxious postwar year. But a court-appointed auditor heard from hundreds of witnesses over several years and found otherwise. The tank had been poorly designed, carelessly built, and never adequately tested. The company paid roughly 600,000 dollars in settlements.

The disaster helped change how America builds. Engineers and architects began to face stricter rules, and Massachusetts soon required licensed professionals to sign off on construction plans. In this way, a strange, almost comic catastrophe left a very serious legacy.
