On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Children were playing, dockworkers were eating lunch, and horses were hauling wagons along Commercial Street. Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across, owned by the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a use that had grown more urgent as the nation prepared to go dry under Prohibition and as munitions makers needed the product for their wartime work.

The tank had been built quickly and cheaply in 1915, and it showed signs of trouble from the start. It leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the syrup in cups, and the company responded by painting the tank brown to hide the stains rather than by fixing the seams. Nobody performed a proper stress test, and the man who oversaw the construction had no training as an engineer. A fresh shipment of warm molasses had been pumped in the day before, and the sudden change in temperature may have added pressure to a structure that was already weak.

At about twelve thirty, witnesses heard a low rumble and a series of sharp sounds like machine-gun fire, which were probably the rivets shearing off. Then the tank burst open. A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen feet high at its front, rushed out at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed buildings, bent the supports of the elevated railway, and knocked a firehouse off its foundation. People caught in its path were swept along, struck by debris, or trapped in the thick, sticky mass, and the cold air soon made the syrup even harder to escape.

Rescuers arrived within minutes, among them police officers, sailors from a nearby ship, Red Cross workers, and soldiers. They waded into the mess and tried to pull survivors free, but the molasses clung to everything and slowed every step. The search for victims went on for days. In the end, twenty-one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Horses were killed as well, and the cleanup took weeks, with crews using salt water and sand to scrub the streets. Residents claimed that the harbor ran brown until summer, and that on hot days the neighborhood smelled of molasses for years.

The aftermath proved just as important as the disaster. Hundreds of residents joined in one of the first major class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company argued that anarchists had sabotaged the tank, but an auditor who heard years of testimony rejected that claim and concluded that the structure had been unsafe. The company was ordered to pay damages, and the families of the dead received settlements of roughly seven thousand dollars each. The case helped push states to require that engineers review and approve construction plans, which contributed to the building codes and safety standards we rely on today.
