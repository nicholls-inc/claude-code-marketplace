Every so often, the world's clocks have to pause for a single second. That extra tick is called a leap second, and it exists because two ways of measuring time do not quite agree.

The first way is astronomical. A day is the time the Earth takes to spin once, and for most of history that was how people told the time. The second way is atomic. Since the 1950s, clocks that count the vibrations of atoms have kept time with astonishing steadiness, losing perhaps a second over hundreds of millions of years. The Earth is a far less reliable timekeeper. Tides slowly drag on its spin, and shifts in its molten core, its oceans and its ice caps nudge it this way and that.

In 1972 the world adopted Coordinated Universal Time, which runs on atomic clocks but is kept within a second of the sky. Whenever the two drifted too far apart, officials inserted a leap second, usually at the end of June or December. Clocks counted a sixty first second in the final minute before rolling over to the new day. Twenty seven have been added so far, the most recent at the end of 2016.

So why stop? Mainly because computers dislike them. Most software assumes a minute has exactly sixty seconds, and a sixty first breaks that assumption. Over the years, leap seconds have scrambled airline booking systems, knocked websites offline and caused servers to freeze. Some large technology companies spread the extra second across a whole day instead of inserting it at once, but each firm does it differently, which creates its own confusion. Financial markets, satellite navigation and telecoms networks all depend on timestamps accurate to tiny fractions of a second, and they find the irregularity a constant headache.

There is another twist. In recent years the Earth has begun spinning slightly faster, so the world might one day need to remove a second rather than add one. That has never been tried, and engineers fear it would be far riskier than an addition.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that defines units such as the second, voted to abandon leap seconds by 2035. The decision followed years of debate. Technology companies and many governments were in favour, while some countries, notably Russia, were wary.

What happens next is simple enough. Clocks will run on atomic time without adjustment, and the gap between them and the sun will slowly widen. It is expected to reach a full minute only after a century or more. Experts will then choose a new way of correcting it, perhaps a larger adjustment made rarely, far in the future. For everyday life nothing will change. Sunrise and sunset will not noticeably shift, and nobody will feel the difference. The leap second, a small patch for an imperfect planet, will quietly retire.
