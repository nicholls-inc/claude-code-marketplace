The Broad Street Pump

In the last days of August 1854, cholera came to Soho. It arrived the way it always did. A person who had been fine at breakfast would be seized by violent cramps and diarrhoea by the afternoon, and could be dead by the next morning, the body so drained of water that the skin turned bluish and wrinkled. In the streets around Golden Square, in London's West End, about 127 people died in the first three days. By the time the outbreak burned itself out, more than 600 were dead. Whole households were wiped out. Those who could leave did, and within a week the crowded neighbourhood was half empty.

Most doctors of the day were sure they knew the cause. Cholera, they believed, was spread by "miasma", a poisonous vapour rising from rotting rubbish, sewage and filth. On this view, the disease hung in the air of poor, dirty districts, and the way to prevent it was to clear away the smell.

John Snow thought otherwise. He was a London physician best known for his work with anaesthetics. He had given chloroform to Queen Victoria when she gave birth to Prince Leopold the year before. He had also studied earlier cholera epidemics closely, and in 1849 he had published an argument that cholera was not breathed in at all. The disease attacked the gut, not the lungs, so he reasoned that something must be swallowed. His suspect was drinking water contaminated with sewage from people who were already ill. Few people took the idea seriously. Snow had no microscope image of a germ to show anyone, only a chain of reasoning.

Snow lived a short walk from the outbreak, and he saw it as a chance to test his idea. At that time most households in the area had no running water. People filled buckets at public pumps on street corners, which drew water from shallow wells beneath the pavement. Snow had a hunch about one of them: the pump on Broad Street, which was popular because its water was thought to be clear and pleasant to drink.

He went to the General Register Office and got the list of cholera deaths in the district, with the address of each. Then he walked the streets and knocked on doors. When he plotted the deaths, they gathered tightly around the Broad Street pump. Where a death had happened closer to another pump, he asked questions, and often found that the victim had preferred the Broad Street water anyway, or that a child who died had gone to school near it and drunk from it on the way.

The exceptions mattered as much as the pattern. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street held more than 500 people, yet very few of them died. It had its own well. A brewery on Broad Street itself employed about seventy men, and none of them caught the disease. The workers were given a daily allowance of beer, and the brewery had its own water supply, so they rarely touched the pump. If foul air were to blame, these people should have suffered along with their neighbours. They breathed the same air but drank different water.

The most striking case came from Hampstead, miles away. A widow there named Susannah Eley died of cholera, even though there was no outbreak in her area. Snow found out that she had once lived near Broad Street and liked its water so much that her sons had a bottle of it carried to her by cart every day. Her niece, who visited and drank from the same bottle, died too, back home in Islington.

On 7 September Snow put his evidence before the local parish authorities, the Board of Guardians of St James's. They were doubtful, but they agreed to act. The next day the handle was taken off the Broad Street pump, so nobody could draw water from it.

The famous image of that moment, a single action stopping an epidemic in its tracks, is not quite accurate. New cases were already falling by then, partly because so many residents had fled or died. Snow himself admitted this. Removing the handle may still have prevented a second wave, and the evidence he had gathered remained solid.

A local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, at first set out to prove Snow wrong. He knew the parish families personally, and he began his own investigation. Instead of disproving Snow, he found what was probably the starting point. At 40 Broad Street, just feet from the pump, a baby had fallen ill shortly before the outbreak. Her mother had rinsed the child's soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspit, a pit for household waste, that sat close to the well. When the cesspit was dug up, its brickwork turned out to be crumbling, and its contents had been leaking into the drinking water.

Snow published his findings, including a now-famous map marking each death as a small black bar beside the street pumps. Even so, the official inquiry was not fully persuaded, and the pump handle was soon put back. Snow died in 1858, before his ideas were widely accepted. It took several more decades, and the work of scientists who identified the bacterium that causes cholera, for the world to accept that he had been right. Today the Broad Street investigation is often treated as the founding story of epidemiology, the study of how diseases spread through populations. Snow did not prove his case in a laboratory. He did it by counting the dead, walking the streets, and asking people where they got their water.
