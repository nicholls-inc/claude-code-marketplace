On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unseasonably warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit after several bitterly cold days, and the neighborhood's mostly Italian and Irish working families were going about their lunchtime routines. Towering over Commercial Street stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet high and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, a thick syrup shipped up from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol and rum.

The tank had never been trusted. Built hastily in 1915 to capitalize on wartime demand, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in pails. Rather than reinforce the structure, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. It was never properly tested, and a few days earlier a fresh shipment of warm molasses had been pumped into it, adding pressure and stress to the aging seams.

At about 12:40 in the afternoon, witnesses heard a low rumble, followed by a sound some compared to a machine gun as rivets shot loose from the steel. Then the tank ruptured. A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged outward at roughly thirty-five miles per hour. It carried enough force to buckle the elevated railway's supports, knock a train off its tracks, and shove a nearby firehouse off its foundation. Buildings splintered. Wagons, horses, and people were swept up in a sticky tide that rose to waist and shoulder height in the streets.

The cold made matters worse. As the syrup cooled, it thickened, becoming a trap that held victims in place as surely as concrete. Rescuers, including sailors from the USS Nantucket docked nearby, along with police, Red Cross workers, and Boy Scouts, waded in to pull survivors free. It was grueling work. Doctors reported that the molasses clung to every limb and filled the lungs and mouths of the injured. Horses, unable to escape, had to be shot. Cleanup took weeks. Crews washed the streets with salt water from fireboats and covered the ground with sand and sawdust, yet the harbor ran brown into the summer, and locals swore the area smelled of molasses for decades.

Twenty-one people died, and around 150 were injured. Among the dead were children, dock workers, and a city employee eating his lunch. The aftermath turned into one of the longest legal battles in Massachusetts history. Purity Distilling blamed anarchists, claiming a bomb had destroyed the tank. But an auditor's exhaustive review, which heard from more than 3,000 witnesses, concluded that the tank was structurally unsound and that the company had neglected basic safety. In 1925, the company was ordered to pay damages to victims and families.

The disaster left a lasting mark on American law. It helped push for stricter building codes and requirements that engineers sign off on construction plans, a milestone in modern regulation.
