# How Two Strangers Can Agree on a Secret in Public

## The problem

Imagine Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret number, which they can later use as a password to scramble their messages. The only way to communicate is a line that anyone can listen to, including an eavesdropper called Eve. Everything Alice and Bob say, Eve hears.

This sounds impossible. If Eve hears everything, how can anything stay secret? For most of history it was impossible. Secret messages needed a shared key, and the key had to be handed over in person, or carried by a trusted courier. In the 1970s, researchers found a way around this. The trick is a kind of maths that is easy to do in one direction and extremely hard to undo.

## The paint analogy

Start with colours instead of numbers. Suppose Alice and Bob publicly agree on a starting colour, say yellow. Each then picks a private colour that they never reveal. Alice picks red and Bob picks blue.

1. Alice mixes her red into the yellow and sends the resulting orange mixture to Bob.
2. Bob mixes his blue into the yellow and sends the resulting green mixture to Alice.
3. Alice adds her private red to Bob's green mixture. Bob adds his private blue to Alice's orange mixture.

Both now hold a mixture of yellow, red and blue, in the same proportions. The two final colours are identical. Eve saw yellow, orange and green go past. But she cannot unmix paint. To get the final colour she would need either Alice's red or Bob's blue, and mixed paint will not give them up.

The two key ideas are in that story. First, mixing is easy but unmixing is practically impossible. Second, the order of mixing does not matter. Red-then-blue gives the same result as blue-then-red. Real cryptography replaces paint with arithmetic that behaves the same way.

## The arithmetic version: clock maths

You already know "clock arithmetic". On a 12-hour clock, 9 o'clock plus 5 hours is 2 o'clock, because you wrap around after 12. Mathematicians call this working "modulo 12", and the leftover after wrapping is called the remainder. For example, 17 divided by 12 leaves a remainder of 5.

We can use a clock with a different number of positions. Let us use a clock with 23 positions, numbered 0 to 22. Whenever a number goes past 22, we divide by 23 and keep only the remainder.

Now consider repeated multiplication. Take the number 5 and multiply it by itself several times, keeping only remainders on our 23-position clock each time. Mathematicians write "5 to the power of 6" for 5 multiplied by itself six times. Here is a short run of this:

- 5 to the power of 1 is 5.
- 5 to the power of 2 is 25, which wraps to 2.
- 5 to the power of 3 is 2 × 5 = 10.
- 5 to the power of 4 is 10 × 5 = 50, which wraps to 4.

The results jump around with no obvious pattern. That jumbling is what makes this useful.

## The easy direction and the hard direction

Here is the key asymmetry. If I tell you to work out 5 to the power of 6 on the 23-clock, you can do it in a minute. But suppose I say, "5 to the power of some secret number gives 8 on the 23-clock. What is the secret number?" You would have to try the powers one by one: 1, 2, 3, and so on, until 8 turns up. With a clock this small, that is quick. (The answer is 6.)

Now make the clock enormous. Real systems use a clock with a number of positions that is hundreds of digits long. Calculating the forward direction is still fast, because computers have clever shortcuts. But working backwards means searching through an astronomically large number of possibilities. No known method does it quickly, and even the fastest computers would need longer than the age of the universe.

So we have our "mixing that cannot be unmixed": raising to a power on a big clock is easy, and finding the power from the answer is not.

## The exchange, step by step

Here are the numbers. These are tiny so you can check them by hand, though real ones are vastly bigger.

**Public agreement.** Alice and Bob announce, openly, the clock size 23 and the starting number 5. Eve hears both. This plays the role of the shared yellow paint.

**Private choices.** Alice secretly chooses the number 6. Bob secretly chooses the number 15. They never say these out loud. These are the red and blue paint.

**Mixing.**
- Alice works out 5 to the power of 6 on the clock. The answer is 8. She sends 8 to Bob.
- Bob works out 5 to the power of 15 on the clock. The answer is 19. He sends 19 to Alice.

Eve hears 8 and 19. These are the orange and green mixtures.

**Finishing.**
- Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power, 6. On the 23-clock, 19 to the power of 6 comes to 2.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power, 15. On the 23-clock, 8 to the power of 15 also comes to 2.

Both arrive at 2. That is their shared secret, and they can use it as a password.

## Why both get the same answer

This is the "order does not matter" idea. Alice's final number is (5 to the power of 15) raised to the power of 6. Bob's is (5 to the power of 6) raised to the power of 15. In both cases, 5 has been multiplied by itself 6 × 15 = 90 times in total. Multiplying 6 times and then 15 times is the same as 15 times and then 6 times, so the answers match. Clock wrapping does not disturb this, because wrapping at each step gives the same remainder as wrapping at the very end.

## Why Eve is stuck

What does Eve know? She knows the clock size (23), the start (5), and the two mixtures (8 and 19). What she lacks are the secret powers 6 and 15. To get the shared secret, she needs one of them.

With a 23-position clock, she could just try every power until she finds one that turns 5 into 8, and then finish the calculation. That is why such a small clock is only for illustration. With a clock hundreds of digits long, the trial-and-error route is hopeless. Alice and Bob's own calculations stay quick, while Eve's task becomes unmanageable. The gap between "easy for the people who know a secret" and "impossibly hard for everyone else" is what the whole scheme relies on.

## Where "public key" comes in

The method above is usually called key exchange. It is closely related to what is called public-key cryptography. In that setting, each person has a pair of numbers: a private one they keep, and a public one they share with everyone. Bob's 19 above is like a public number, and his 15 is the matching private one. Anyone can use Bob's public number to prepare a message or a shared secret meant for him, but only someone holding his private number can complete the job. Publishing one half of the pair does not give away the other half, for the same reason Eve cannot recover 15 from 19.

This is what happens behind the padlock symbol in your web browser. Your device and the website have never met, and the connection passes through many machines you do not control. Yet in a fraction of a second the two ends agree on a secret, then use it to scramble everything that follows.

## Two honest caveats

First, the method as described protects against someone who only listens. It does not stop someone who actively pretends to be Bob. If Eve can sit in the middle and carry out one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, she can fool both. Real systems add a way of proving identity, using certificates that vouch for who owns which public number.

Second, the claim that unmixing is "very hard" is a belief based on decades of failed attempts by clever people, not a mathematical proof. Today's clocks are chosen large enough to be safe against known methods. If someone found a fast shortcut, the scheme would need replacing, and researchers are already preparing alternatives for that possibility.

## The takeaway

Two strangers can make a shared secret in public because some maths runs easily forwards and not at all backwards. Each person mixes a private ingredient into something shared and trades the result. Each then mixes in their own private ingredient again. Because the order of mixing does not matter, they end up with the same thing, while an onlooker who saw only the in-between mixtures cannot separate the ingredients.
