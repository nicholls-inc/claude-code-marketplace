The Case for the Joined-Up Hand

Every few years someone asks whether children still need to learn joined-up handwriting. The question sounds modern, but the argument behind it is old: a skill looks out of date, and the time spent on it looks wasted. Adults type, swipe and dictate. Exams are moving onto screens, and in professional life the pen mostly comes out for a signature. Why, then, should a seven-year-old spend afternoons practising the join from "o" to "n"?

I think primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and not out of nostalgia. It is one of the cheapest and most effective tools a primary school has for building fluent writers. Taking it out of the curriculum would save a little time now and cost children's literacy more later. The case rests on three points: what handwriting does for the developing brain and for composition, what joining in particular adds to plain printing, and what we would actually lose if it went.

Handwriting is not just a slower keyboard

The case against joined-up writing usually assumes that handwriting is only a way of getting words out: technology that a better technology has replaced. If that were true, the argument would be over. A child who can type 40 words a minute has no obvious reason to struggle at 15 with a pencil.

That is not how writing works for a child who is still learning to read and spell. Forming a letter by hand is a motor act, a visual act and a linguistic act at once. The child has to recall the letter's shape from memory, plan the strokes, make them, and check the result. Pressing a key needs none of that. The child only has to recognise the right letter among several on offer. Recall and recognition are different cognitive tasks, and recall builds stronger memories. This is the same reason teachers ask pupils to write out spellings rather than pick them from a list.

A sizeable body of research supports this. Studies of young children learning letters have found that those who practise by writing them by hand recognise them better later than those who learn by typing or tracing. Brain-imaging work has found that handwriting engages the areas involved in reading more strongly than typing does. More recent EEG studies, such as those by Audrey van der Meer and colleagues in Norway, report richer patterns of brain connectivity when children and young adults write by hand than when they type. Individual studies can be overstated, and some well-known results, such as the claim that students who take notes on laptops learn less than those who write longhand, have replicated less cleanly than their headlines suggested. But the overall direction of the evidence is consistent. For a young child, writing by hand is part of how literacy is learned, not just a way of displaying it.

Why joining matters

A sceptic might grant all of that and still ask why handwriting has to be joined. Children could learn to print neat separate letters, get the cognitive benefits, and skip the loops.

The answer is fluency. What matters most about handwriting for a primary-age child is not how it looks but whether it has become automatic. Researchers such as Virginia Berninger, who has spent decades studying how children learn to write, have repeatedly found that handwriting fluency predicts the quality and length of children's compositions, especially in the early years. The reason is simple. A child has limited working memory. If forming each letter takes conscious effort, there is less attention left for ideas, sentence structure, vocabulary and spelling. A child who has to think about the pencil cannot fully think about the story.

Joined writing is designed for fluency. Each letter flows into the next, so the hand doesn't lift, reposition and restart up to six times in a single word. Common letter strings such as "ing", "th" and "ough" become single, practised movements, motor chunks that run as smoothly as a pianist's scale. Over time this makes the child faster and, more importantly, makes writing automatic. Many teachers also report that whole-word movement patterns reinforce spelling. The hand remembers how "because" feels, and a misspelling feels wrong as well as looking wrong.

Joining also fixes problems that printing tends to leave in place. Letter reversals such as "b" and "d" are harder to make when each letter starts from the baseline and has a fixed entry stroke. Spacing between words becomes clearer when the letters within a word are visibly connected. Children who never learn to join often end up, by the age of ten or eleven, with print that is slow, laboured and hard to read, just when the volume of written work in school begins to rise sharply.

It matters which style is taught. The old copperplate cursive, full of flourishes and capital letters that look nothing like their printed forms, deserves some of the criticism it gets. But that is not what most schools now teach. England's national curriculum, for example, expects pupils from around Year 3 to use "the diagonal and horizontal strokes needed to join letters", and most schools use a simple, legible style in which joined letters look much like their printed versions. Many children start with letters that have small exit strokes ("flicks") so the move to joining is natural. This is a practical writing system, not an exercise in calligraphy.

The objections, answered

The strongest objection is time. The primary curriculum is crowded, and every hour spent on handwriting is an hour not spent on reading, maths, science or, ironically, typing. That is a real cost. But the time joined handwriting needs is modest: short, frequent practice sessions of ten or fifteen minutes, built into the routine of the early years and largely finished by the end of Key Stage 2. If those sessions produce children who write faster and more freely, they pay back the time many times over in every lesson that involves writing.

A second objection is that the future is digital, so we should teach keyboarding. We should, and the two do not compete. Typing is a valuable skill and schools should teach it properly, which many currently do not. But keyboarding fits best later, once literacy is established, while handwriting supports literacy as it forms. Teaching typing does not require dropping joined writing any more than teaching calculators requires dropping times tables. In each case the older skill is what makes the newer tool useful.

A third objection is about inclusion. Children with dyspraxia, motor difficulties or certain visual impairments can find handwriting, and joined handwriting especially, frustrating. Some will be better served by a keyboard, voice recognition or another approach. That is true, and a good school makes those adjustments. Some children with dyslexia actually find a continuous joined style easier, because it reduces the decisions about where each letter begins. Either way, the right response to children for whom a method doesn't work is to adapt it for them, not to remove it for everyone. We do not stop teaching reading aloud because some children stammer.

Finally, people point out that some countries have moved away from cursive. Finland, often cited as an educational model, stopped requiring joined handwriting in 2016 and put more weight on keyboard skills. Policy has moved the other way too. Several US states, after dropping cursive from their standards, have since brought it back, with California passing such a law in 2023. Neither group of decisions settles the question. They show that the trend is not one-way, and that systems which abandoned the skill have sometimes regretted it.

What would be lost

There is also an argument beyond the cognitive one, and it should be made without embarrassment. A fluent, personal hand is a small but real part of being literate in a full sense. A child who can join letters can read old letters, family recipes, historical documents and the handwritten notes that still pass between people. They can take notes in a meeting without a device, fill in a form, write a card, or jot an idea down in seconds. Handwriting is also one of the few things a child makes that is recognisably their own. That is not a sentimental extra. Children who are proud of their writing tend to write more.

There is a fairness point too. Dropping joined writing from the curriculum would not stop children learning it. Children whose parents value it and have time to teach it would still learn it at home. Children without that support would not. A skill moved from school to home becomes a privilege.

Conclusion

Joined-up handwriting is sometimes treated as a relic, kept in the curriculum by habit and by adults' memories of their own school days. The evidence points another way. Writing by hand helps children learn letters, spellings and words. Joining makes that writing fast and automatic, which frees attention for the thinking that writing is supposed to express. The cost in curriculum time is small, the alternatives can be taught alongside it, and children with genuine difficulties can be supported without abandoning it for everyone.

Schools should teach joined handwriting simply, sensibly and early, and they should teach typing as well. The aim is not pretty copperplate. It is a child who can pick up a pencil and write as fast as they can think. That is still worth teaching.
