Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case against it has a surface appeal, because keyboards dominate adult life and the curriculum is crowded. But the argument for dropping cursive rests on a thin view of what handwriting is for. It treats the skill as a quaint way of getting words onto paper, when it is better understood as a tool for thinking, for fluency and for fairness between children. Taught well and briefly, joined-up writing earns its place in the timetable.

Consider first what the critics get right. Most adults type far more than they write by hand, and children will do the same. Nobody needs a looping signature to apply for a mortgage or to submit an essay, and the ability to produce elegant copperplate is not a measure of intelligence. It is also true that schools have limited hours, and that every minute spent on letter formation is a minute not spent on reading, number or science. Some countries, and some American states at various times, have decided that print and keyboarding are enough. These are serious points, and any defence of cursive has to answer them rather than appeal to nostalgia.

The first answer is that handwriting is not mainly about the final product. It is about what happens in a child's head while the hand moves. Research comparing children who learn letters by writing them with those who learn by typing or tracing has repeatedly found advantages for the writers. Forming a letter by hand demands that the child recall its shape, plan the strokes and coordinate eye and hand, and this appears to strengthen the memory of the letter itself. Studies using brain imaging have found that handwriting engages networks involved in reading more strongly than keyboard input does. None of this proves that cursive in particular is the magic ingredient, but it does show that the physical act of writing is doing cognitive work that a keyboard does not replicate. If that is the case for handwriting generally, then the question becomes which style of handwriting serves children best.

Here the argument for joined-up writing is practical rather than mystical. Print, in which every letter is lifted off the page and placed separately, is slow. Each letter needs its own start and its own finish, so the pen leaves the paper constantly and the writer must reorient before beginning the next shape. Joined writing lets the pen flow through whole words, and a child who has mastered it can write faster and with less physical effort. Speed matters more than it first appears. A child who writes slowly spends so much attention on the mechanics that little is left for the ideas, the spelling and the sentence structure. Teachers see this constantly: a pupil with plenty to say who produces three laboured lines, because the effort of forming each letter has exhausted them. Fluent handwriting frees working memory for composition, and composition is the thing we actually care about.

Joined writing also helps with spelling, which is a quieter benefit but a real one. When letters are connected, the word becomes a single movement with a rhythm, and many teachers report that children absorb common letter strings, such as the endings of words, more securely because the hand learns them as a pattern. Children with persistent spelling difficulties often benefit from this kind of motor memory, and several specialists in dyslexia recommend cursive precisely for that reason. Print can lead to the characteristic reversal of letters like b and d, because the two shapes are mirror images that differ only in which side the stem sits. In a joined script the entry strokes differ, and the confusion is reduced. This is not a universal rule, and some children thrive on print, but it counts against the idea that cursive is merely decorative.

A further point concerns the transition that children make anyway. Most pupils who are taught print eventually develop their own joined style, usually in secondary school and usually without any instruction. They do so because the pressure of note-taking and exams rewards speed, and the result is often a messy hybrid that is hard to read. Leaving this to chance is the real inefficiency. If joining up is going to happen regardless, it is better to teach it deliberately, when children are young and their habits are still forming, than to let each child improvise a script that may be slow, cramped or illegible. A taught style gives every child the same sound foundation, whereas an untaught one favours those who happen to have good fine motor control or a parent who cares about penmanship.

That brings me to fairness. Handwriting is the point at which children from different backgrounds can look very different, and schools are one of the few places where the gap can be narrowed. A child with a tablet at home and a child without one start from unequal places in digital skills, but a pencil and a lined page cost almost nothing. Teaching handwriting systematically gives all children a skill they can use in any classroom or exam, without electricity, without a charged device and without a login. Exams in many countries are still handwritten, and examiners still reward legibility and penalise scripts they cannot read. A pupil who writes quickly and clearly at sixteen has a concrete advantage over one who does not, and that advantage is far easier to build in the early years than to repair later.

There is also the matter of access to the written past. Cursive is the script in which most personal and historical documents were produced, from family letters to wartime diaries to the notes in the margins of archives. A generation that cannot read joined-up writing will find these sources closed to them, or will depend on others to transcribe them. That is not a reason on its own to fill the curriculum with it, since reading cursive is a far smaller task than producing it. But since the two skills are learned together, it is an additional argument in its favour, and one that matters for children who go on to study history, genealogy or literature.

Critics will say that the same time could be spent teaching touch typing, and here I agree that typing deserves a proper place in the primary curriculum. The two are not rivals. Typing is a valuable skill and children should learn it, ideally from around the age of nine or ten when their hands are ready for it. But the evidence suggests that handwriting should come first and that it supports the later skill rather than competing with it. A child who has learned how letters are built and how words flow is better placed to understand what they are typing. The sensible position is that both skills belong in school, in sequence, and that cursive handwriting is the foundation rather than the casualty.

It is worth being honest about how much time this actually needs. The claim that cursive consumes the curriculum is exaggerated. Short, frequent practice sessions, perhaps ten or fifteen minutes a day in the early years, are enough to establish a fluent joined hand by the age of eight or nine. Many schools already do this efficiently, and the sessions can be woven into spelling and phonics work so that they do double duty. What matters is that the teaching is explicit and consistent, with clear models and gentle correction, rather than the haphazard approach that gives handwriting a bad name. The complaint that children find it tedious is partly a complaint about poor teaching, and partly a reminder that mastery of any skill involves some repetition.

We should also resist the assumption that the future will be entirely digital. Technologies change, and the tools that dominate today may not dominate in thirty years. Handwriting, by contrast, has survived for millennia precisely because it is robust, portable and independent of any platform. It remains the quickest way to jot a note, sign a form, annotate a document or work through a problem on paper. There is growing evidence that students who take notes by hand process lectures more deeply than those who type verbatim, because the slower pace forces them to summarise rather than transcribe. Fast, legible handwriting makes that approach workable, whereas slow printing makes it frustrating.

None of this requires that schools demand perfection or punish children for untidy work. The aim should be a comfortable, legible and reasonably quick hand, not an exam in calligraphy. Left-handed children and those with motor difficulties may need adapted approaches, and a few may do better with a simplified or semi-joined style, which is a reasonable accommodation. But the default should be to teach joining, because for the great majority of children it brings real benefits in speed, spelling and confidence.

Dropping cursive would save little and cost more than it seems. It would remove a skill that supports fluent writing, strengthens the link between hand and mind, narrows differences between children and keeps open the written record of the past. The sensible course is to teach it early, teach it well and keep it short, alongside a proper introduction to typing. Joined-up handwriting is not a relic. It is a small, efficient investment that pays back across a child's entire time in school and well beyond it.
