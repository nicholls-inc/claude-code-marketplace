**Apollo 13: The Long Way Home**

By April 1970, a trip to the Moon had started to look routine. Two crews had already landed, and the television networks had moved on. When Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center on April 11, carrying Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise, few Americans were paying close attention. The crew was headed for the Fra Mauro highlands, a hilly region that geologists hoped would hold rock older than anything brought back so far.

Even the crew lineup had changed at the last minute. Ken Mattingly, the original command module pilot, had been exposed to German measles, and doctors worried he would fall ill in flight. Three days before launch, NASA replaced him with his backup, Swigert. Mattingly never caught the disease. He stayed behind in Houston, and the mission would soon need him there.

The launch had its own scare. One of the five engines on the rocket's second stage shut down two minutes early because of violent vibration. The remaining engines burned longer to make up the difference, and the spacecraft reached orbit as planned. After that the flight went smoothly. The crew rode in the command module, named *Odyssey*. Attached to its nose was the lunar module, *Aquarius*, which would carry Lovell and Haise down to the surface.

On the evening of April 13, nearly 56 hours into the flight and about 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew finished a television broadcast that no network carried live. A few minutes later, Mission Control asked Swigert to run a routine task: switch on the fans that stirred the supercooled oxygen in the service module's tanks. He flipped the switch. Seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder.

"Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Lovell repeated it: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first nobody could tell how bad it was. Warning lights came on. Electrical readings dropped. The instruments seemed to contradict each other, and some engineers in Houston suspected faulty sensors. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming away from the spacecraft into space. That gas was their oxygen.

One of the two main oxygen tanks had exploded, and the blast had damaged the other. Those tanks supplied the crew's breathing air. They also fed the fuel cells that combined oxygen and hydrogen to make the command module's electricity and water. Within a couple of hours the oxygen would be gone, and *Odyssey* would have nothing left except a set of small batteries meant only for the last hours of the flight, after the service module was cast off. There would be no Moon landing. The question now was whether the crew could get home alive.

The answer was the lunar module. *Aquarius* had its own oxygen, water, batteries and engine, and it was still undamaged. The crew powered it up as fast as they could. Before shutting down *Odyssey*, they had to copy the command module's navigation alignment into the lunar module's guidance computer, working the numbers by hand. Then they switched off almost everything in *Odyssey* to save its batteries for reentry. The command module, the only part of the spacecraft able to survive the return through the atmosphere, went cold and dark.

*Aquarius* had been designed to keep two men alive for about two days on the lunar surface. Now it had to support three men for about four days. In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his team began rationing every resource on board: power, water, oxygen and time.

The first problem was the route home. Earlier in the flight, Apollo 13 had left its "free-return" path, the trajectory that would have swung it around the Moon and back to Earth on its own. That had been necessary to reach Fra Mauro. Turning around directly was impossible, because the service module's main engine might have been damaged in the blast and nobody trusted it. The crew would have to go around the Moon. A few hours after the explosion, Lovell fired the lunar module's descent engine, the engine meant for landing, to put the spacecraft back on a path that would return to Earth.

On April 15 the crew swung behind the Moon. For a while radio contact was cut off, and they passed about 158 miles above the far side. Because the Moon was near its farthest point from Earth, Apollo 13 set a record that still stands: no humans have ever been farther from home. Haise and Swigert took photographs of the craters below. Lovell, who had already flown around the Moon on Apollo 8, knew he would never walk on it.

Two hours after passing behind the Moon, the crew fired the descent engine again. This burn cut about ten hours from the trip home and moved the landing point to the Pacific Ocean, where the Navy's recovery ships waited. Every hour saved meant less strain on the dwindling supplies.

Then a new danger appeared. With three men breathing inside a craft built for two, carbon dioxide was building up. The lunar module removed it with canisters of lithium hydroxide, but its supply would not last. The command module had plenty of spare canisters. They were square, though, and the lunar module's sockets were round. In Houston, engineers worked out a fix using only items the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard from the flight plan covers, hoses from the spacesuits and a lot of duct tape. They read the instructions up step by step, and the crew built the adapter. The carbon dioxide levels fell.

Life aboard became miserable. To save power, almost all the heaters stayed off, and the cabin dropped to near freezing. Moisture beaded on the walls and the windows. The crew had little water to drink, since water also cooled the equipment. Haise developed a kidney infection and ran a fever. None of them slept much. The spacecraft drifted slightly off course, so the crew had to make another correction burn without using the computer, which had been shut down to save power. Lovell steered by hand while keeping the edge of Earth's shadow lined up in the window, and Swigert timed the burn with a watch. It worked.

Back in Houston, Mattingly and a team of engineers spent long hours in simulators working out how to bring the frozen command module back to life with its limited battery power. No one had ever planned a restart like it. The procedure had to switch systems on in exactly the right order so that the batteries would not run dry before splashdown. The crew copied down the long checklist by hand.

On April 17, a few hours before reentry, the crew cast off the service module. As it drifted away, they saw the damage for the first time. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. An entire panel had been blown off. Because the explosion had happened so close to the heat shield, everyone wondered whether the shield itself was damaged. Nobody could check.

The crew climbed into *Odyssey*, powered it up, and released *Aquarius*, the craft that had kept them alive. Then they began the plunge into the atmosphere. During reentry, the superheated air around a capsule always blocks radio signals for a few minutes. This time the silence lasted longer than expected. In Mission Control, and in living rooms around the world now watching closely, people waited. Then Swigert's voice came through. The parachutes opened, and *Odyssey* splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa. The USS *Iwo Jima* picked up the crew soon afterward.

NASA's investigation traced the explosion to a chain of small mistakes made years earlier. A thermostat switch inside the oxygen tank had never been upgraded to handle a higher ground voltage. During a test before launch, the switch fused shut, and the tank's heaters overheated and damaged the insulation on the wiring inside. When Swigert stirred the tank in flight, a spark from the bare wires set off the fire.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon. NASA came to call it a "successful failure," because the agency had shown what a crew and a ground team could do when nearly everything went wrong. It took three men and thousands of people on the ground, working with the tools they happened to have, to bring the crew home.
