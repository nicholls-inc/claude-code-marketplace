A leap second is a single extra second added to the world's official clock time so that it stays in step with the turning of the Earth. Since 1967 the length of a second has been defined by atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of caesium atoms at 9,192,631,770 cycles per second. These clocks are extremely steady. The Earth is not. It is a slightly uneven spinner, slowed over long periods by the pull of the Moon and nudged from year to year by shifts in the oceans, the atmosphere and its molten core. A day measured by the planet therefore differs a little from a day counted by atoms.

Coordinated Universal Time, the standard that civil clocks follow, was set up in 1972 to keep both in view. It runs on atomic seconds but is kept within 0.9 of a second of the time given by the Earth's rotation. When the gap threatened to exceed that, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service announced a leap second, which was added at the end of 30 June or 31 December. The clock then read 23:59:60 before moving on to midnight. This has been done 27 times, most recently on 31 December 2016. Each one meant the world's clocks ran 37 seconds behind pure atomic time by the end.

The trouble is mainly with computers. Most software assumes that a minute has 60 seconds and that time only moves forward. A 61st second can make systems repeat a timestamp, freeze or record events out of order. Leap seconds cannot be predicted far ahead, because they depend on the Earth's behaviour, so engineers get only a few months' notice. Several websites and airline systems suffered outages around the 2012 leap second. Large technology firms now spread the extra second over many hours in a method called smearing, but this means their clocks briefly disagree with others. Telecommunications, finance and satellite navigation all depend on timing accurate to millionths of a second, and they have found the occasional extra second a source of risk with little benefit.

In November 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, which brings together national governments, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. Until at least 2135 the gap between atomic and Earth-based time will be allowed to grow well beyond one second, and what limit to set afterwards is still to be decided. Some scientists argue that a larger correction could be made less often, perhaps a minute at a time, though no schedule has been agreed. Astronomers who track the sky by the Earth's rotation will need to make their own adjustments. In recent years the Earth has been turning slightly faster, which could call for a first negative leap second, and that prospect has added to the case for ending the practice.
