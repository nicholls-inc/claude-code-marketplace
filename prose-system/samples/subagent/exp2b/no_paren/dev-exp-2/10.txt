A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to the world's official clocks to keep them in step with the turning of the Earth.

To see why it exists, consider that there are two ways to measure time. The first is astronomical. A day is the time the Earth takes to spin once, and for most of history we judged time by the sun's position in the sky. The second is atomic. Since the 1950s, clocks have counted the vibrations of atoms, notably caesium, and they tick with extraordinary steadiness. The most precise of them would lose less than a second over many millions of years.

The trouble is that these two systems drift apart. The Earth is not a perfect timekeeper. Tides caused by the moon create friction that slowly slows the planet's rotation, and earthquakes, shifting ice and movements of the molten core nudge it in unpredictable ways. Over the long run, the average day grows slightly longer than the atomic day.

In 1972, international authorities introduced Coordinated Universal Time, the standard on which everyday clocks are based. It follows the atomic clocks but promises never to stray more than 0.9 seconds from the time set by the Earth's rotation. When the gap approached that limit, a leap second was inserted, usually on the last day of June or December. On those occasions the clock read 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Twenty-seven have been added since 1972, the most recent at the end of 2016.

So why stop? The main reason is that leap seconds cause far more trouble than they solve. Computers assume that every minute has sixty seconds, and an extra one can confuse software. Past leap seconds have knocked websites offline, crashed airline booking systems and caused errors in the networks that run financial markets, where transactions must be time stamped to tiny fractions of a second. Large technology companies came up with their own workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across many hours, which meant different systems disagreed about the exact time.

There is also a surprising twist. In recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the possibility of a negative leap second, in which a second is removed. No one has ever tested that in practice, and engineers fear it could be even more disruptive.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to abandon leap seconds by 2035. The gap between atomic and astronomical time will instead be allowed to grow, and a fix will be considered in the future, perhaps a larger adjustment, such as a leap minute, made once in many decades. For ordinary people nothing will change. The difference amounts to about a minute over a century, far too small to notice in daily life.
