In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell on Soho, a crowded district of London. The disease was cholera. It begins with sudden, violent diarrhea and vomiting, and it can drain the body of fluid so quickly that a healthy person may be dead within a day. In the space of about ten days, hundreds of people living within a few streets of one another died. Many residents fled the area. Shops closed. The dead were carried out in numbers that overwhelmed the neighborhood.

Almost everyone of standing in medicine believed they knew the cause. The dominant idea was miasma, the belief that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting matter, sewage and filth. London certainly stank. The Thames was an open drain, and cesspools lay beneath many houses. The theory seemed to fit what people could smell.

A physician named John Snow was not convinced. Snow was a respected doctor, and he had made his name by studying anesthesia and administering chloroform to patients, including Queen Victoria during childbirth. He lived close to the affected streets. During an earlier cholera epidemic in 1849, he had come to suspect that the illness was not breathed in at all. Cholera, he reasoned, struck the digestive system first. If the sickness began in the gut, then the thing that caused it must be swallowed. He guessed that some invisible agent in contaminated water or food passed from the sick to the healthy. He had published this view in 1849, and almost no one had taken it up.

When the Broad Street outbreak erupted, Snow saw a chance to test his idea. He did not have the germ theory to rely on. The microscopic organism that causes cholera would not be properly identified for decades. What he had was method. He went door to door through the stricken streets, asking who had fallen ill and who had died, and where those people lived. He obtained the official death records from the registrar and compared them with his own findings.

The pattern that emerged was striking. The deaths clustered tightly around one spot, a public water pump on Broad Street. Residents drew their drinking water from it by the bucket. Snow marked the deaths on a map of the district, and the cases gathered like iron filings around a magnet, thickest near the pump.

Snow pressed further, because a cluster alone proves little. He looked for the exceptions, and the exceptions were the most persuasive part of his case. A workhouse near the pump, home to hundreds of poor residents, had remarkably few deaths. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped lightly. The workers there were given free beer, and they drank little water, and the brewery had its own supply too.

Then came the opposite kind of evidence. Snow heard of a widow who lived far from Soho, in Hampstead, and who had died of cholera. She had no apparent link to the outbreak. Investigating, he discovered that she had once lived near Broad Street and liked the taste of the pump's water so much that she had it carried to her home each day. Her niece, who drank it too, also fell ill and died. These stories showed that the water, rather than the neighborhood air, was the thread connecting the victims.

On September 7, Snow presented his evidence to the local Board of Guardians, the officials responsible for the parish. He asked them to remove the handle of the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. The officials were skeptical, but they agreed. The handle was taken off the following day.

The historical record is more complicated than the popular legend suggests. The outbreak had already been fading by the time the handle was removed, partly because so many people had fled and partly because the epidemic was running its course. Removing the handle was therefore not a dramatic cure. Snow himself acknowledged this. The true value of what he had done lay in the investigation.

Later inquiries revealed the source. A clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who had at first doubted Snow's theory, carried out his own detailed investigation of the neighborhood. He traced the beginning of the outbreak to an infant who had fallen ill with cholera in a house at 40 Broad Street. The family's cesspool, it turned out, lay very close to the well, and its walls were decaying. Waste from the sick child had leaked into the water that hundreds of neighbors drank.

The authorities did not immediately accept Snow's conclusions, and an official inquiry rejected his theory. Snow died in 1858, long before his view was widely vindicated. Yet his patient counting, his mapping and his hunt for the exceptions became a foundation for modern epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations. Snow had shown that careful questions can reveal the cause of an epidemic, even before anyone can see the cause itself.
