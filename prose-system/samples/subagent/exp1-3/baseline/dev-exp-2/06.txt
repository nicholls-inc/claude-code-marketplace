**The Leap Second, and Why Time's Keepers Are Giving It Up**

A leap second is a single extra second occasionally slipped into the world's clocks, so that the time they show stays in step with the turning of the Earth.

The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time. Astronomers once defined the second as a fraction of the day, based on the planet's rotation. Since 1967, however, the second has been defined by atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of caesium atoms and are almost unimaginably steady. The trouble is that the Earth is not. Tides, shifting oceans, earthquakes and the slow drag of the Moon make the planet's spin irregular, and over the long run it has tended to slow, so that a day is slightly longer than 86,400 atomic seconds.

Left alone, the two timekeepers would drift apart. To prevent that, international authorities introduced leap seconds in 1972. When the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation threatened to exceed 0.9 of a second, an extra second was added at the end of June or December, so that the clock read 23:59:60 before ticking over to midnight. Twenty-seven have been added so far, the last at the end of 2016.

For ordinary people this is invisible. For computers it is a headache. Software generally assumes that every minute has 60 seconds and that time only moves forward. A minute with 61 seconds can confuse systems, cause timestamps to repeat or run backwards, and trigger crashes. Failures linked to leap seconds have disrupted websites, airline systems and data centres. Technology companies have invented workarounds, such as "smearing" the extra second across many hours, but each firm does it differently, adding fresh inconsistency. Satellite navigation, financial trading and power grids all depend on precisely synchronised clocks, and the irregular insertion of seconds is a risk to each.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to abandon leap seconds by or before 2035. The reasoning was practical: the cost and risk of the adjustments now outweigh the benefit of keeping clocks aligned with the sky to within a second.

There is a twist. In recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, raising the odd prospect of a "negative" leap second, in which a second is removed. That has never been tried, and engineers worry it would break software even more badly than an added one.

Nothing will change overnight. Clocks will be allowed to drift away from solar time by a larger margin, perhaps a minute or more, before any correction is considered, likely decades or a century from now. Sunrise and sunset will not noticeably move. The decision simply acknowledges that, in a world run by machines, a steady clock matters more than a perfectly aligned one.
