On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. James Lovell, the commander, was the most experienced spaceflyer alive, a veteran of two Gemini missions and Apollo 8. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was making his first flight. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only days before, after the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, was exposed to German measles and grounded by flight surgeons. The public had grown a little bored with Moon missions by then. The networks had not even planned to broadcast the crew's television transmissions. That would change within two days.

The first minutes were not entirely smooth. One of the second-stage engines shut down early and the others burned longer to compensate, but the spacecraft reached orbit and was sent on its way toward the Moon. Over the next two days the crew settled into routine. They named their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius. On the evening of April 13, they finished a television broadcast that few networks carried, showing viewers the cramped interior of the lunar module. Mission Control told them to stir the cryogenic tanks aboard the service module, a routine procedure that keeps the supercooled oxygen and hydrogen from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch.

About ninety seconds later, a bang shook the spacecraft. Swigert reported a problem, and Lovell followed with the words that became famous in slightly altered form, saying that Houston, they had a problem. Warning lights flickered, the main electrical buses lost voltage, and the craft's attitude thrusters fired on their own. Looking out of a window, Lovell saw gas venting into space. The cause was an oxygen tank that had ruptured. Years earlier, a thermostat switch inside the tank had been damaged during ground testing, and a later heating cycle had burned away the insulation on the wiring. When Swigert stirred the tank, a spark ignited the Teflon insulation, and the explosion tore a panel off the side of the service module. The second oxygen tank was damaged and quickly leaked away. The fuel cells that produced the ship's electricity and water depended on that oxygen, and so did the air the astronauts breathed.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers watched the numbers fall. At first they tried to save the command module, working through checklists to shut down systems and conserve what power remained. Within an hour it was clear that Odyssey was dying. The only lifeboat was Aquarius, a lunar module designed to keep two men alive for about two days on the Moon, not three men for four days on the way back. The crew moved in, and Swigert and Haise powered up the little craft while Lovell worked out a position fix with the help of the stars, since the guidance system on Odyssey had to be shut down to save battery power.

The first decision was which way home. The spacecraft was on a trajectory that would swing around the Moon and then miss Earth by a wide margin unless something changed. Controllers considered aborting immediately, which would have required using the service module's main engine, now of unknown condition. They chose instead to continue around the Moon and use the lunar module's descent engine, the one meant to land on the surface. About five hours after the explosion, the crew fired that engine to put the ship on a path that would return to Earth in roughly eighty hours. Later, after passing behind the Moon, they fired it again for a longer burn to speed the return, cutting the trip to about two more days and aiming for a Pacific splashdown.

The days that followed were an ordeal of cold, thirst and stale air. With Odyssey's power gone, the spacecraft's temperature dropped until the cabin hovered near the freezing point, and moisture condensed on the walls. The crew wore only the clothes they had on, and Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection, made worse by the cold and by his reduced water intake. The astronauts rationed water to a few ounces a day, since the lunar module's supply was needed to cool its electronics. Sleep was nearly impossible.

The air presented the most dangerous puzzle. The lunar module carried canisters of lithium hydroxide to absorb the carbon dioxide the crew exhaled, but they were sized for two men over a short stay. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's receptacles were round. As carbon dioxide levels crept upward toward the point where the men would grow confused and then incapacitated, engineers on the ground gathered the same materials the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from flight manuals, hoses from spacesuits and a great deal of duct tape. They devised a way to fit a square canister to a round hole and read the instructions to the crew over the radio. Lovell, Swigert and Haise built the contraption themselves, and the carbon dioxide readings began to fall.

Another problem came from the nearly empty batteries of the command module. Engineers needed to write an entirely new procedure for powering Odyssey back up after days of being dormant, using far less electricity than the normal checklist assumed, because the ship's reentry batteries now had to last through the final hours. Working in simulators, with Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, taking a leading part, the team wrote and tested a sequence that brought the systems on in a careful order. They read it up to the crew over several hours.

As the ship neared Earth, the crew made a final course correction by hand, sighting on the terminator, the line between day and night, because no instrument could be trusted. On April 17, they separated the damaged service module and saw for the first time how much had been torn away. An entire side panel was gone, and the equipment inside was mangled. Lovell photographed it and told the ground what he saw. Then they released Aquarius, which had saved them, and it burned up in the atmosphere. Odyssey was powered up with the new procedure, and the crew prepared for the fiery return, with a question hanging over them about whether the heat shield had been damaged by the explosion.

The blackout during reentry lasts about three minutes in a normal flight. This time it ran longer, and the silence stretched on until controllers feared the worst. Then, after a pause that felt endless in Mission Control, Swigert's voice came through the static. Parachutes opened and the capsule dropped into the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima. The crew had been gone for almost six days.

An investigation board later traced the accident to the oxygen tank's history of damage and a chain of overlooked mistakes, and NASA redesigned the tanks, removing the fragile wiring and adding a third. The mission never landed on the Moon, and it is remembered as a successful failure, a flight where ingenuity and teamwork under pressure turned a disaster into a rescue. For Lovell, Swigert and Haise, it was a long way home.
