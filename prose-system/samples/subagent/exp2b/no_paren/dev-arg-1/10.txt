Let Cities Stop Forcing Parking

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet rule shaping what gets built. Before a developer can open a restaurant, a apartment block or a clinic, the zoning code says how many parking spaces must come with it. The numbers look scientific. A restaurant needs one space per hundred square feet. An apartment needs two spaces per unit. A medical office needs five per thousand square feet. Cities should scrap these minimums entirely, and the sooner the better.

Start with where the numbers come from. Most were not derived from careful study of local needs. They were borrowed from a handbook that compiled parking counts at suburban sites with plenty of free land, often a handful of locations, and then treated the peak figure as a universal law. Planners copied neighboring towns, who had copied other towns. The result is a set of mandates that bears little relation to how people actually live in a dense neighborhood with transit, sidewalks and delivery bikes.

The cost of that mistake is large and hidden. A single surface parking space takes roughly three hundred square feet once you count the lanes needed to reach it. A space in a structure can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost far more. Those costs do not vanish. They land in the rent of the tenants and the price of the coffee. A household without a car still pays for the garage, because the developer cannot sell or rent the apartment without it. Minimums force carless people to subsidize drivers, and since car ownership rises with income, they often make poorer residents subsidize richer ones.

Housing is where the damage shows most clearly. Developers who want to build modest apartments on a small lot frequently discover that the parking requirement eats the site. The ramp or the lot leaves room for fewer homes, so the project does not pencil out and nothing gets built at all. Cities then complain about a housing shortage while their own codes forbid the buildings that would ease it. Older neighborhoods built before minimums existed, with narrow lots and corner shops and flats above stores, are often illegal to rebuild today. We have regulated our best urban forms out of existence.

The same logic crushes small business. A family that wants to convert an old storefront into a bakery may be told it needs eight spaces it cannot possibly provide. The shop stays vacant, or a chain with a larger footprint takes the site instead. Whole downtowns have been hollowed out this way, with buildings torn down to make room for lots that sit mostly empty on a Tuesday afternoon, and everything within walking distance pushed farther apart so that driving becomes the only sensible option. A mandate meant to ease congestion ends up creating the trips it was supposed to handle.

Critics raise a fair worry. If cities stop requiring parking, will every street fill with cars and spark neighborhood fights? The answer is that removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it wherever tenants and customers want it, and lenders will often insist on it. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision rather than a legal command. Some projects will build more than the old code demanded, and many will build less. That is exactly the point. The people putting up their own money are better placed to judge demand than a formula from a manual written decades ago.

Where street parking really does get tight, the remedy is pricing, not mandates. Charge a modest fee for curb spaces, let the revenue fund local improvements, and the scramble for free spots fades. Cities that have done this report fewer people circling the block and more turnover in front of shops. Residents can also be given permits for their own street. These tools manage scarcity directly instead of pretending that unlimited free storage for private vehicles is a public duty.

The evidence from places that have acted is encouraging. Buffalo repealed its citywide minimums in 2017 and has seen builders propose projects that would have been impossible before. Minneapolis, Austin and a growing list of other cities have followed, and so have entire states. Reports so far show no collapse into gridlock. Instead they show more homes on tight lots, more reuse of old buildings, and parking that tracks real demand.

There is also a climate and fairness argument that should matter to anyone who wants cities to work for everyone. Paving land for vehicles that sit idle most of the day raises runoff, worsens summer heat and locks in long commutes. Households that choose to live with fewer cars deserve a market that lets them find homes priced accordingly.

Parking is a useful thing, and nothing here says otherwise. But a thing can be useful and still be wrong to compel. We do not require every home to include a bookshelf or every office to contain a bakery. We trust the market to supply them, and we step in only where it truly fails. Parking belongs in the same category. Cities that repeal their minimums are not attacking drivers. They are handing back to builders, shopkeepers and residents a decision that was never the planner's to make.
