A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The reason it exists is that there are two ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. Atomic clocks count seconds with extraordinary steadiness, using the vibrations of atoms, and they would keep perfect time for millions of years. The Earth, by contrast, is a rather unreliable timekeeper. Its spin is slowed gradually by the pull of the Moon's tides, and it also speeds up and slows down in small, unpredictable ways because of earthquakes, shifting ice, ocean currents and movements in its molten core.

Since 1972, the international standard known as Coordinated Universal Time has tried to serve both masters. It ticks at the pace of atomic clocks, but whenever it drifts too far from the time measured by the planet's rotation, the authorities insert an extra second, usually at the end of June or December. On those occasions a clock goes from 23:59:59 to 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Twenty-seven of these seconds have been added so far, the most recent at the end of 2016.

The trouble is that the modern world runs on computers, and computers dislike a minute with sixty-one seconds. Software generally assumes that every minute has exactly sixty, so a leap second can cause systems to freeze, crash or record events out of order. Several well-known outages have been blamed on leap seconds, affecting websites, airline systems and data centres. Large technology companies developed their own workarounds, such as spreading the extra second across the whole day, but because they did not all do it the same way, the fixes themselves created confusion. Financial markets, telecommunications networks, satellite navigation and power grids all depend on clocks that agree to tiny fractions of a second, and an irregular, unannounced insertion is a risk to each of them.

There is also a quirk in how the Earth has recently behaved. Over the past few years it has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second would be removed. That has never been tried, and engineers fear it would be even more disruptive than adding one.

For these reasons, the international body that sets the measurement standards voted in 2022 to stop using leap seconds by or before 2035. After that date the gap between atomic time and the planet's rotation will be allowed to grow to a minute or so before any correction is considered, probably not for a century or more. For ordinary people, nothing will change in daily life. The Sun will still rise on schedule, and the difference will be far too small to notice.
