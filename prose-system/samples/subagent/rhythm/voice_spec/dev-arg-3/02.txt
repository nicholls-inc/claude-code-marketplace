Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. In England the national curriculum has asked for it since 2014. Pupils in Year 2 are expected to start using the strokes that join letters and to learn which letters are better left unjoined. By Years 5 and 6 they are expected to write legibly, fluently and with increasing speed, choosing whether to join particular letters. I think this is the right expectation. In this essay I argue that it earns the classroom time it takes, while admitting that some of the usual claims made for cursive do not hold up well.

The weakest claim is that joined writing is quicker than print. It seems obvious, since the pen stays on the page and there are fewer lifts. The studies that have compared the two have mostly found little difference in speed or legibility once a child is fluent in either style. Anyone who defends joined writing on those grounds is defending it with evidence that does not exist. I do not rely on it. The case I want to make is narrower and, I think, stronger. It is that handwriting still matters in school, that children need to learn to do it well, and that joining is the ordinary way a fluent hand is organised.

Start with the fact that handwriting is still how pupils are judged. In England nearly all GCSE and A-level examinations are written by hand. A typical GCSE English literature paper lasts between one hour forty-five minutes and two hours fifteen minutes, and most of that time is spent writing continuous prose. A sixteen-year-old in that room needs a hand that is fast enough to get their thinking onto the page, and clear enough that an examiner can read it. Nobody acquires that at sixteen. It is built across the primary years, and by the end of Key Stage 2 a child either has a fluent hand or has a slow one that will tire and slow down further under pressure. Primary schools are the only place where it can be taught in a planned way.

A second argument comes from what research does show about handwriting in general. In a series of studies in the 2000s, Virginia Berninger and her colleagues found that children in the middle primary years wrote longer compositions, faster, and with more ideas when they used a pen than when they used a keyboard. In 2012 Karin James and Laura Engelhardt used brain imaging with children who had not yet learned to read. Those who practised letters by hand showed more activity in reading-related areas than those who typed or traced them. In 2014 Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer reported that students who took lecture notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those who typed, though later attempts to repeat the result have given mixed findings. A Norwegian study in 2024 found that students writing by hand showed wider connections between brain regions than students typing, though it involved only a few dozen people.

I should be plain about what these studies do and do not show. None of them compares joined with unjoined handwriting, so they cannot settle the question in this essay. What they support is a more general view, which is that forming letters by hand does something for young learners that pressing keys does not. My own view is that the case for teaching handwriting is therefore well supported, and that the case for teaching it in its joined form follows from how handwriting develops. I put that forward as a view and not as a finding.

The reasoning is as follows. A child who learns to print letters one at a time has to make a fresh decision at the start of every letter. A child who learns to join has a different task, in which each letter ends in a stroke that leads into the next. Words become single movements, and the hand starts to treat a word as a unit. That is how most adults write, and it is why adult handwriting tends to drift toward some form of joining whatever they were taught. Surveys of adult writers generally find that most use a mixture of joined and unjoined letters. Given that children are likely to end up there anyway, it seems better to teach the movement properly than to leave each child to invent a hybrid from nothing.

Teaching it also removes a source of unevenness between pupils. When schools leave the matter open, children with a tidy-handed parent or a patient early teacher end up with an easy, regular script, and others copy whatever is nearest. Handwriting practice at school is one of the few activities in which a plain, taught routine makes a visible difference within a year. A class that spends ten or fifteen minutes a few times a week on letter shapes and joins produces writing that is easier to read and quicker to produce. That helps the weakest writers most, because they are the ones for whom the physical act of writing is still using up effort that ought to go to spelling and ideas.

A different argument is about reading. Joined writing is the script of most handwritten text that children will meet outside school. It appears in teachers' comments on their work, in birthday cards, in notes from grandparents, in recipes, and in historical documents at museums and archives. A child who has only learned print can still read some of it, but often with difficulty. In the United States, the Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out, and a generation of pupils has grown up with little instruction in it. A number of states have since reversed course, and in 2023 California passed a law requiring cursive to be taught in the primary grades. One reason given by supporters was that students could not read the Declaration of Independence in its original form. I would not build a policy on that example alone, but the underlying point is sound. Being unable to read joined script is a small but real limit on what a person can do.

The strongest objection is about time. The school day is already full, and children will spend their adult lives typing. Both points are true. A school that teaches handwriting for twenty minutes a day at the expense of reading would be making a mistake, and no one is proposing that. The amount involved is small. It also does not stand in the way of keyboard skills, which schools should teach as well, and probably should teach more deliberately than most do now. Keyboard fluency is a separate competence and it takes its own practice. Children can learn both, and the evidence above suggests that they do better at writing generally when handwriting comes first.

Another objection is that joining is hard for some children. Children with motor difficulties, and some left-handed children, can find the strokes awkward, and a rigid cursive requirement can make writing a source of distress. This is a fair concern, and the curriculum already allows for it. It does not require every letter to be joined. It asks pupils to learn which letters are best left apart, and it gives the older pupils room to choose. A teacher can adapt the approach for a child who struggles, as with any other skill. What the objection supports is flexible teaching. It does not support dropping the subject for everyone.

There is also a point about dignity that I will state as a view. A child who can write a clear, even line of joined script is often proud of it, and children notice when their writing starts to look like adult writing. It is a small marker of competence that is easy to see and easy to improve. Teachers who have run handwriting sessions in Year 3 or Year 4 will recognise the way a class goes quiet and absorbed. I would not rest the policy on that, but I would not leave it out either.

What I am arguing for is modest. Schools should pick one simple script, with plain joins and without loops or flourishes that make it hard to write. They should teach it from Year 2, practise it briefly and often, and aim for a fluent hand by the age of eleven. After that, pupils should be free to develop a personal style, as every adult does, so long as it stays legible. Schools should not make a ceremony of it, and should not award pen licences or treat a messy hand as a moral failing. The aim is a working skill.

The argument for dropping joined-up handwriting depends mostly on the idea that it is old-fashioned and that screens have replaced it. Screens have not replaced it in examination halls, and the research that exists suggests that the habit of forming letters by hand is useful to young children in ways we do not fully understand. Where the evidence is thin, in the question of joined against unjoined, the balance of practical reasons still favours teaching joining. It is how fluent hands work, it is how most handwriting that children meet is written, and a few hours a term is a small price. Primary schools should keep teaching it.
