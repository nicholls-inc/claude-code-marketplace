On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unseasonably warm afternoon. The temperature had climbed above 40 degrees after days of bitter cold, and residents ate lunch, ran errands, and went about their business along Commercial Street. Overlooking the waterfront stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It was fifty feet tall, nearly ninety feet across, and held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses.

The tank had been built hastily in 1915, to meet the soaring wartime demand for industrial alcohol used in munitions. Its construction was careless. The steel was too thin, the rivets were poorly set, and no one had calculated the stresses the walls would bear. When it was first filled, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups. The company's response was to paint the tank brown to hide the stains. Nobody ran a proper stress test.

Just after 12:30 that afternoon, a shipment of warm molasses had recently arrived and been pumped in on top of the cooler contents. Witnesses later described a deep rumbling, and a sound like a machine gun as rivets shot loose. Then the tank ruptured. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged out at around 35 miles per hour.

The flood destroyed everything in its path. It tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports, lifted a train, and crushed buildings as if they were made of matchsticks. It pushed a firehouse off its foundation. Wagons, horses, and people were swept along or trapped in the thick, sticky mass. As the molasses cooled in the winter air, it grew even more viscous, and the victims could scarcely move. Rescuers waded in waist-deep, while sailors from a nearby ship, the USS Nantucket, joined the effort. Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Among the dead were children, dockworkers, and a Boston Fire Department member.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed salt water from fireboats and covered the streets in sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown until summer, and locals claimed they could smell the sweetness on warm days for decades afterward.

The aftermath proved just as significant as the disaster. Survivors and families sued the company in one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. United States Industrial Alcohol insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank, hoping to blame sabotage for the failure. But an auditor appointed by the court, Hugh Ogden, heard from hundreds of witnesses over three years. He found that the tank had been unsafe from the start. In 1925 the company was ordered to pay damages, and settlements averaged about 7,000 dollars per death.

The disaster also reshaped how America builds. Massachusetts and other states tightened regulations, requiring architects and engineers to certify and sign off on their plans. The flood stands as a stark lesson in what happens when profit outpaces safety.
