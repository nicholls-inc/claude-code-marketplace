**Sharing a secret in public**

**The problem**

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret number, which they can then use as a key to scramble their messages. The only way to talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Eve, an eavesdropper, hears every word they say.

This looks impossible. If a secret is spoken aloud, it is no longer secret. For centuries the only fix was to meet in advance and agree on a code in private. Public-key cryptography, worked out in the 1970s, removed that need. It rests on one idea: some jobs are easy to do and very hard to undo.

**Easy one way, hard the other**

Mix yellow paint with a splash of blue and you get green. Now try to un-mix the green back into the exact yellow and blue. You can't. Mixing is easy, and separating is practically impossible.

Maths has the same kind of one-way job. Multiplying 101 by 103 takes a few seconds by hand and gives 10,403. Now go the other way. What two prime numbers multiply to 10,403? You would have to hunt. With small numbers that is annoying. With numbers hundreds of digits long, the best known methods would take all the world's computers longer than the age of the universe.

**The paint version of the trick**

Here is the whole scheme with paint, before any numbers.

1. Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common starting colour, say yellow. Eve hears this and it doesn't matter.
2. Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red. She mixes it into the yellow and sends the result (orange) to Bob.
3. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, say blue. He mixes it into the yellow and sends the result (green) to Alice.
4. Alice takes Bob's green and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange and adds his secret blue.

Both now hold the same muddy brown, which is yellow, red and blue combined. The order of mixing makes no difference to the final colour.

Eve saw yellow, orange and green. To get brown she would need to know red or blue, and she can't un-mix the orange or the green to find them. Alice and Bob never sent their private colours, and they still ended up with the same result.

**The same trick with numbers**

Real systems use numbers in place of paint. This needs one idea from school maths: a clock.

On a 12-hour clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, not 15. When you go past the top you wrap round and start again. Mathematicians call this "mod". We write 15 mod 12 = 3, which means the remainder when 15 is divided by 12.

We will use a clock with 23 hours instead of 12. Every calculation below wraps at 23.

The public starting number plays the part of yellow. Let it be 5. Everyone, including Eve, knows both the number 5 and the clock size 23.

- Alice picks a secret number, 6. She calculates 5 multiplied by itself 6 times, which is 15,625. Dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8, so she sends **8** to Bob. This is her orange.
- Bob picks a secret number, 15. He calculates 5 multiplied by itself 15 times. The raw figure is enormous, but wrapping at 23 along the way, it comes to 19, so he sends **19** to Alice. This is his green.

Now each combines the other's public number with their own secret.

- Alice takes Bob's 19 and multiplies it by itself 6 times, wrapping at 23. The result is **2**.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and multiplies it by itself 15 times, wrapping at 23. The result is also **2**.

They have the same number, and it never travelled down the line. Eve saw 5, 23, 8 and 19. The shared secret is 2.

**Why does it work?**

Alice's final number is "5 multiplied by itself 15 times, then that multiplied by itself 6 times". Bob's is "5 multiplied by itself 6 times, then that multiplied by itself 15 times". Both amount to 5 multiplied by itself 6 × 15 = 90 times. Swapping the order of the steps changes nothing, just as the paint colours could be added in either order.

**Why can't Eve work it out?**

Eve knows 5, 23, 8 and 19. To find the secret she needs Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. She must answer a question like "5 multiplied by itself how many times gives 8 once wrapped at 23?"

With a clock of 23 she can just try 1, 2, 3 and so on until she hits 8. That takes seconds. Real systems use a clock with hundreds of digits, and the starting number is chosen carefully. Going forward, "multiply by itself many times and wrap" can be done quickly using repeated squaring, so Alice and Bob's work is small. Going backward means searching an astronomically large list of possibilities, and no one has found a shortcut. This is the one-way street: easy to do, impractical to undo.

**What about the "public key" part?**

The scheme above is the best-known way to agree on a secret, called Diffie-Hellman after its inventors. A close cousin, called RSA, is the one most people mean by "public-key cryptography", and it works like a padlock.

Imagine Bob makes thousands of identical open padlocks and posts them in the town square. Anyone can take one, and anyone can snap it shut. But only Bob holds the key that opens it. If Alice wants to send Bob a message, she puts it in a box, snaps one of his padlocks on it, and posts it. Eve can see the box and the padlock, and even has her own supply of Bob's padlocks, but she can't open it.

In real RSA, the "padlock" is a pair of huge numbers that Bob publishes, and the "key" is the two prime numbers that were multiplied together to make one of them. Remember the earlier one-way job: multiplying two primes is easy, and recovering them from the product is practically impossible. Bob knows the primes because he picked them. Everyone else sees only the product. Anyone can use the product to lock a message. Only someone who knows the primes can unlock it.

So Alice can use Bob's padlock to send him a fresh secret number. They have now agreed on a secret without ever meeting, which is exactly the original problem solved a second way.

**Where you meet this every day**

Whenever your browser shows a padlock icon, something like this has just happened. Your device and the website have never met. In the first fraction of a second they swap public numbers, as Alice and Bob did, and both end up holding the same secret. They then use it as the key for a fast, ordinary scrambling method for the rest of the conversation. The expensive one-way maths is used only for the handshake, and everything after that is quick.

**The honest caveats**

Nothing here is proven impossible to break. It is believed to be hard because very clever people have tried for decades and found no fast method. Some future technology, such as large quantum computers, could change that, which is why researchers are already building replacements.

There is also a subtler weakness. The scheme guarantees that Eve can't read the secret. It does not guarantee that Alice is really talking to Bob. If Eve can sit in the middle, pretending to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, she can agree one secret with each of them. Real systems fix this with certificates, which are signed statements from trusted organisations saying "this public number really belongs to this website". That layer is a separate problem, but it is why the padlock icon means more than just scrambled data.

**The essential idea**

Alice and Bob each do something that is easy for them and impossible for an observer to reverse. They publish only the result, and a clever bit of symmetry means they both arrive at the same private answer. Nothing secret ever crosses the line, which is why listening to the whole conversation does not help.
