In the last days of August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district of London whose narrow streets and courts held tradesmen, laborers, and families living several to a room, and within about ten days some five hundred people living within a few hundred yards of the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street had died, many of them within a day or two of first falling ill. Cholera in that era was a terror that arrived without explanation: a healthy person developed violent vomiting and diarrhea, lost so much fluid that the skin turned blue and shriveled, and was often dead by the next morning. Most physicians and officials held that such diseases spread through miasma, a poisonous vapor rising from rotting refuse, marshes, and the general filth of poor neighborhoods, and the remedies they proposed were accordingly aimed at the air.

John Snow, a physician living nearby on Sackville Street, believed otherwise and had believed so for years. Already known for his work in anesthesia, which had made him the man who administered chloroform to Queen Victoria during two of her confinements, he had argued in a pamphlet published in 1849 that cholera was something swallowed, that it multiplied in the intestines of the sick, and that it passed into the drinking water when the waste of one victim found its way into the supply that others used. Few had been persuaded, partly because he could not show the agent itself, since no one yet knew that a bacterium caused the disease, and partly because his idea carried an uncomfortable implication about how a city handled its sewage and its wells.

When the Soho outbreak began, Snow set out to test his idea on the ground, going door to door and obtaining from the registrar the names and addresses of the dead, then asking the families what they had drunk. The pattern that emerged was that the deaths clustered around the pump on Broad Street, a well that many in the neighborhood used and that was reputed to give particularly clean, pleasant water. Snow marked each death on a map of the district with a small black bar stacked against the house where it occurred, and the bars piled thickly near the pump and thinned with distance, though he was careful to note that people living closer to other pumps sometimes went out of their way to fetch water from Broad Street because they preferred its taste.

The exceptions carried much of the weight of his case. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed more than five hundred inmates yet suffered only a handful of deaths, and Snow learned that it had its own well and never needed the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street itself lost none of its workers, who were allowed malt liquor and in any case had a well on the premises. Most striking was the story of a widow in Hampstead, miles from Soho, who died of cholera along with her niece after a cart brought her bottles of water from the Broad Street pump every day because she had grown fond of its flavor, and no one else in her suburb fell ill.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow took his findings to the Board of Guardians of St. James's parish, and the next day they ordered the handle removed from the pump so that no one could draw water from it. The epidemic was already subsiding by then, as many residents had fled the neighborhood and the pool of susceptible people had shrunk, which later gave critics the argument that the removal accomplished nothing, though Snow never claimed that it had ended the outbreak and presented the evidence as bearing on how the disease spread. What the removal did accomplish was to stop the pump from supplying any further victims while the cause was still being sorted out.

The most important contribution to the explanation came from a local curate, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, who began as a skeptic and who knew the neighborhood and its people far better than Snow did. Whitehead's inquiries turned up the first case, an infant girl at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill with diarrhea a few days before the outbreak exploded, and whose mother had rinsed the child's soiled linens and emptied the water into a cesspool beside the house. When the ground was dug up, the cesspool proved to be leaking and lay only a few feet from the well, so that the infant's waste had plausibly seeped into the water that hundreds of neighbors then drank.

Snow had also already carried out a larger comparison that he considered even more decisive, one that ran across south London, where two water companies supplied customers on the same streets, sometimes serving adjacent houses, and where one company, the Lambeth, had recently moved its intake upstream of the city's sewage on the Thames while the other, the Southwark and Vauxhall, continued to draw from the polluted stretch. The death rate in houses supplied by the downstream company was around eight times that in houses supplied by the upstream one, among people who otherwise lived in the same conditions.

The authorities were slow to accept the conclusion, and an official inquiry that year found no sufficient reason to blame the water, but the evidence persisted and the pump handle became a symbol of an argument that public health officers eventually won, with Snow himself dying in 1858 before most of the world had come round to his view.
