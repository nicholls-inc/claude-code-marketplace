A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to the world's official clocks every so often, and the international bodies that set the time have agreed to stop adding them.

To see why leap seconds exist, you have to know that there are two ways of measuring a day. For most of history, a day was simply the time the Earth takes to spin once, judged by the position of the Sun. In the 1950s scientists built atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of atoms and keep time with astonishing steadiness. These clocks do not care what the planet is doing. The Earth, it turns out, is a less reliable timekeeper. Tides, the churning of its molten core and the movement of ice and oceans all nudge its rotation, and over the long run the spin has been slowing slightly. A day is now a little longer than the 86,400 seconds that atomic clocks count out.

Left alone, the two systems would drift apart. Noon on the clock would eventually fall at the wrong moment for the Sun. So in 1972 the world adopted a compromise. Coordinated Universal Time, the standard behind civil clocks everywhere, would follow the atomic clocks but be nudged by a leap second whenever the gap with the Earth's rotation grew near one second. Usually this happens on the last day of June or December, when a clock shows 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Since 1972 there have been 27 of them, the most recent at the end of 2016.

The trouble is that the modern world runs on computers, and computers dislike a minute with 61 seconds. Many systems assume time only ever moves forward in neat steps of 60. A repeated or extra second can confuse software, scramble the order of records and, in past cases, crash websites and disrupt airline and financial systems. Technology companies have had to invent workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across many hours, and not everyone does it the same way. Because leap seconds cannot be predicted far ahead, being announced only months in advance, the risk of error is hard to manage.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the official definition of time, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. The gap between atomic time and the Sun will then be allowed to grow for decades, perhaps a minute or more, before a fix is considered. A curious recent twist has helped the argument. The Earth has lately been spinning slightly faster, which could one day call for a negative leap second, removing a second. That has never been tried, and engineers fear it would be even riskier.

For ordinary people nothing will change in daily life. The Sun will stay in step with the clocks well beyond our lifetimes.
