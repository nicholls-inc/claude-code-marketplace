Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. The case against it has become fashionable, and it is not foolish, but it rests on a narrow view of what handwriting is for. If we think of it only as a way of getting words onto a page, then keyboards do the job faster and more legibly, and cursive looks like a relic. If we think of it as a skill that shapes how children read, spell, think and compose, then the argument looks very different. A fluent, joined hand is still one of the most useful things a child can leave primary school with, and teaching it well costs far less than its critics suggest.

Start with the objection that handwriting is obsolete. Adults do type most of what they write, and children will too. But the conclusion that children therefore need not learn to write by hand does not follow, any more than the existence of calculators means children need not learn arithmetic. Primary school is not job training for the average office worker of 2040. It is where children build the underlying machinery that later learning depends on. Handwriting belongs to that machinery because it trains fine motor control, visual attention and the link between sound and symbol in a way that tapping a key does not. Every letter on a keyboard is produced by the same small movement of a finger, whereas every letter on a page demands its own distinct shape, formed from memory. That difference matters for learning.

The research here is not unanimous, and honesty requires saying so. Some of the more striking claims, such as the idea that handwriting makes children smarter in a general sense, have been oversold. Still, a body of work comparing children who learn letters by writing them with those who learn them by typing or tracing has found real advantages for the writers. Children who form letters by hand tend to recognise them more reliably and show stronger activity in the brain regions associated with reading. Studies of note-taking in older students suggest that writing by hand pushes people to summarise rather than transcribe, and that this can improve understanding and recall. None of this proves that cursive in particular is the magic ingredient. It does show that the physical act of making letters is doing cognitive work, and that we should be wary of discarding it on the grounds that it feels old-fashioned.

That brings us to the more specific question of why the letters should be joined. A reasonable sceptic could accept that children need to write by hand and still ask why they should not simply print. Print is clear, it matches the letters in their reading books, and some countries have moved toward it. The answer lies in what joining does to the movement of the hand. When letters are written one at a time, the pen lifts after every stroke, and the writer must reposition and restart for each character. Joining makes the writing continuous, so that a word is produced as a single flowing movement rather than a series of separate stamps. The rhythm that results helps children to think of a word as a unit rather than a pile of letters, which supports spelling, since the hand begins to remember common sequences such as the ending of a word or the pairing of vowels. Teachers of children with spelling difficulties have long noticed this, and many specialist programmes for dyslexic pupils use joined writing for exactly that reason.

Speed is the second practical argument. A child who can only print will, as the demands of school increase, hit a ceiling. Written work grows longer through the upper years of primary and into secondary school, and examinations require extended writing under time pressure. A joined hand, once it is fluent, is quicker, because the pen travels less and stops less. Children who never develop that fluency often write slowly, tire quickly, and make their work shorter and less ambitious than their ideas deserve. This is an uncomfortable truth, because it means that handwriting difficulties quietly limit the quality of what children can express. When a child struggles to form letters, much of their attention goes to the mechanics, and there is less left over for choosing words and organising an argument. Fluency frees up that attention, and joined writing is the most dependable route to it that schools have.

There is also the question of legibility, which critics sometimes assume works against cursive. It is true that careless joined writing can be a mess, and anyone who has tried to read a doctor's note knows it. But the problem there is poor teaching or poor habits, not the style itself. Taught properly, with consistent letter shapes and sensible joins, cursive is no harder to read than print, and when it becomes quick it often stays neater than rushed print, which tends to collapse into a mixture of capitals, lower-case forms and half-formed letters. A consistent system, practised regularly in the early years, produces handwriting that holds up better under pressure. The decline in legibility that worries many teachers is more often a sign that handwriting has been squeezed out of a crowded timetable than evidence that the system has failed.

Equity should also weigh in this debate. Children from homes with plenty of books, devices and quiet time to practise will pick up many skills regardless of what school does. Children from less advantaged homes depend more heavily on what happens in the classroom. If schools stop teaching handwriting systematically, the children most likely to be left without a fluent hand are those whose families cannot fill the gap with private tuition or extra practice. A pen and paper are the cheapest tools in education, and they work without a charger, a login or a wireless connection. Teaching every child to write well with them is a modest but real way of narrowing the gap.

The strongest counterargument is about time. The primary curriculum is full, and every minute spent on handwriting is a minute taken from something else. That is a fair point, and I do not think handwriting should swallow hours. But the time needed is smaller than it sounds. Short, daily, well-structured practice sessions, in the style of a few minutes of focused letter formation, are more effective than long and occasional lessons. Once a child has learned the basic shapes and joins, the skill is maintained through ordinary writing across the curriculum, so the cost falls mostly in the first few years. Set against the benefits to spelling, speed and confidence in every subject that follows, it is an efficient investment. Few other classroom activities pay back so widely for so little.

A second counterargument is that cursive is a cultural affectation and children may as well learn something more modern. Here I would say that the argument from tradition is not the one I am making, though tradition is not worthless either. Handwriting connects children to a vast written inheritance, from family letters to historical documents, and a child who cannot read joined writing is shut out of much of it. A grandparent's birthday card, a note from a teacher, or an archive of original sources all become harder to approach. That is a small loss for any individual, but a real one, and it is easily avoided. Reading cursive is much easier for a child who has learned to write it.

None of this requires hostility to technology. Children should learn to type, and they should do so early enough to be competent by the time they reach secondary school. The two skills are complements rather than rivals, and the sensible position is that schools should teach both. The point is only that typing should be added to handwriting rather than substituted for it, because the evidence suggests that the foundation laid by writing by hand supports what comes after. A child who has learned to form letters carefully, to join them smoothly, and to write at a comfortable speed is better placed to use a keyboard well, since they already understand how letters and words are built.

It is worth adding that children often enjoy learning to write in a joined hand. There is pride in a first signature, in a neat page, in the sense of having mastered something that looks grown-up. That motivation is not trivial in the early years, when confidence shapes everything. A child who feels capable as a writer is more likely to write more, and to write more willingly, and that volume of practice is what builds a good writer in the end.

So the case for teaching joined-up handwriting in primary schools is a practical one. It supports fluency, spelling and stamina, it helps those with the least support at home, and it costs little time when taught well. The fact that adults now spend much of their working lives at keyboards is a reason to teach typing as well, not a reason to abandon the pen. Schools should keep teaching children to join their letters, and they should do it properly, early and with confidence that it is still worth doing.
