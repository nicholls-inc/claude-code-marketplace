Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it with more confidence than many of them currently do. The case for dropping it has become fashionable, and it rests on an appealing but shallow argument: that children will spend their adult lives typing, that cursive is a relic like the quill, and that the hours spent on looped letters could be better used elsewhere. Each part of that argument contains something true, and each fails when examined closely. Joined-up writing is not a nostalgic ornament. It is a practical, cognitively useful skill that serves children now, and it costs far less than its critics suppose.

Begin with the claim that nobody writes by hand any more. This is simply false for the people we are actually discussing. Children in primary school write by hand constantly. They copy, annotate, label diagrams, answer questions, take spelling tests and, in most education systems, sit examinations on paper well into their teens. In England, for instance, the great majority of high-stakes assessments at sixteen and eighteen are still handwritten, and a pupil who cannot write quickly and legibly is at a real disadvantage in them. The question is therefore not whether handwriting will matter, but whether the child will do it well or badly. A school that withdraws instruction in the fastest, most fluent form of handwriting does not abolish the activity. It leaves children to improvise their own methods, and improvisation is how you get the cramped, slow, half-printed, half-joined scrawl that teachers know all too well from the exam hall.

Speed is the first practical argument. Printing requires the pen to leave the paper after every letter. Each lift is a small interruption, and across a thousand-word essay those interruptions add up. Joined writing allows whole words to be produced in a single movement, which is why adults who develop a fluent personal hand almost always end up joining at least some of their letters. Research on handwriting in the early secondary years has repeatedly found that fluency is associated with the quality and quantity of what pupils write, not merely with how neat it looks. This is not mysterious. A child whose hand is labouring has less attention left for ideas, spelling and sentence construction. Automaticity in the mechanical task frees working memory for the creative one. Anyone who has watched a struggling writer stop after every word to form the next letter has seen the problem directly.

The second argument concerns the brain, and here I want to be careful not to overclaim, because the neuroscience is sometimes stretched beyond what it can support. It would be silly to say that cursive makes children cleverer. What the evidence does suggest is that handwriting in general, as opposed to typing, engages the brain differently. Studies using brain imaging and behavioural measures have found that forming letters by hand is associated with stronger letter recognition in young children than typing or tracing, and that students who take notes by hand often process and retain material better than those who transcribe on a keyboard, because the slowness forces them to select and summarise. Joined writing sits within that tradition. It asks for continuous, rhythmic motor planning, and it ties the shape of a whole word to a single gesture. Whether it has some unique advantage over print is still debated, and honest advocates should concede that. But the burden of proof is oddly reversed in the current argument. We are being asked to remove a long-established practice on the grounds that its special benefits are unproven, while nobody has shown that dropping it produces any benefit at all.

That brings me to the supposed opportunity cost. The claim is that time spent on handwriting is time taken from coding, from keyboard skills, or from richer literacy work. But the time involved is modest. Children who are taught joins systematically, in short and frequent sessions, can acquire a fluent joined hand within a year or two of starting. Fifteen minutes a few times a week is not a curriculum-wrecking commitment. More to the point, handwriting practice is not separate from literacy but part of it. A child who is learning to join the letters in a word is also attending to how that word is spelled, which letter patterns recur, and how common endings such as ing and ed behave. Teachers who use joined handwriting to reinforce spelling patterns are getting two lessons for the price of one. The same cannot be said for most of the alternatives proposed to fill the gap.

There is also a question of equity that the abolitionists tend to overlook. A fluent hand is not equally distributed. Children from homes full of books, pens and patient adults pick up good habits almost regardless of what the school does. Children without those advantages depend on the classroom. If schools stop teaching a formal joined style, the pupils who lose out will be the ones whose families cannot compensate, and the gap will show up where it hurts, in timed written exams and in the first impression made by a piece of written work. It may seem unfair that legibility is judged, but it is. Markers are human, and a script that is slow, messy and hard to read costs marks. Teaching every child a clear, efficient hand is a modest and egalitarian way of removing that penalty.

Some children, moreover, find joined writing helpful for specific reasons. Many teachers report that pupils with dyslexia and related difficulties benefit from the continuous flow of cursive, because it reduces the reversal of letters such as b and d, which look alike in print but have quite different movements when joined, and because it gives a word a distinct shape and rhythm. Again, the research here is not decisive, and I would not claim cursive as a cure. But it is telling that the practice has long been recommended in specialist teaching, and that it can do no harm to the rest of the class.

The strongest objection deserves a direct answer. It says that typing is the future, so children should learn to touch-type early and be done with it. I agree that keyboard skills should be taught, and taught well. Nothing in my argument requires a choice between the two. The mistake is to treat them as rivals when they serve different purposes. Typing is superb for producing long, revisable text. Handwriting is unbeatable for quick notes, for exams, for annotating, for filling in forms, for thinking on paper, for the thousand small moments when a device is absent, inappropriate or simply slower. Literate adults routinely use both. We do not abolish mental arithmetic because calculators exist, nor stop teaching children to read maps because phones can navigate. We teach the basic skill because it underlies fluency and independence, and because tools fail, run out of battery or are banned from the room.

A related objection is that cursive is hard to read and that print is clearer. This confuses the formal model with the finished product. Nobody is asking that children be drilled in the looping, ornamented scripts of a Victorian copybook. The modern approach is to teach a simple, mostly unadorned joined style with entry and exit strokes, so that print letters flow naturally into one another. Most adults end up with a personal hybrid, and that is fine. The point of teaching joins is to give children a sound foundation from which to develop a hand that is fast and legible, not to enforce a single approved script. Where schools have done this sensibly, the result is not unreadable writing but more consistent writing.

There is a final consideration that is harder to measure but easy to feel, and that is the matter of signature and self. Handwriting is one of the few skills that is visibly, personally ours. A child who has learned to write in a flowing hand can sign her name, write a card to a grandparent, read a letter from one, and decipher the handwritten notes that fill archives, family papers and old recipe books. A generation that cannot read joined-up writing is cut off from a large part of its own written heritage. That may sound like sentiment, but it is a real loss, and it is one we would be inflicting casually, in the name of an efficiency that has not been demonstrated.

None of this means that handwriting instruction should be rigid or dogmatic. It should begin with clear print letters in the early years, move to joins once the child's hand is secure, and be taught briefly, regularly and with attention to posture, grip and comfort. Left-handers need particular care. Children with motor difficulties should be given alternatives and support, not pushed to the point of tears. A sensible school treats handwriting as a skill to be mastered and then largely forgotten, which is exactly what fluency means.

What is at stake, in the end, is a question about what primary education is for. If the purpose is to give children the tools they need to think, express and learn across a lifetime, then a fast, legible, automatic hand belongs among them, alongside the keyboard and not beneath it. The case for dropping joined-up writing offers a tidy story about the march of technology, but it is a story that does not survive contact with a Year Six classroom, an exam hall or a child struggling to keep up with her own ideas. Keep teaching it. Teach it well, teach it briefly, and teach it to everyone.
