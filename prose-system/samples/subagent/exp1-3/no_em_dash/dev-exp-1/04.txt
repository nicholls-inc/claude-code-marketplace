A heat pump can warm your house from colder air outside because it does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists, and heat exists in outdoor air even on a freezing day.

**Cold is relative**

Temperature is a measure of how much thermal energy molecules carry. Air at 0°C is far above absolute zero (-273°C), so it still holds a large amount of energy. A heat pump pulls some of that energy out of the outdoor air and delivers it indoors. The outside air ends up slightly colder, and your house ends up warmer.

**How it works**

A heat pump uses the same principle as a fridge, run in the other direction. A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature. There are four steps.

1. **Evaporator (outside unit).** The refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, perhaps -20°C, so heat flows from the air into it. The refrigerant boils into a gas.
2. **Compressor.** This electric pump squeezes the gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, so the gas leaves the compressor at perhaps 50 to 70°C.
3. **Condenser (indoor side).** The hot gas gives up its heat to your heating water or indoor air, and turns back into a liquid. This is the heat that warms your home.
4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid is released through a valve, its pressure and temperature drop, and the cycle repeats.

The key trick is the compressor. Heat only flows from hot to cold by itself, so the refrigerant has to be made colder than the outdoors to collect heat, then hotter than your radiators to release it. Pressure changes do both jobs.

**Why it can beat a boiler**

A gas boiler turns the energy in the fuel into heat, and the best modern ones reach about 90 to 95 percent efficiency. They can never deliver more heat than the fuel contains.

A heat pump spends electricity only on moving heat, not creating it. For every 1 kWh of electricity, a typical system delivers about 2.5 to 4 kWh of heat. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance (COP), or seasonal COP (SCOP) over a year. A COP of 3 means 300 percent efficiency in boiler terms. Even at -10°C, most modern units still manage a COP of around 2.

**What to weigh in your decision**

- **Efficiency falls as it gets colder.** The bigger the gap between outside temperature and the temperature you need, the harder the compressor works. Modern units keep working to about -15°C or below, but they use more electricity in a cold snap.
- **Lower flow temperatures suit heat pumps best.** A boiler often sends water at 70°C or more. A heat pump is most efficient at 35 to 50°C. That usually means larger radiators or underfloor heating, and a well-insulated house. Poor insulation is the most common reason a heat pump disappoints.
- **Running costs depend on prices.** Electricity usually costs more per kWh than gas. A heat pump wins when its COP outweighs that price gap. At a COP of 3, it breaks even if electricity costs less than three times as much as gas. Check your local tariffs, and consider a time-of-use tariff.
- **Upfront cost is higher.** Installation commonly costs several times a like-for-like boiler replacement, though grants and subsidies often close much of the gap. Ask installers what is available in your area.
- **Practicalities.** You need outdoor space for the unit, some noise tolerance (modern units are about as loud as a fridge at close range), and often a hot water cylinder.
- **Defrosting.** In damp, near-freezing weather the outdoor coil can ice up. The unit periodically reverses briefly to melt it. This is normal and is built into the design.

**Practical next steps**

Get a heat loss survey from at least two installers. It should calculate how much heat each room needs and whether your radiators can deliver it at a lower temperature. Improve loft and wall insulation first if it is cheap to do so. Ask for the expected SCOP and annual running cost compared with your current gas bill, not just the headline price.

A heat pump works in the cold because it relocates heat rather than generating it. If your home is reasonably insulated and your electricity is not very expensive compared with gas, it is usually the cheaper and lower-carbon option over its lifetime.
