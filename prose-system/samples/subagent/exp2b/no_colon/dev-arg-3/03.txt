Keep the Joins

Every few years a headline announces the death of cursive. A state drops it from the curriculum, a school district replaces it with keyboarding, and commentators declare that joined-up handwriting is a Victorian relic, as useful to a modern child as a slide rule. I think this is a mistake. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it properly, with time, structure and purpose. The skill is not sentimental decoration. It is one of the cheapest and most durable tools a child can own, and abandoning it would cost more than the few hours a week it takes to teach.

Begin with the strongest case against, because it deserves a fair hearing. Children now grow up surrounded by screens. Most of the writing they will do as adults will be typed, and a good deal of it will be thumbed into a phone. The primary timetable is crowded, and every minute spent looping the tail of a letter y is a minute not spent on reading, number, science or music. Cursive is also hard to teach well, hard to mark and, for some children, a source of tears. Print-style letters are what children see in books and on screens, so why force a second alphabet on them? If the aim of education is to prepare pupils for the world they will live in, the argument goes, joined-up writing belongs in a museum.

The argument is tidy, but it rests on a false choice. Nobody is proposing that children should stop typing. The question is whether a child leaves primary school able to write by hand quickly, legibly and without strain, and whether joined writing is the best route to that. On this point the practical evidence favours joining. Printed letters require the pen to lift after every stroke. Each lift is a small decision, a pause and a restart. Joined writing links letters into whole words, so the hand moves in a continuous rhythm and the writer spends less effort on the mechanics. Teachers who have watched a class move from laboriously printed sentences to fluent joined ones will recognise the change. Speed rises, and the words on the page start to keep pace with the thoughts in the head.

That matters more than it sounds. A child who must concentrate on forming each letter has less attention left over for what she wants to say. Researchers who study writing describe this as a competition for working memory. When the physical act is automatic, ideas flow into the sentence. When it is not, they stall or get dropped. Anyone who has marked examination scripts knows the consequence. Pupils who write slowly or illegibly under time pressure lose marks, not because they lack knowledge, but because the pen could not keep up. For these children, fluent handwriting is not a nicety. It is a precondition of showing what they know. Exams in most countries are still written by hand, and they will remain so for a long time yet. A school that does not equip children for that reality is failing them in the most immediate way.

There is also a growing body of research suggesting that handwriting does something for the mind that typing does not. Studies using brain imaging have found that writing by hand activates a richer network of regions than tapping keys, particularly in young children learning letters. Experiments comparing note taking by hand and by laptop have often found that students who write by hand remember concepts better, probably because they cannot transcribe everything and so must think about what matters. I would not want to overstate this. Some of the findings are contested, some are drawn from small samples, and most do not separate cursive from print. It would be dishonest to claim that science has proved joined writing makes children cleverer. But the weight of the evidence points one way. Forming letters by hand supports learning to read and spell, and a continuous, rhythmic script is well suited to that work, because it treats the word, rather than the isolated letter, as the unit of movement. Spelling patterns become patterns of motion as well as patterns of sight.

Then consider the children who struggle. Those with dyslexia or dyspraxia are often cited as proof that cursive is cruel, yet many occupational therapists and specialist teachers recommend joined writing for exactly these pupils. Because there are fewer starting points and the pen stays on the page, letters are less likely to be reversed, and the spaces between words are easier to control. A b and a d, which look like mirror images in print, begin from different movements in a joined script. This is not universal, and some children do better with a simple print style. Good teachers adapt. But the claim that joined writing is generally harder for struggling writers does not survive contact with the classroom. Taught gently and in small steps, it often helps the very children it is supposed to punish.

Another reason is easy to overlook. Cursive is how our shared past is written. Letters from grandparents, wartime diaries, parish registers, the marginal notes in a family Bible and the annotations on an old recipe card are almost all in joined script. A generation that cannot read cursive cannot read these documents without a translator. We are already seeing archivists and teachers report that young people struggle with handwritten primary sources. This is a small loss in any one household, but it compounds into a real distance between children and the written record of their own families and nations. Teaching a child to read cursive without teaching her to write it is possible, but it is a poor bargain. People read most fluently the scripts they can also form.

Then there is the plain question of cost. Handwriting takes little. It needs a pencil and paper, a few minutes of daily practice and a teacher who knows what she is doing. It does not need a charger, a licence or a stable network connection. In a country where many schools are short of devices, and where many homes are shorter still, handwriting is the most equitable technology in the building. Insisting that children be taught keyboarding as a substitute simply moves the cost elsewhere and quietly advantages those who already have a laptop at home. Meanwhile touch typing, which is the skill that really matters on a keyboard, is itself slow to teach and is better taught later, when hands are larger and children can type at a useful speed. There is no need to choose. Primary children can learn both, in the right order.

None of this is an argument for the old way of teaching it. Nobody should defend hours of copying identical rows of loops, or the shaming of children whose slant fails to match a chart. The best modern practice teaches letter families together, introduces joins early, so that children never learn habits they must later unlearn, and keeps the sessions short and frequent. Ten minutes a day, built into lessons the children are already doing, beats an hour a week of drill. Teachers should aim for a script that is legible and fluent rather than perfect, and they should allow personal style to emerge once the basics are secure. A child who develops a distinctive but readable hand has achieved precisely what handwriting teaching is for.

The deeper point is about what a primary school is for. It is supposed to hand children the basic instruments of thought, the things they will use for the rest of their lives without ever thinking about them. Reading is one, number is another, and the ability to put a thought on paper without a machine is a third. Technology changes quickly, and the apps and devices of today will be curiosities within a decade. A pen will not. The person who can write fluently by hand is never without a way to record an idea, sign a document, leave a note, take a note, or sit an examination. That independence is worth protecting.

Teaching joined-up handwriting is a modest commitment, and its returns are broad. It helps children write faster and think more freely, it supports reading and spelling, it can help those who find writing hardest, and it opens the door to the handwritten past. Schools that drop it will save a few hours, and they will almost certainly spend more than that making up the difference in remedial support, in lost exam marks and in children who cannot read their own grandmother's letters. Primary schools should keep the joins.
