A lithium-ion cell is charged with a method called constant-current, constant-voltage, usually shortened to CC-CV. A typical cell using a graphite negative electrode and a lithium cobalt oxide or nickel-based positive electrode has a nominal voltage near 3.6 V and a upper limit of 4.2 V. The charger first pushes a fixed current into the cell. A common rate is 0.5C, which for a 3 Ah cell is 1.5 A. During this stage the cell voltage rises from about 3.0 V to the 4.2 V limit, and most of the charge, roughly 70 to 80 per cent, is delivered in this phase.

The current moves lithium ions from the positive electrode, through the electrolyte and separator, and into the graphite, where they sit between the carbon layers. Electrons travel through the external circuit to balance this. The voltage the charger must apply is the cell's open-circuit voltage, which rises as the graphite fills with lithium, plus the drop across the cell's internal resistance. That resistance is typically a few tens of milliohms for a 3 Ah cell, so at 1.5 A the drop is a few tens of millivolts.

Once the terminal voltage reaches 4.2 V, the charger stops holding the current and holds the voltage instead. Pushing past 4.2 V would drive electrolyte decomposition, plate metallic lithium onto the graphite and damage the positive electrode, so this limit is fixed. With the voltage held constant, the current falls on its own. The open-circuit voltage of the cell keeps climbing toward 4.2 V as it fills, which leaves a shrinking difference between the applied voltage and the cell's own voltage. That difference, divided by the internal resistance, sets the current.

There is a second contribution. As the graphite near the separator fills with lithium, ions have to diffuse further into the particles and further into the thickness of the electrode to find vacant sites. Diffusion in solids is slow, and the concentration gradient that drives it flattens as the sites fill. This adds a diffusion overpotential that also lowers the current the cell will accept at a fixed voltage.

The charger ends the cycle when the current has fallen to a set cutoff, commonly 0.05C to 0.1C, which is 150 to 300 mA for a 3 Ah cell. The constant-voltage stage usually takes 30 to 60 minutes and adds the last 20 to 30 per cent of the capacity. It is slow by design, because attempting to speed it up would mean exceeding the voltage limit.
