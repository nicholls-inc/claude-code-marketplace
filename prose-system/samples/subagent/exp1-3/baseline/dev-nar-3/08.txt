On a mild January day in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Around half past noon on January 15, workers, children and horses crowded the streets near Commercial Street and the harbor. Looming over them stood a steel tank the height of a five-story building, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a business that was about to become urgent. Prohibition was coming, and wartime demand for munitions had already made the stuff valuable. The tank had been built quickly, in 1915, by a company eager to profit. Its walls were thin, it was never properly tested, and when first filled it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the drips in pails. Rather than fix it, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. Residents had long noticed the tank groaned and shuddered when filled. Nobody in authority seemed to care.

Just the day before, a fresh shipment had arrived from the Caribbean, warm from the voyage. The tank was packed to the brim, and that morning the temperature climbed sharply after a cold snap. Then, with a sound some witnesses compared to a machine gun, the rivets began to pop.

The tank burst. A wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high surged outward, thought to be moving at around thirty-five miles an hour. It tore the tank apart, flattened buildings, and lifted a freight car off the elevated railway track. Wooden houses folded. A firehouse was knocked from its foundations, and men inside were swept away or crushed. The cold air of January soon thickened the flood, turning the rescuers' job into a sticky nightmare. People caught in it could barely move, and each breath was a struggle.

Rescuers from the Navy, the Red Cross and the police waded in, as did local boys. They searched for survivors through ankle-deep and waist-deep syrup. Doctors reported that the molasses clung so thickly to the injured that it was hard to even tell where wounds were. Twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty more were hurt. Horses, too, perished in large numbers.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews used salt water from fire boats and sand to scour the streets. The harbor ran brown into the summer. Locals insisted for decades afterward that on hot days, the North End still smelled faintly sweet.

Then came the question of blame. Purity Distilling insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, an idea that played well in an era of fear and unrest. But a long civil suit, one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts, took a different view. An auditor heard years of testimony from engineers and metallurgists, and concluded that the tank was simply too weakly built. The company paid roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements to victims' families and survivors.

The disaster's legacy was lasting. It helped push Massachusetts to require that architects and engineers sign off on building plans, and it led to stronger oversight of construction nationwide. Few disasters sound as strange, but this one was entirely preventable.
