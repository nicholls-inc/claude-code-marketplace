Apollo 13 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida on 11 April 1970 at 19:13 GMT. It was meant to be the third crewed landing on the Moon. The commander was Jim Lovell, who was 42 and on his fourth spaceflight. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot and was making his first. The command module pilot was Jack Swigert, who joined the crew only three days before launch. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and had no immunity, so NASA replaced him. The command and service module was named Odyssey and the lunar module was named Aquarius. The crew were to land in the Fra Mauro highlands, a region of rough upland that no one had visited.

The first two days went mostly to plan. One of the five engines on the second stage of the Saturn V had shut down early, and the others had burned longer to make up the difference, but the spacecraft reached its planned path. On the evening of 13 April the crew gave a television broadcast from Odyssey, which the main American networks did not carry. At about 55 hours and 55 minutes into the flight, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that keeps the liquid oxygen and hydrogen from separating into layers. Swigert switched on the fans in oxygen tank 2. About ninety seconds later there was a bang, and the crew felt the spacecraft shudder. It was 21:08 in Houston and the ship was about 320,000 kilometres from Earth. Swigert said that they had a problem, and Lovell repeated it.

Oxygen tank 2 had ruptured. The explosion damaged the service module, blew off one of its outer panels and cut off the supply from oxygen tank 1, which then leaked away. The service module held the fuel cells that made electricity and water for Odyssey, and the fuel cells needed oxygen to run. Within a few hours two of the three cells had failed and the pressure in the remaining tank had fallen close to zero. From a window, Lovell saw gas streaming out into space, and he reported it to Houston. The crew understood that Odyssey would soon lose power and that the planned landing was no longer possible.

The review board that investigated the accident, chaired by Edgar Cortright, reported in June 1970. It found that the tank had been damaged years earlier. In 1968 it had been lifted out of an earlier spacecraft and dropped by about five centimetres, which probably loosened a fill tube. During a countdown test at Kennedy in March 1970 the tank would not empty normally, and technicians used its internal heaters to boil off the oxygen. The heaters were controlled by thermostat switches rated for 28 volts, but the ground equipment supplied 65 volts, and the switches welded shut. The heaters ran for hours and burned the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank. When Swigert switched on the fans, a spark from the bare wires ignited what was left of the insulation in an atmosphere of pure oxygen, and the pressure rose until the tank failed.

The flight director on duty was Gene Kranz, and his team had to decide how to bring the crew home. Odyssey had enough battery power for about fifteen hours, and that was needed for the return to Earth. Aquarius was designed to support two men for about two days, mainly on the lunar surface, and now it had to support three for four days. The crew moved into the lunar module and shut Odyssey down almost completely. The spacecraft was at that point on a path that would have missed the Earth, so about five hours after the explosion Aquarius fired its descent engine for roughly half a minute. This put the ship on a free return trajectory, meaning that it would swing around the Moon and come back toward Earth without needing the main engine.

The spacecraft passed behind the Moon about 77 hours into the flight, at an altitude of about 250 kilometres. At its greatest distance the crew were 400,171 kilometres from Earth, which is still the record for human beings. Two hours after the closest approach Lovell and Haise fired the descent engine a second time, for a little over four minutes. This speeded up the return by about ten hours and moved the landing to the Pacific Ocean, where the recovery ships were waiting. Without the burn the splashdown would have been later and in the Atlantic.

The next days were uncomfortable. Aquarius had been built for a short stay, and its systems were being run well beyond their design. The carbon dioxide the crew exhaled was removed by canisters of lithium hydroxide. Aquarius had only enough for two people, and the spare canisters in Odyssey were a different shape and did not fit the lunar module's system. Engineers on the ground worked out an adapter that the crew could build from items on board, using a plastic bag, a sock, cardboard from a flight manual cover and duct tape. They described it step by step over the radio, and the crew built it. The carbon dioxide level fell. Power was limited, so the cabin temperature fell to about 3 degrees Celsius, and the crew slept very little. Water was rationed to about 170 grams per person each day. Haise developed a urinary infection and a fever. By the end of the flight the three men together had lost about 14 kilograms.

The ship also drifted slightly off its path, and the angle at which it would reach the atmosphere was too shallow. A burn of about fourteen seconds was needed to correct it. The ship had no power for its guidance platform, so Lovell lined up the spacecraft by hand, using the sunlit edge of the Earth as a reference. Meanwhile Mattingly and a team of engineers worked in a simulator in Houston on a procedure for turning Odyssey back on. The command module would have to be restarted after days in the cold, using only the battery power left, and no one had written such a procedure before. They finished it in a few days, and Swigert and Lovell read it from the sheets they were sent.

On 17 April the crew released the service module about four and a half hours before entry. It drifted away, and the crew could see that an entire panel had been blown off and that equipment hung out of the side. This was the first time anyone on board had seen the damage. They then moved back into Odyssey, powered it up and released Aquarius about an hour before re-entry. Odyssey entered the atmosphere with a longer radio blackout than the planned three minutes, and controllers on the ground waited for a signal. Then the radio came back, and the parachutes opened. The spacecraft landed in the South Pacific at 18:07 GMT, 142 hours and 54 minutes after launch, about six kilometres from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The three men were taken aboard by helicopter, tired and cold, and Haise was ill. Aquarius burned up in the atmosphere, and the plutonium generator it carried for the surface experiments fell into the Pacific near the Tonga trench.

NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks and removed the thermostat switches from the heaters, and it added a third oxygen tank to the service module. Apollo 14 flew in January 1971 and landed at Fra Mauro, the site that Apollo 13 had been meant to reach. Lovell later described the flight as a successful failure, a view that many people have repeated. The mission did not land on the Moon, but the three crew members returned alive, and the improvised work of the engineers on the ground and the crew in space is still studied as an example of how to handle an emergency.
