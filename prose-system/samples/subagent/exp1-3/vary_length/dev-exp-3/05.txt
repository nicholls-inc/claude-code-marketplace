**Sharing a Secret in Public**

Imagine you and a stranger want to agree on a secret number. You can only talk by shouting across a crowded room. Everyone hears every word. Can you possibly end up with something only the two of you know?

It sounds impossible. Yet it happens millions of times a day, every time your phone connects to a bank or a shop. The trick was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, building on ideas from Ralph Merkle. It is called public-key cryptography, and the core idea fits in a few pages.

**The paint analogy**

Start with paint, because it shows the shape of the idea before any numbers appear.

Suppose Alice and Bob each pick a secret colour. They also agree, out loud, on a shared starting colour: say, yellow. Everyone hears that, and that's fine.

Alice mixes a generous splash of her secret colour into the yellow and shouts the result across the room. Bob does the same with his own secret colour. Now the room has seen yellow, Alice's mixture and Bob's mixture. Nobody has seen either secret colour on its own.

Here is the clever step. Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her own secret colour to it. Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his. Both end up with yellow plus Alice's colour plus Bob's colour. The order of mixing doesn't matter, so the final shade is identical.

An eavesdropper holds only the two mixtures. To reach the final shade, she would need to un-mix paint. And un-mixing is brutally hard. Mixing is easy in one direction and almost hopeless in the other.

That lopsidedness is the whole secret. Mathematicians call such a process a one-way function.

**Finding a one-way process in maths**

We need something with the same property, but made of numbers. Paint is vague, and computers need exactness.

The answer comes from clock arithmetic. You already know it. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You divided by 12 and kept the remainder. Mathematicians write this as "mod": 15 mod 12 = 3.

Now take a different clock, with 23 hours on its face. Any number you reach is wrapped back into the range 0 to 22 by keeping only the remainder after dividing by 23.

Next we need one more ingredient: powers. Writing 5^6 means 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 × 5, which is 15,625. Divide 15,625 by 23 and you get 679 with a remainder of 8. So 5^6 mod 23 = 8.

Working forwards like that is easy. Give me the 5, the 6 and the 23 and I get 8 in seconds. But go backwards. If I tell you that 5 raised to some unknown power, mod 23, gives 8, can you find the power? With a clock this small, yes, by trying each power in turn. With a clock of several hundred digits, nobody on Earth can, because the answers jump around with no pattern to follow. The only method is brute-force guessing, and the number of guesses is beyond astronomical.

This is our paint-mixing. Raising to a power and wrapping round the clock is the mixing. Recovering the power is the un-mixing.

**The exchange, step by step**

Let's run the real thing with small numbers, so you can follow every step. Real systems use enormous ones.

First, Alice and Bob openly agree on two numbers: a clock size of 23, and a base of 5. These are the "yellow paint". Anyone may know them.

Second, each picks a private number. Alice chooses 6. Bob chooses 15. They tell nobody.

Third, each computes a public value. Alice works out 5^6 mod 23, which we saw is 8. Bob works out 5^15 mod 23, which comes to 19. They shout these across the room: Alice announces 8, Bob announces 19.

Fourth, each combines the other person's public value with their own private number. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power of 6, then wraps: 19^6 mod 23 = 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his 15: 8^15 mod 23 = 2.

Both arrive at 2.

That is not a coincidence. Alice computed (5^15)^6 and Bob computed (5^6)^15. Both equal 5^90, because multiplying the exponents gives 6 × 15 either way. Order doesn't matter, just as with the paint. The clock wrapping does not disturb this, since remainders behave consistently through multiplication.

Now consider the eavesdropper, Eve. She heard 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret 2, she needs Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. Finding either one means answering "5 to what power gives 8?" on the clock. With these tiny numbers she'll manage it in a minute. With 600-digit numbers, the best known methods would outlast the lifetime of the universe on any computer we have.

The secret 2 never travelled anywhere. It was built independently at each end. That is the part people find hardest to believe, and the most important to grasp.

**Why call it "public key"?**

In this exchange, each person has a private number and a public value. Only Alice knows her 6; the 8 is open to all. The pair is a key pair: one half kept hidden, one half published.

The name grows more natural with the second great invention in this area, which handles messages directly. Here Bob publishes a public key, and anyone can use it to lock a message to him. Only his private key unlocks it. Think of a padlock: Bob hands out thousands of open padlocks, and anyone can snap one shut on a box. Only Bob holds the key that opens it.

The best-known scheme of this kind is RSA, named for Rivest, Shamir and Adleman, who devised it in 1977. It rests on a different one-way process. Multiplying two huge prime numbers together is quick. Taking the result and working out which two primes made it is, for big enough numbers, not feasible. The product is the public padlock; the two primes are the private key.

**What it does not do**

Be careful here. Nothing so far stops an impostor. Suppose a man in the middle intercepts everything, does one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, and relays messages between them while reading every word. Neither victim would know. Public-key methods alone cannot prevent this.

The remedy is a digital signature, which uses the same key-pair idea in reverse. You sign with your private key, and anyone can check the signature with your public key. Websites carry certificates, vouched for by trusted authorities, tying a public key to a real identity. That is what the padlock in your browser address bar represents.

Also, these methods are slow. So in practice the exchange is used only briefly, to agree a shared secret. That secret then feeds a much faster scrambling method, and the bulk of your data travels under that.

**Why it matters**

Before the 1970s, sharing a secret meant meeting in person, or sending a courier with a codebook. Armies and banks spent fortunes on this. Public-key cryptography broke that constraint. Two strangers can now establish trust across an open channel, in the time it takes a page to load.

All of it hangs on one humble asymmetry: some things are easy to do and miserable to undo. Paint is mixed in a moment and can never be separated. Powers on a clock are computed in microseconds and can never be practically reversed. Build on that, and a room full of eavesdroppers learns nothing at all.
