On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third lunar landing, and by then the public had grown comfortable with the idea. Two crews had already walked on the Moon, and television networks had stopped carrying the flights live. The commander was Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier missions who was hoping to walk on the lunar surface at last. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot. The command module pilot, Jack Swigert, had joined the crew only days before the launch, when doctors grounded Ken Mattingly after he was exposed to German measles. Swigert had trained hard in the simulators, but he was still the newcomer on a crew that had worked together for months.

The first two days passed with little to worry anyone. The crew named their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius, and they settled into the routine of the long coast outward. Early on, one of the rocket stages had shaken more than expected, and the center engine of the second stage had shut down early, but the rest of the stack made up the difference and the flight went on. On the evening of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the mission and some two hundred thousand miles from home, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the supercritical oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module. The stirring was routine, because the fluids tended to stratify and the gauges read more accurately after they were mixed. Swigert flipped the switch, and a little under two minutes later a loud bang shook the spacecraft.

Swigert's calm words to Houston, that they had a problem, became famous, and Lovell repeated them a moment later. Warning lights lit across the panel, and the readings on the electrical system dropped. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas venting into space, a sign that something had gone badly wrong with the service module. Oxygen tank two had ruptured, and the blast had damaged the neighboring tank so that it too began to leak. The oxygen fed the fuel cells that made the command module's electricity and water, and it also supplied the air the men breathed. Within about three hours the spacecraft had lost nearly all of its oxygen and most of its power, and it was clear that the Moon was no longer the goal. The goal was to get three men home alive.

The investigators later traced the cause to a chain of small errors that began before the mission. The oxygen tank had been dropped slightly during handling at the factory, and it was damaged in a way nobody noticed. During a ground test it would not drain properly, so technicians used its internal heaters to boil off the oxygen, and the heaters ran far longer than intended because their protective switches had not been upgraded to the higher voltage at the launch pad. The heat scorched the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark from the bare wires ignited the damaged insulation in the pure oxygen, and the tank burst.

The controllers in Houston, led on the flight director's console by Gene Kranz and his team, faced a problem no checklist covered. The command module could not be kept alive for the days it would take to get home, and it held the only heat shield that would survive reentry. The engineers decided to use the lunar module, which was designed to carry two men for about two days on the Moon, as a lifeboat for three men for much longer. The crew moved into Aquarius and shut down Odyssey to save its batteries for the end of the flight. The spacecraft was also still headed for a loop around the Moon, and the engine that would have slowed it into lunar orbit was no longer usable. The ground teams worked out that Aquarius's descent engine could change the course.

About five hours after the explosion, Lovell fired the descent engine to put the spacecraft on a path that would bring it back to Earth. The spacecraft swung around the far side of the Moon, and for a time the crew was farther from Earth than any human beings have ever been, nearly two hundred and fifty thousand miles. They had little time to look at the Moon they would not land on. After they came around, a second burn of the descent engine sped up the return, with the aim of reaching the Pacific sooner and with more margin for their dwindling supplies.

The days that followed were cold, cramped, and exhausting. With almost all of the power turned off, the cabin temperature fell to a little above freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The men slept badly, if at all. Water was rationed so tightly that the crew drank only a few ounces a day, and Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection. The most dangerous problem was the air itself. Carbon dioxide was building up in the lunar module faster than its canisters could absorb it, and the spare canisters from the command module were square, while the lunar module's system took round ones. Engineers on the ground, working with a mockup of the items the crew had on board, devised an adapter from plastic bags, cardboard, a spacesuit hose, and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the men built it in space. The carbon dioxide levels fell, and a crisis that might have killed them was settled with materials found in the cabin.

Another correction was needed as the spacecraft drifted off its intended path. With the guidance platform shut down to save power, Lovell had to line up the spacecraft by hand, using the Earth itself as a reference, and then fire the engine for a precise length of time that Swigert counted. The burn worked. Meanwhile, back in Houston, teams in the simulators wrote a new procedure to bring the command module back to life using far less power than the normal sequence allowed, and Mattingly, who had been kept off the flight, was among the people who worked it out. The sequence had never been tested in space, and the crew would have to follow it perfectly.

On April 17, shortly before arrival, the crew separated the service module, and for the first time they could see the extent of the damage. A whole panel had been blown away, and the side of the spacecraft was scorched and torn. Lovell described it to Houston, and the photographs the crew took became a part of the investigation. They then powered up Odyssey, and the systems came on, one after another. Last of all they cast off Aquarius, which had saved them, and watched it fall away behind them.

Reentry brought one more anxious stretch. The radio blackout caused by the heat of the atmosphere normally lasts about three minutes, and this time it dragged on longer, because the flight path was slightly shallower than planned. Controllers waited in near silence for a call that did not come. Then Swigert's voice returned, and shortly afterward the parachutes appeared on television. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific, near Samoa, on April 17, 1970, after almost six days in space. Recovery ships were waiting, and the crew was taken aboard the USS Iwo Jima in good health, if thin and tired.

The accident did not end the Apollo program at once, but it changed it. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tanks and their wiring, and later missions flew with added safeguards. Apollo 13 is remembered as a failure that turned into a rescue, owing to the skill of the three men aboard and the thousands of engineers who worked through the days that followed. It showed that a crew and the people on the ground, working together under pressure, could improvise a way home out of nothing but what was on board.
