How Two Strangers Can Share a Secret in Public

Picture this. You want to send a private message to someone you have never met. You have never spoken and have no way to meet in person. The only way to reach them is a line that anyone can listen in on, like shouting across a crowded room or talking on a phone you know is tapped. Before you can send anything private, the two of you need a shared secret: a number that only you two know, which you can then use as the key to scramble and unscramble your messages.

It looks impossible. Anything you say to agree on the key, the eavesdropper hears too. If you say "let's use 4,817," they now know 4,817. For most of history people assumed this couldn't be done, and that two people had to meet first, or trust a courier, to swap a key.

In 1976 two researchers, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, published a way around it. Their method is called Diffie–Hellman key exchange, and versions of it run every time your phone or computer opens a secure connection to a bank, a shop or a messaging app. The idea fits on a single page and needs only school maths.

The paint analogy

Start with an analogy using paint, before any numbers.

Alice and Bob want to end up with the same tin of paint, in a colour nobody else can make. Eve is watching everything that passes between them.

1. Alice and Bob agree out loud on a starting colour, say yellow. Eve hears this. That's fine.
2. Alice privately picks a secret colour, say red, and never tells anyone. Bob privately picks his own, say blue.
3. Alice mixes her red into a pot of yellow and sends the result (orange) to Bob. Bob mixes his blue into yellow and sends the result (green) to Alice. Eve sees both pots go past.
4. Alice takes Bob's green pot and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange pot and adds his secret blue.

Now both pots contain the same three ingredients: yellow, red and blue. Alice and Bob have the same colour, a sort of brown, even though neither ever revealed their secret colour.

What about Eve? She has yellow, orange and green. To make the final brown she would need to get red back out of the orange, or blue out of the green. Mixing paint is easy, and un-mixing it is practically impossible. That one-way quality is what the whole scheme rests on.

Two facts carry the analogy. Mixing is easy to do and very hard to undo. And the order in which you add colours doesn't matter: yellow then red then blue gives the same result as yellow then blue then red.

Swapping paint for numbers

Computers don't mix paint, so we need a piece of arithmetic with the same two properties: easy one way, hard to reverse, and the same result whatever order you do things in.

The arithmetic we use is "clock arithmetic". On a 12-hour clock, 5 hours after 10 o'clock is 3 o'clock, not 15 o'clock, because you wrap round once you pass 12. Mathematicians call this working "modulo 12". In practice you do the sum normally, divide by 12, and keep only the remainder. 15 divided by 12 leaves a remainder of 3, so 15 becomes 3.

You can use any size of clock. On a 23-hour clock, 30 becomes 7, because 30 divided by 23 leaves 7.

Here is the recipe. Alice and Bob agree out loud on two public numbers: the size of the clock (we'll use 23) and a starting number (we'll use 5). These play the part of the yellow paint. Eve hears both.

Step 1: secret numbers. Alice secretly picks 4 and Bob secretly picks 3. These are their secret colours, and they never send them.

Step 2: each mixes in their secret. Alice multiplies 5 by itself 4 times and wraps the answer round the 23-clock:
5 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 625. 625 divided by 23 is 27, remainder 4. So Alice gets 4.

Bob multiplies 5 by itself 3 times:
5 × 5 × 5 = 125. 125 divided by 23 is 5, remainder 10. So Bob gets 10.

Step 3: they swap in public. Alice sends 4 to Bob and Bob sends 10 to Alice. Eve writes both down.

Step 4: each mixes in their secret again. Alice takes Bob's 10 and multiplies it by itself 4 times (her secret number):
10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. 10,000 divided by 23 is 434, remainder 18.

Bob takes Alice's 4 and multiplies it by itself 3 times (his secret number):
4 × 4 × 4 = 64. 64 divided by 23 is 2, remainder 18.

Both arrive at 18. That is their shared secret.

Why do they always agree?

This isn't luck. Alice's final number is "5, multiplied by itself 3 times (Bob's step), and then that result multiplied by itself 4 times (her step)". Bob's is "5 multiplied by itself 4 times, then that multiplied by itself 3 times". Either way, 5 has been multiplied by itself 3 × 4 = 12 times in total, and 3 × 4 is the same as 4 × 3. The wrapping round the clock doesn't spoil this; you can wrap as you go or at the end and get the same remainder. This is the "order doesn't matter" property of the paint.

Why can't Eve work it out?

Eve knows the clock size (23), the starting number (5), and the two numbers sent across: 4 and 10. To get the secret she needs to answer a question like this: "5 multiplied by itself how many times, wrapped round a 23-clock, gives 4?"

With our tiny numbers she can just try 1, 2, 3, 4 and find the answer quickly. That's why real systems use enormous numbers. In practice the clock size is a number hundreds of digits long, and the secret numbers are about as large.

At that size, the forward direction is still quick. A computer can raise a number to a huge power round a huge clock in a fraction of a second, using shortcuts such as squaring repeatedly instead of multiplying one step at a time. The reverse question, "how many times was it multiplied?", has no known shortcut. Wrapping round the clock scrambles the results so that the answers jump about unpredictably; getting close to the answer gives no hint that you're close. Trying every possibility would take all the computers on Earth far longer than the age of the universe. This reverse problem has a name, the "discrete logarithm problem", and its difficulty is what the whole system depends on.

So the clock arithmetic is the paint. Raising to a power is mixing, which is easy. Finding the power is un-mixing, which is effectively impossible.

Where the "public key" idea comes in

The numbers Alice and Bob send openly (4 and 10 in our example) are called public keys. The numbers they keep to themselves (4 and 3 in our example, by coincidence Alice's public and secret numbers are both 4 here) are their private keys. A public key can be shown to the whole world, because nobody can work backwards from it to the private key. That is the central idea of public-key cryptography: a key split in two, one half you publish and one half you guard.

Once Alice and Bob share their secret number, they use it as the key for an ordinary, fast scrambling method to protect the actual conversation.

One catch

This method stops someone who only listens. It doesn't, on its own, stop someone who can intercept and replace messages. Eve could sit in the middle, pretend to be Bob when talking to Alice and pretend to be Alice when talking to Bob, and set up a separate secret with each. To block that, real systems add a way to check that a public key really belongs to the person it claims to, usually through digital certificates issued by trusted organisations. That is the padlock icon in your browser doing its job.

The core trick remains the one Diffie and Hellman found: two strangers, each keeping one number secret, can do some arithmetic in public and both end up holding the same secret, while anyone listening is left trying to unmix the paint.
