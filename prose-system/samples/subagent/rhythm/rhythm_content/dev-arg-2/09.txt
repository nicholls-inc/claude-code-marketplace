Teams should stop estimating how long work will take, and the strongest reason is that the estimate rarely tells anyone anything the ticket did not already say. The effort that goes into planning poker, story-point calibration and quarterly roadmap sizing buys a number whose accuracy is limited by the one thing nobody can know in advance, which is what the engineer will find when they open the code. Task durations in software have a long right tail, so the average task is easy to guess and the task that eats the quarter is the one that surprises everybody, including the person who sized it. The planning fallacy is among the best documented findings in the psychology of forecasting, and years of retrospectives in which the team resolves to estimate more carefully next time have not repealed it.

The deeper damage comes from what an estimate turns into once it leaves the room. A manager repeats it to a director, the director repeats it to a customer, and the guess becomes a promise with a name attached. From then on engineers have an incentive to pad the number, to trim tests when the date approaches, to avoid investigating the strange behavior that might expand the work, and to report green until the day it turns red. A measure that people are rewarded for hitting stops measuring the thing it was meant to measure, and teams that live under date pressure learn this the hard way, usually in the form of an outage that traces back to a corner cut in week five of a six-week plan.

What replaces estimation is mostly dull and mostly already available. If you slice work into pieces small enough that each takes a few days, and you count how many pieces finish per week, you can forecast from your own history with more honesty than any group of engineers holding cards. Cycle-time data from the last six months will give a manager a range of likely completion dates for a given backlog, and the range will be wide in exactly the places where the uncertainty is real. When a hard deadline exists, because a regulation changes or a conference opens its doors, the sensible move is to treat the date as fixed, treat scope as the variable, and cut features from the bottom of the list as the date approaches, which is a decision that involves no prediction at all.

The objection that the business must plan is fair, but it confuses a budget with a forecast. Deciding that a problem is worth six weeks of three people is a statement about priorities that the team then works within, and it asks for judgment about value instead of a guess about duration. Some rough sense of magnitude does remain useful, and a five-minute conversation about whether something is a matter of days or of months will prevent most of the truly bad surprises. That conversation is cheap, and it stays cheap as long as nobody writes the answer on a slide and holds the team to it.

Teams that drop formal estimation tend to find that the meetings they lose were the least useful ones on the calendar, and that the arguments which replace them, about what to build and what to leave out, are the ones that determine whether the product succeeds.
