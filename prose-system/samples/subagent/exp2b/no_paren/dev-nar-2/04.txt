The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of four spaceflights and was about to make his second trip around the Moon. Fred Haise was a rookie who would pilot the lunar module. The third seat belonged to Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was pulled from the flight at the last minute. Swigert had trained quickly and slipped into the role.

By this point in the Apollo program, the public had begun to treat lunar flights as routine. Apollo 11 had landed the first men on the Moon the previous summer, and Apollo 12 had followed in November. The television networks did not even carry the crew's broadcasts from Apollo 13 live. The first two days went smoothly. The crew named their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius, and they settled into the quiet rhythm of a long journey.

On the evening of April 13, about fifty-six hours into the flight and nearly two hundred thousand miles from home, Mission Control asked the crew to perform a routine chore. They were to switch on the fans inside the oxygen tanks in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the command module that held the ship's power and life support. The fans stirred the supercooled oxygen so that the gauges would read accurately.

Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights lit up across the panel. Swigert radioed the famous understatement, and Lovell followed with the line that became history: "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause lay in a chain of small errors that had begun years earlier. Oxygen tank number two had been dropped slightly during handling at the factory. Later, during a ground test, its heater had been left running far longer than intended and had cooked the Teflon insulation off the wires inside. When Swigert switched on the fan, a spark ran along the bare wires and ignited the Teflon. The fire raised the pressure inside the tank until it burst, and the blast tore away a whole panel of the service module and crippled the second tank as well.

Oxygen was the lifeblood of the ship, because it fed the fuel cells that made electricity and water, and it also provided air to breathe. Within hours the fuel cells were dead and the command module was drawing on its small reserve of battery power, which would be needed for the final return to Earth. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space, a sight that told him the mission to land on the Moon was over. The only question now was whether the crew could survive.

The answer was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, power and water, and a descent engine that could be used to maneuver the whole stack. But it had been built to support two men for about two days on the lunar surface. Now it had to keep three men alive for four days. The crew moved in, shut down Odyssey to save its precious batteries, and began a hard improvisation.

The first decision was how to get home. Mission planners considered turning around at once, but that would have required using the damaged service module engine, which no one trusted. Instead they chose to let the Moon's gravity swing the ship around its far side and fling it back toward Earth. The spacecraft was already on a path that would have missed Earth if left alone, so the crew first had to fire the lunar module engine to put them on a trajectory that would pass closer to home. As they curved behind the Moon, Lovell, Haise and Swigert became the humans farthest from Earth in history, nearly two hundred fifty thousand miles away, a record that still stands. Some two hours after passing the Moon, they fired the engine again to speed the return and shorten the trip.

Then came a quieter danger. Three people breathing in a small cabin produce carbon dioxide, and the lunar module's filters were built for far less use. As the gas crept toward lethal levels, the crew had spare filters from the command module, but those were square while the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the same loose items the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, hoses and duct tape. In a matter of hours they devised an adapter, then talked the astronauts through building it step by step. The contraption, nicknamed the mailbox, worked, and the carbon dioxide level fell.

Conditions inside were miserable. To conserve power, the crew switched off nearly everything, including the heaters. The cabin temperature fell to near freezing, and condensation dripped from the walls. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and the men barely slept. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection, and Lovell and Swigert were exhausted and dehydrated. Through all of it they stayed in contact with a Mission Control that worked around the clock. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams refused to treat the situation as hopeless, and the ground crews tested each fix in simulators before sending it up.

One of the hardest problems was still ahead. The command module had been shut down for days, and no one had ever planned to restart it in flight with so little power. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded, spent long hours in a simulator working out a sequence that would bring the ship back to life without draining the batteries. When the time came, the crew followed his procedure line by line.

On April 17, the crew prepared for reentry. First they cast off the damaged service module, and as it drifted away they finally saw the extent of the injury. An entire side of it had been blown out. Then, with great reluctance, they left the lunar module that had saved their lives and climbed back into Odyssey, which was cold and damp but functioning.

Reentry brought one last agony. Normally the plasma around a returning capsule cuts off radio contact for about three minutes. Because of the odd angle and the low speed of this return, the blackout seemed to stretch on, and the controllers stared at their screens in silence. Past the expected time, there was still nothing, and then Swigert's voice finally broke through. Parachutes bloomed over the South Pacific, and the capsule splashed down near Samoa. The crew was picked up by the USS Iwo Jima and brought aboard to cheers, almost six days after launch.

A review board later traced the accident to the tank design and the damaged wiring, and NASA redesigned the oxygen system before flying again. Apollo 14 reached the Moon the following year. Lovell never flew in space again, and he, Haise and Swigert were honored around the world. Apollo 13 never made its landing, but many people regard it as the finest hour of the space program, because it showed that a crew and a team on the ground could work through a disaster that seemed impossible to survive.
