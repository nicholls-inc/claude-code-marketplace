Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years, a headline announces the death of handwriting. Keyboards are everywhere, tablets sit on desks, and a child born today may spend more of their working life typing, swiping and dictating than holding a pen. From this the conclusion is drawn that joined-up writing, the flowing cursive script that generations of children practised in lined exercise books, is a relic. Teach printing, the argument goes, and spend the saved time on coding or critical thinking.

I think this is wrong. Primary schools should continue to teach joined-up handwriting, and teach it properly. The case does not rest on nostalgia or on tradition for its own sake. It rests on what handwriting does for children's minds, on what it does for their speed and fluency, and on the plain practical fact that handwriting has not gone away and is not about to.

Start with the practical fact. Despite decades of predictions, pupils still write by hand for a large share of their school lives. In the United Kingdom, GCSE and A-level examinations are overwhelmingly handwritten. Students sit for hours, producing thousands of words under time pressure, and the legibility and speed of their writing directly affects what the examiner can credit. A pupil who prints each letter separately writes more slowly than one who has mastered a fluent joined hand. Over a three-hour paper, that gap is real. Teachers also know the opposite problem: a rushed, unjoined scrawl in which letters are poorly formed and indistinguishable can cost marks simply because it cannot be read. Handwriting is a gateway skill. If a child cannot do it fluently, every subject that requires written output becomes harder.

The speed argument deserves care, because it is sometimes overstated. Cursive is not automatically faster than print for every writer. What the research does suggest is that the benefit comes from fluency and automaticity, and that a well-taught joined script is a route to both. When letters are connected, the pen stays on the paper through whole words. There are fewer lifts, and the movement of the hand has a rhythm. Each word becomes a single motor pattern rather than a string of separate stops and starts. This is a different kind of learning from printing, and for many children it produces writing that is quicker and also more consistent. Teaching joins early, once letter formation is secure, builds that automaticity before bad habits set.

Automaticity matters because of what it frees up. A child who has to think about how to form each letter is spending working memory on the mechanics of writing instead of on the ideas the writing is meant to carry. This is one of the best-established findings in the psychology of writing: when transcription is effortful, composition suffers. Sentences shorten, vocabulary narrows, and children avoid saying what they actually think because getting it down is too laborious. When handwriting becomes automatic, those cognitive resources are released. This is the strongest single reason to teach handwriting well, and joined-up script, because it encourages flow, is a good vehicle for achieving it.

There is also a body of research suggesting that handwriting does something that typing does not. Studies using brain imaging have found that writing by hand activates networks associated with learning and memory more strongly than keyboard use, and experiments with young children have found that those who practise letters by hand recognise them better than those who type them. Part of the explanation is that handwriting requires the child to produce the shape of each letter from scratch, which engages the motor system in a way that pressing a key does not. Another part is that handwriting is slower than typing, which forces a degree of processing: students taking notes by hand tend to summarise and rephrase, while those typing are more likely to transcribe verbatim. I would not want to overclaim here. Some of these studies are small, and the findings do not all point the same way. But the direction of the evidence is consistent enough that a school system should be cautious about discarding a skill that appears to support literacy and learning at the very moment children are acquiring both.

Critics will say that this is an argument for handwriting, not for joined-up handwriting specifically. Fair enough. A child could learn a clear, fast printed hand and gain many of the same benefits. Several countries have moved in that direction, and some highly literate education systems teach print first and cursive later or not at all. I take that objection seriously. But I think it undersells what is lost if joins are dropped. Printed letters taught one at a time encourage a stop-start movement that tends to remain slow. Most adults who print do so in capitals or in a hybrid that is neither clean print nor true cursive, because they were never taught anything better. A structured joined script gives children a common, efficient model to build on, and a consistent target for teachers to correct. The alternative is not freedom; it is a patchwork in which each child improvises.

Then there is the question of access to the written past. Cursive is the script of most historical documents, family letters, old recipes, annotated books and archives. A generation that cannot read it will depend on transcriptions made by others. This is not a decisive argument on its own, since we could teach reading cursive without teaching writing it, but in practice the two are closely tied. Children who learn to write in joins can read them. It is also worth saying that handwriting is personal in a way that type is not. A signature, a note in a card, a line written to a friend: these carry something of the writer. Schools exist partly to hand on cultural skills that have value beyond the immediately useful, and this is one of them.

Now to the strongest objection, which is about time. The curriculum is crowded, and every minute spent on handwriting is a minute not spent on reading, mathematics or digital skills. This is true, and it is why the answer is not to teach handwriting badly or endlessly. Short, regular, well-structured practice, ten or fifteen minutes a few times a week, is enough for most children to develop a secure joined hand by the end of key stage two. Handwriting practice also overlaps with other aims: it supports spelling, because the child rehearses letter patterns as motor sequences, and it supports fine motor development, which matters especially for younger children. The cost is modest, and the return, in fluency and in freed-up attention, compounds across every subject that follows.

A related objection is that digital skills matter more. They do matter, and children should learn to type properly. But this is not a competition. Touch typing and handwriting are complementary skills, and a child can and should learn both. The mistake would be to treat the arrival of one tool as a reason to abandon the other, as though a child who can use a calculator should not learn to add. We teach children to do things by hand not because machines cannot do them, but because doing them builds understanding and capacity that the machine does not supply.

What about children who struggle? Some pupils, including many with dyslexia or dyspraxia, find joined writing difficult, and a rigid insistence on cursive can be counterproductive. This is a reason for flexibility in how we teach, not for removing the subject. A school should teach joined-up writing as the default and support children who need adaptations, whether that means a simplified joining style, more time, or a keyboard for extended work. The existence of exceptions does not undermine the rule; it is the reason good teachers pay attention to individuals. And for many children with motor difficulties, the fluid, continuous movement of cursive is actually easier than the repeated precise starts of printing, which is why some specialists recommend it.

Finally, consider what the alternative would look like in practice. If primary schools stop teaching joins, the skill will not vanish; it will simply become a privilege. Families with time, money and inclination will teach their children a fluent hand at home or through private tuition, and others will not. Handwriting quality already correlates with how much practice and support a child receives. Dropping it from the curriculum would widen that gap, and would do so in a skill that still affects how exam scripts are read and how children's work is judged. A universal, well-taught approach is the fairer one.

None of this requires treating cursive as sacred. If a better, faster, clearer script were available, we should teach it. Schools should be pragmatic about style, relaxed about perfection, and honest that the goal is legible, fluent, comfortable writing rather than copperplate. But the goal is worth keeping. Handwriting remains how children first learn to put thought onto the page, how they take notes and sit exams, and how they develop a physical fluency that supports their thinking. Joined-up writing is the most efficient and best-supported way we have of teaching it.

So keep the joins. Teach them early, teach them briefly but regularly, adapt them for children who need it, and teach typing alongside. The technology will keep changing, as it always has. The child with a pen in hand, writing a whole word in one movement, is still doing something worth the lesson time.
