Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. The case rests on a simple point: a rule that tells every builder how many spaces to provide is a poor way to find out how many spaces are needed, and the cost of getting it wrong falls on people who may not own a car.

Minimum requirements became common in American cities from the 1950s onwards. A council sets a ratio, such as one or two spaces per home, or around ten per 1,000 square feet of restaurant floor space, and no permit is granted until the plans show that many. The ratios were often borrowed from other cities or taken from manuals that surveyed demand at suburban sites where parking was free. Donald Shoup, the UCLA planning professor, made this argument at length in The High Cost of Free Parking in 2005. His point was that the ratios were set to meet peak demand at a price of zero, and then treated as a fact of nature.

Parking is not free to build. A surface space takes up roughly 25 to 30 square metres once the aisles are included, and in a dense area that land could hold a shop or a flat. A space in a multi-storey car park commonly costs somewhere in the range of $25,000 to $40,000 to build in the United States, and an underground space costs more. That cost is added to the price of the building and passed on to tenants and buyers. A household without a car pays for a space it does not use, because the developer was not allowed to sell the flat without one. This is the strongest objection to the rule, and in my view it is also the plainest: it makes housing dearer in order to subsidise driving.

The rule also blocks buildings that would otherwise be put up. On a small city lot, fitting the required spaces can leave too little room for the homes themselves. Older towns have many corner shops, churches and converted warehouses that could not be built today because their sites could never meet the ratio. A change of use in such a building often triggers a new parking calculation, and owners give up. The result is vacant premises in the places that are easiest to reach on foot.

The experience of cities that have acted is useful here. England dropped national minimum standards for housing in the early 2010s and left the decision to local authorities and developers, and many new schemes in city centres are built with little or no parking. Auckland removed its minimums across the city in 2016. Buffalo, in New York State, became the first large American city to abolish them in 2017, and Minneapolis, Austin and others have since followed. The early evidence from these places is that cities did not fill with cars parked on every street. Many developers still provide parking where they judge tenants will want it, but they build fewer spaces, and some projects that were previously impractical have gone ahead. This is a modest result, and I would not claim more for it. But the predicted harm has not shown up.

The usual objection is spillover: if a new block has no parking, its residents will park on neighbouring streets and crowd out existing residents. This is a real risk, but it is a problem of street management, not of building rules. Cities that price kerbside parking, or issue permits for residents only, can control it directly. Many already do. A council that charges a fair price for the space outside a house does not also need to force the builder next door to provide one.

A second objection is that people will drive anyway, so buildings without parking simply move the problem elsewhere. Some will. But car ownership varies a great deal by location. In central London roughly half of households have no car, and in many American inner neighbourhoods the share is similar. A flat near a rail station and a bungalow on the edge of town do not need the same number of spaces, and a single ratio cannot tell them apart. Developers and lenders can. They have a financial reason to provide enough parking to let and sell their units, and they know their own sites better than a zoning table written decades ago.

Removing the minimum does not ban parking. A supermarket will still build a car park, and a suburban estate will still have driveways. What changes is who makes the decision. Today the city decides in advance, for every site, and the builder complies. After reform the builder decides, and the city keeps its tools to manage the street. I think that is a better division of labour, and the evidence so far does not contradict it.

Cities should therefore repeal the requirements, and do it in full rather than lowering the ratios by a little. Lower ratios keep the same bad logic and invite the same fights. If a place needs more parking, the market and the price of kerb space will say so.
