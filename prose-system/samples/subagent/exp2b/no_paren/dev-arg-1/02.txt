Park the Rules, Not the Cars

Cities should repeal their minimum parking requirements. The rules are a relic of a planning fad, they raise the price of everything built under them, and they have never been shown to deliver the benefits their defenders claim. Removing them does not ban parking. It simply returns a decision to the people who actually pay for it.

Start with what a minimum parking requirement is. A city tells a developer that every apartment must come with one or two spaces, every restaurant with a set number of spaces per thousand square feet, every office with a fixed ratio to its floor area. The numbers rarely come from local evidence. Many were copied from a manual of parking generation rates, which was compiled from a small number of suburban sites with free parking and little transit. A planner in one town borrowed the figure from a planner in another, and the result hardened into law.

The first problem is cost. A surface space takes up roughly three hundred square feet once you count the lanes needed to reach it. A space in a structure can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost far more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, sale prices and menu prices, and everyone pays them, including the household that owns no car. A tenant who gives up driving still subsidizes the neighbor who keeps two. Researchers who have studied the effect find that a single mandated space can add a meaningful share to the rent of a modest apartment. In a housing crisis, we are legally requiring builders to pour concrete that nobody asked for.

The second problem is what the rules do to land. Parking mandates eat lots. A small site that could hold twelve homes may only hold eight once the required spaces are laid out, or the project may not pencil at all. Older neighborhoods are full of charming corner shops and small apartment buildings that could never be built today because they lack the parking that current code demands. The mandate quietly outlaws the kind of gentle density that made those places walkable in the first place. It also pushes buildings back from the sidewalk and surrounds them with asphalt, so that the street feels empty and unsafe to anyone on foot.

The third problem is that the mandate guesses wrong. Nobody knows how much parking a given building needs, least of all a code written decades ago. A rule that demands too little leaves some tenants circling the block. A rule that demands too much, which is far more common, leaves lots half empty for most of the year. Developers and lenders have every incentive to estimate demand carefully, because an unused space is money wasted. A bank will not finance a building that cannot attract tenants. When the city steps aside, the market is not careless. It is simply the party with the best information and the most to lose.

Defenders of the mandates raise a few serious objections, and they deserve answers. The first is spillover. If a new building provides too little parking, residents will fill the street in front of nearby homes. That is a real worry, but it is a problem about the curb, not about the private lot. The remedy is to manage street parking directly, through permits for residents, time limits and fair pricing. Cities that price their curb space well find that spaces turn over and that drivers can find a spot. Forcing every developer to overbuild is a clumsy way to protect a public resource that the city already controls.

The second objection is that people will always own cars, so the demand is real. Many will, and nothing in repeal prevents a developer from building parking where customers want it. Suburban shopping centers and family housing will keep generous lots because their tenants demand them. Repeal changes the default from one size fits all to what works here. Buenos Aires, Minneapolis and a growing list of American cities that have scrapped their mandates have not seen a collapse into gridlock. They have seen developers build parking when it makes sense and skip it when it does not, and a number of long stalled projects have finally moved forward.

The third objection is fairness to people with disabilities and to families who depend on vehicles. Accessible spaces can remain a separate requirement, tied to the needs of those who use them, and it is entirely reasonable to keep it. That is a targeted rule with a clear purpose, quite unlike a blanket mandate for hundreds of ordinary spaces.

There is also a climate and fiscal case. A city that dedicates a large share of its central land to storing empty vehicles gives up taxable buildings, shops and homes, and it pushes everything farther apart, which makes driving unavoidable. Compact places generate more tax revenue per acre and cost less to serve with pipes and roads. Parking mandates work against that arithmetic.

Nothing here requires a leap of faith. Cities already regulate the street, the signals and the sidewalks. Letting builders and buyers settle how many private spaces to include is a modest step, and the evidence from places that have tried it is encouraging.

So the case comes down to a simple question of who should decide. Today a rule written long ago, often by copying another city, decides for everyone, and it makes housing and small business more expensive in the process. Repealing the requirement does not end parking. It ends the pretense that a planner can know the right number in advance. Cities that want more homes, livelier streets and lower costs should take the mandate off the books and manage the curb with care.
