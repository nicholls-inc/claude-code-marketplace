Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon, because the rules do not protect drivers so much as they quietly rewrite the economics of every building a city allows. A minimum parking requirement tells a developer how many spaces must come with a new apartment, shop or restaurant, usually calculated from a formula that assumes every household owns cars and every customer arrives in one. The formulas trace back to manuals compiled by traffic engineers who counted cars at a handful of suburban sites with free parking and generalized from there, and the numbers have hardened into law in thousands of municipalities without anyone having to defend them. When a rule that arbitrary determines what gets built, the burden of proof ought to fall on the rule.

Consider what a required space costs. A surface stall takes roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisle, and in a growing city that is land that could hold a bedroom, a storefront or a small park. Structured parking runs from tens of thousands of dollars per space above ground to far more below it, and that cost does not vanish; it gets folded into rents and sale prices whether or not the tenant owns a car. A household without a vehicle ends up paying for a space it will never use, and the arrangement taxes the poorest renters hardest, since they are the people most likely to go without a car. Cities that say they want affordable housing while mandating an expensive amenity that many residents do not want are working against themselves, and the contradiction is not subtle.

The mandates also decide which buildings are possible at all. A narrow downtown lot that could hold a four-storey building with shops on the ground floor may be unbuildable once the code demands twenty spaces, because the ramps alone would eat the site, so the lot stays a parking lot or a single-storey chain pharmacy ringed by asphalt. Older neighbourhoods that people love, the ones with corner groceries and rowhouses on small plots, would be illegal to build today for exactly this reason. Requirements punish small businesses and modest infill projects most severely, because large developers can spread the cost across hundreds of units while a family converting a garage or opening a bakery cannot. Much of what we call the housing shortage is a shortage of legal ways to build the kinds of places that already exist and already work.

Defenders raise a reasonable worry, which is that without requirements new buildings will spill their cars onto neighbouring streets and make life miserable for people who already live there. The worry deserves an answer, but removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build spaces wherever tenants and customers want them, and lenders often insist on it, so the realistic effect is that the number of spaces becomes a market decision and not a legislated one. Where the effect is truly local, cities have better tools than a blunt mandate, such as pricing curbside spaces, issuing resident permits, and using the revenue to improve the neighbourhood. Free street parking is itself a policy choice that invites overuse, and the answer to a crowded curb is a price on it, not a requirement that every private lot subsidize it.

The evidence from places that have tried it is encouraging and, in the main, unremarkable. Buffalo eliminated its requirements citywide in 2017 and saw no collapse in the quality of life and no flood of parkless towers; developers kept building parking where it made sense and skipped it where it did not. Other cities, including Minneapolis, Anchorage and a growing list across Canada and Europe, have followed with similar results. Cities that have loosened the rules report more projects penciling out, more small-scale housing, and more commercial space along streets that used to be dead zones, which suggests the requirements were suppressing activity and not merely rearranging it.

There is also a plain question of fairness and of what a city is for. A parking mandate forces everyone to cover the cost of car storage, including those who walk, bike or take the bus, and it pushes destinations further apart so that driving becomes the only practical choice, which in turn seems to justify the mandate. The cycle is self-reinforcing, and breaking it is how a city begins to offer residents a genuine choice about how they live. Climate goals, road safety, and municipal finances all point in the same direction, since sprawling, low-density development generates less tax revenue per acre than compact blocks and costs more to serve with pipes, roads and emergency crews.

None of this requires a city to pretend that cars will disappear. It requires only that planners stop guessing, on behalf of every future builder, how much parking each building needs, and let the people who know their tenants, their customers and their financing make that call. Some will build plenty, and some will build none, and most will land somewhere reasonable in between. A city that trusts that process will end up with more homes, more shops and more varied streets than one that keeps legislating from an outdated spreadsheet, and the cost of making the change is a single vote to delete a section from the zoning code.
