**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American suburb and you will see the same thing: a sea of asphalt wrapped around a modest building. The pharmacy is a few thousand square feet. Its parking lot is five times that. On a Tuesday afternoon, most of it is empty. This is not an accident of the market. It is the product of law. Cities should repeal their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon.

Start with what these rules actually are. A minimum parking requirement tells a developer how many spaces they must build before they can build anything else. Two per apartment. One per 300 square feet of retail. One for every four seats in a restaurant. The numbers look scientific, but they usually trace back to a thin handbook of traffic counts taken at suburban sites, often in places with no transit and plenty of cheap land. Cities copied them, one after another, until a guess became doctrine.

The costs are real, and they are paid by everyone. A single structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build. A surface space is cheaper, but it eats land that could hold homes, shops, or trees. Those costs do not vanish. They get folded into the rent, the price of the sandwich, the price of the condo. Researchers who have tried to estimate the effect find that parking mandates raise housing costs noticeably, and that the burden falls hardest on households that own no car at all. A renter who bikes to work still pays for a space she never uses.

This is the quiet unfairness at the heart of the policy. Roughly one in ten American households has no vehicle, and the share is far higher in dense, older neighborhoods. Forcing a developer to build parking for them is a tax on the people least able to afford it.

Then there is the question of what gets built at all. Parking requirements act like a hidden veto. A small lot in a walkable neighborhood might comfortably hold eight apartments, but once you subtract the space for mandated parking, the project no longer pencils out. So nothing gets built, or something much smaller does. Old main streets are full of beloved buildings that could never be constructed today because they have no room for a lot. We have legislated against our own best neighborhoods.

Critics raise a fair worry here. If cities stop requiring parking, won't cars flood the streets and make life miserable for neighbors? It is a reasonable fear. It is also mostly wrong.

Removing a minimum does not ban parking. It simply lets the builder decide. And builders are not fools. A developer who wants to sell or lease units knows that many buyers want a space, and will build one. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision rather than a bureaucratic one. In a suburban office park, the lot will still be big. In a downtown apartment block near a train station, it may be small. That is exactly the point. One number cannot suit every site, and the people with money on the line are better placed to judge than a clerk applying a formula.

Cities that have tried reform are instructive. Buffalo, New York, became the first large American city to eliminate its minimums citywide in 2017. The sky did not fall. Developers kept building parking where it made sense and skipped it where it did not. Others, including Minneapolis, Austin, and a growing list of mid-sized cities, have followed. The early evidence points toward more housing, more small businesses, and more adaptive reuse of old buildings, with no collapse in the ability to park.

The remaining concern, spillover onto neighborhood streets, has a better answer than a mandate. Price the curb. Charge for on-street parking where demand is high, and use the revenue to improve the very neighborhood that generates it. Residential permit districts can protect blocks that are genuinely stressed. These tools address scarcity directly. Minimums address it by blindly covering the ground in concrete, which is a bit like curing hunger by building more refrigerators.

There is also a larger principle. A city that mandates parking has decided, in advance and for everyone, that driving is the default way to live. That bet made some sense in 1960. It makes less sense now, when cities are trying to cut emissions, lower housing costs, and make streets safer. Repealing the rules does not force anyone out of their car. It simply stops forcing everyone else into one.

The honest case against reform is that change is uncomfortable and parking is visible. People notice a full street; they never notice the apartment that was never built. But the invisible costs are the larger ones. Every required space is land not used for housing, a business not opened, a tree not planted.

So repeal the minimums. Let developers build the parking the market demands and not an inch more. Charge fairly for the curb. And let our cities grow back into places designed for people rather than for the storage of vehicles.
