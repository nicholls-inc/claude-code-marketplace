In the late summer of 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district of central London. Cholera is an infection of the gut that causes violent diarrhoea and vomiting. The body loses water so fast that a healthy adult can die within a day or two. The disease had reached Britain in 1831 and had returned several times, and the epidemic of 1848 and 1849 had killed tens of thousands of people. Nobody knew what caused it. The accepted view among doctors and officials was that it spread through bad air, called miasma, which rose from rotting waste and damp ground.

John Snow did not accept this. He was a physician in his early forties, born in York, who had made his name by giving ether and chloroform to patients during surgery and, later, to Queen Victoria in childbirth. In 1849 he had published a short book arguing that cholera was carried in water. His reasoning was that the illness attacked the bowels and not the lungs, so whatever caused it must be swallowed. He thought it passed from the waste of the sick into drinking supplies. The book had won few supporters.

The Soho outbreak began around 31 August. Snow lived and worked nearby, on Frith Street, and within days he heard that people were dying in large numbers in the streets around Broad Street. In the next ten days about 500 people died within a radius of roughly 250 yards. Many families fled the area, and shops and houses stood empty.

Snow began by finding out where the dead had lived. He obtained the death records from the General Register Office and went door to door, asking survivors and neighbours what the victims had eaten and drunk. He marked each death on a map of the district, with a bar for every case at the address where it occurred. The bars clustered around the public water pump in Broad Street. Almost every household that had lost someone lay closer to that pump than to any other.

The cases that did not fit the pattern taught him the most. A workhouse on Poland Street housed more than 500 inmates, and only five of them died. The workhouse had its own well and did not need the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street employed about seventy men, and none of them died. The workers were given beer as part of their pay, and the brewery also had its own well, so they had little reason to go to the pump. In the other direction, a widow in Hampstead, several miles away, died after the outbreak began. She had once lived in Soho and liked the taste of the Broad Street water, so a cart brought a bottle of it to her house each day. A niece who drank the same water died as well. Neither had been near Soho, which was hard to explain if the disease came from the air of the district.

On 7 September Snow took his findings to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, which ran local affairs. The next day the board had the handle removed from the pump, so that no one could draw water from it. The outbreak was already falling by then, as many residents had left and many of the most susceptible had died, and so it is not possible to say how many lives the removal saved. Snow himself said as much. The episode is still remembered as the point at which the water theory gained its first solid evidence.

The final link came from a local curate, the Reverend Henry Whitehead. He had doubted Snow's theory but knew the neighbourhood and its people well, and he began his own enquiries. He traced the first case to a baby girl at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill in the last days of August and died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspool in front of the house. The cesspool lay only a few feet from the well. When the well was dug up, its brick lining was found to be cracked and the ground around it was soaked with waste. Whitehead came to agree with Snow, and the two men reported their findings together.

Snow had also run a larger comparison in the years before. Two water companies supplied houses across south London, often on the same street. One of them, the Lambeth Company, had moved its intake to a cleaner stretch of the Thames in 1852, above the main sewage outflows. The other, Southwark and Vauxhall, had not. In the first weeks of the 1854 epidemic, the death rate in houses served by Southwark and Vauxhall was many times that in houses served by Lambeth.

The authorities were slow to agree. A committee that reviewed the Broad Street outbreak found the evidence insufficient, and the old view persisted for years. Snow died of a stroke in 1858, aged 45, without seeing his argument accepted. The bacterium that causes cholera was identified and widely recognised in 1883, and by then London had begun building the sewers and clean water supply that ended its epidemics.
