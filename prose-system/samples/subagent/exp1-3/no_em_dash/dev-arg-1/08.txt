**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American suburb and you will see the same thing: a shopping center surrounded by a sea of asphalt that is empty most of the year. This is not an accident of the market. It is the product of law. For decades, cities have required developers to build a fixed number of parking spaces for every apartment, restaurant seat and square foot of retail. It is time to repeal those rules.

Start with what minimum parking requirements actually do. They do not guarantee that parking exists. They guarantee that parking is built in a quantity someone at city hall guessed at. Those guesses often come from a handbook published by traffic engineers, which relied on small samples of suburban sites with free parking and no transit. Its own authors have cautioned that the numbers were never meant to serve as universal standards. Yet cities copied them into law, and the results are baked into our streets.

The first problem is cost. A surface parking space can run several thousand dollars to build. A space in a structure or underground garage can cost tens of thousands. Those costs do not vanish. They are passed on to tenants and buyers, whether or not they own a car. A household that gets around by bike, bus or on foot still pays for the garage through higher rent. Researchers who study this, including the urban planner Donald Shoup, have long argued that bundling parking into housing costs is a hidden tax that falls hardest on people with the least money. Lower-income households own fewer cars, yet they subsidize everyone else's.

The second problem is that parking minimums quietly kill buildings before they are proposed. On a small lot, the required spaces can take up so much room that the project no longer makes financial sense. A corner lot that could hold a four-unit building, or a small restaurant, instead stays vacant or holds a single house. Older neighborhoods are full of charming mixed-use buildings that could not legally be built today because they would not meet current parking rules. We have outlawed the very places many people pay a premium to live in.

The third problem is land. Every acre given to parking is an acre that produces no housing, no jobs and no tax revenue. A downtown ringed by lots and garages is a downtown with holes in it. Cities that need revenue to fix roads and fund services are, by their own rules, forcing valuable land into its least productive use. Parking lots also generate heat and stormwater runoff, and they make walking between destinations longer and less pleasant, which in turn makes people feel they need a car. The requirement creates the demand it claims to serve.

Defenders raise a fair objection. If developers build less parking, will cars simply flood the surrounding streets? Will residents of existing neighborhoods lose their spots? These worries are real, but they point to a different tool. Street parking can be managed with permits, time limits and fair pricing. Charging for curb space in busy areas keeps spots available and brings in revenue that can be returned to the neighborhood. That approach addresses scarcity directly. A blanket mandate on every new building does not.

Notice also what repeal does not do. Removing minimums does not ban parking. Developers will still build it where customers want it. A suburban grocery store with a big lot will keep one. An apartment building near a highway will include spaces because tenants will demand them. The difference is that the decision shifts from a regulator's formula to someone who actually bears the cost and knows the site. Some projects will provide more parking than the old rules required, and some will provide less. Both outcomes are healthier than a one-size-fits-all number.

The evidence from places that have tried this is encouraging. Several American cities, including Buffalo, which ended its minimums citywide in 2017, and others that have followed since, have reported that developers continue to include parking in many projects while others use the freedom to build more homes or smaller, cheaper units. Large cities abroad have operated without such mandates for years. The sky has not fallen.

There is also a fairness argument that deserves more weight. A city that wants to be affordable, climate-friendly and welcoming cannot also insist that every new home come with a car storage unit. Those goals pull in opposite directions. We cannot ask people to drive less while requiring that driving be accommodated at every address.

Repealing parking minimums is not radical. It is a modest correction that returns a private decision to the people who bear its consequences. It lowers the cost of building, makes small and infill projects possible, and lets land serve people instead of vehicles. Cities that care about affordability, tax health and livable streets should strike these requirements from their codes, and manage the curb with prices instead of mandates. The asphalt sea can finally start to shrink.
