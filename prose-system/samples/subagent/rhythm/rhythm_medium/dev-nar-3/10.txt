On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Children were out of school for lunch, dockworkers were on their breaks, and horses and wagons crowded the streets near the harbor. Looming over Commercial Street stood a steel tank nearly fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which was used to make industrial alcohol, and which had arrived by ship from the Caribbean.

The tank had been built hastily in 1915, and it had never been treated with much care. Its walls were thinner than they should have been, and no one had properly tested it by filling it with water first. It leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups and buckets. Instead of repairing it, the company painted it brown so that the stains would be harder to see. Residents also reported that the tank groaned and shuddered whenever it was filled, but their complaints went unanswered.

A fresh shipment of molasses had been pumped into the tank just a day earlier, and the unusual warmth that January afternoon thinned the syrup and increased the pressure inside. At about 12:30, a sound like a machine gun rang out as the rivets began to shoot from the steel plates. Then the tank burst open. A wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high, by most accounts, surged outward at a speed estimated near thirty-five miles an hour, carrying with it a rumbling roar that witnesses never forgot.

The wave crushed everything in its path. It tore a nearby firehouse from its foundation, flattened freight cars, and bent the supports of the elevated railway overhead. Buildings splintered as if they were made of matchsticks, and wagons, horses, and people were swept up together in the sticky flood. Because the molasses was so thick and cold air quickly stiffened it, those caught inside could barely move, and some were trapped as it thickened around them. Twenty-one people died, among them children, laborers, and firefighters, and about one hundred fifty more were injured.

Rescuers rushed to the scene, including soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship and Red Cross workers who struggled through the knee-deep muck. They searched for survivors for days, and the last of the victims was not recovered until months afterward. Cleanup was an enormous task. Crews sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats and covered them with sand, and for weeks the harbor itself ran brown. Residents claimed that the neighborhood smelled of molasses for decades afterward, especially on warm days.

The disaster led to a lawsuit that became one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts. The company insisted that anarchists had sabotaged the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court examined years of testimony and found the structure itself to be at fault. Purity Distilling paid roughly six hundred thousand dollars in damages to victims and their families. The case helped push through stricter rules requiring engineers to approve building plans, and it showed that a corporation could be held accountable for cutting corners.
