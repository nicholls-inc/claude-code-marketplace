Keep Teaching the Join

Every few years a school board or a columnist announces that joined-up handwriting has outlived its usefulness. The argument runs something like this: children will type for the rest of their lives, keyboards are faster than pens, and the hours spent drilling loops and ligatures would be better spent on coding, reading comprehension or anything else that looks more relevant to the twenty-first century. It is a tidy argument, and it is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it early, deliberately and well.

Start with the most practical point. Handwriting is not a separate subject from composition; for young children it is the mechanism through which writing happens at all. A seven-year-old who must stop after every letter to recall its shape, then lift the pen and plan the next, has little attention left for the sentence she is trying to make. Joined writing removes many of those stops. Once the join is automatic, the pen moves in a continuous stroke, and the child's working memory is freed for vocabulary, grammar and ideas. The research supports this, though not as a miracle cure. Meta-analyses of handwriting instruction, including work by Steve Graham and colleagues, have found that improving handwriting fluency is associated with gains in the quantity and quality of children's written compositions. The mechanism is simple. Fluent transcription removes a bottleneck, and a bottleneck removed from a young writer's day tends to show up in the writing itself.

The second point concerns spelling. Learning to spell involves more than memorising visual patterns. The motor sequence of writing a word becomes part of how children retain it. Many teachers have noticed that children who write words in joined script often recall their spellings more reliably, particularly words they have written repeatedly. The evidence here is less tidy than we would like, and I want to be honest about that. But the mechanism is plausible. A word written as one continuous movement is a single motor unit, and motor units are easier to recall than a string of separately planned letters. Neuroimaging work by Karin James and others has also found that children's brains engage reading and writing networks differently when they form letters by hand than when they type them, which suggests handwriting is doing something the keyboard does not.

Third, legibility and speed under pressure. Children sit examinations, write essays and take notes in lessons. A child who can write quickly and without strain has a real advantage in every timed setting, and many national examinations remain handwritten for the foreseeable future. Joined writing is typically faster than print for most children once it is learned, because it involves fewer pen lifts and more fluid movement. Print-only teaching does not automatically produce a legible, fast hand. Many children develop a slow, effortful, capital-heavy print that becomes a burden by age ten, and they never acquire the fluency they need.

Fourth, the fine motor argument, which is often dismissed but matters. Forming joins requires control of the wrist and fingers and a sustained rhythm that supports the wider development of dexterity. Teachers who work with young children know that the same grip and pressure problems turn up in every craft lesson, every science practical and every label. Joined-up practice, done in short, regular sessions, is a structured way to build that control.

Fifth, the argument from literacy itself. Reading and writing are not symmetrical. Children who can read cursive can read handwritten notes, letters, recipes, historical documents, and their grandparents' birthday cards. The parts of adult life that still rely on handwriting, such as signing documents, completing forms, writing cards and annotating a book, do not disappear because schools stopped teaching it. Cursive is a surprisingly durable code. If we remove it from the curriculum, we do not get a generation free of handwriting. We get one that cannot read it fluently and must learn it, unsupervised, as adults.

Now the strongest objection: typing is now dominant, and time is scarce. This deserves a serious answer, because a school day is full. There are three replies.

First, typing and handwriting are not competitors in a child's life. Children type too, and most will type more than they write by hand. But the keyboard requires its own instruction, and it is not free. Children's typing is slow until around the age of ten or twelve, and a child who types with two fingers because nobody taught touch-typing has not escaped motor learning. They have simply chosen a different motor pattern, usually a slow and error-prone one. Schools that drop handwriting should not assume that keyboards will fill the gap without anyone teaching them.

Second, the time cost of joined handwriting is smaller than critics imagine. A well-designed programme needs perhaps ten to fifteen minutes of structured practice a day in the early years, tapering as fluency develops. That is not a large claim on the timetable. The skills it builds, including sustained attention, controlled movement and planning a line across a page, are the same skills that make the rest of the day go well. Time spent on them is not lost to literacy. It is an investment in it.

Third, the objection assumes that the alternative to joined script is some clean, efficient print. In practice, schools that abandon joins rarely replace them with carefully taught print. Many children are left with an undifferentiated mixture of capitals and lowercase letters, reversed forms that persist into year five, and no fluent style at all. Handwriting is not optional. It is either taught or it is improvised, and improvised handwriting is usually worse.

What about children with dyslexia, dysgraphia or motor difficulties? Here the argument is more nuanced, and I think it supports keeping the join rather than abolishing it. Some children find joined script harder, and for them schools should be willing to teach a legible print or allow alternative approaches, including assistive technology for assessed work. A good teacher can do both: teach the joined form to the whole class and adapt for individuals. An accommodation for a few children should not become a removal for everyone. Abolishing a skill because some children struggle with it is a policy built around the average, and it fails the child who finds the skill hard and needs more support, not less teaching.

The deeper worry, I suspect, is that handwriting feels old-fashioned, so its defenders are assumed to be nostalgic. I would say the reverse. The case for keeping it is forward-looking. It gives children a fluent, personal and fast means of recording thought at the moment thought occurs, whether they are sketching a plan in a notebook, jotting a line in a lecture or annotating a textbook with no screen in reach. Those moments are not disappearing. A child who never builds the habit of thinking on paper will find that habit harder to acquire later, and the screen does not reliably supply it.

Nothing here argues for rigid, joyless drilling. The best handwriting teaching is short, regular, varied and built around meaning. Children copy sentences they care about, write labels and letters to real people, and watch their own handwriting improve over the year. It should stop being a grade-level test at age eight and become something children can actually use. But a primary school that teaches children to join their letters is not clinging to the past. It is giving them a tool that is fast, durable, expressive and, for many of them, still the most natural way to put a thought onto paper.

The question is not whether children will need handwriting. They will. The question is whether we will teach them to do it well while we have the chance. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up writing, and they should teach it properly.
