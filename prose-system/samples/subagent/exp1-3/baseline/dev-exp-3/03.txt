# Sharing a Secret in Public: How Strangers Agree on a Key

## The problem

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to talk privately, but the only line between them is one that anyone can listen to. A snooper, Eve, hears every word they say. It sounds impossible for them to agree on a secret under those conditions, because anything one says, Eve also hears.

It is possible, and it is the idea that protects online banking, private messages and shopping. It rests on one observation: some jobs are easy to do and very hard to undo.

## The paint analogy

Picture a shared tin of yellow paint that everyone knows about.

- Alice picks a private colour, say red, and mixes it into the yellow. She sends the resulting orange tin to Bob.
- Bob picks his own private colour, say blue, mixes it into the yellow and sends the greenish tin to Alice.
- Alice adds her private red to Bob's tin. Bob adds his private blue to Alice's tin.

Both now hold the same final tin: yellow plus red plus blue. Eve saw the yellow, the orange tin and the greenish tin. But she cannot unmix paint. Without red or blue to add, she cannot reach the final shade.

The trick is that mixing is easy and unmixing is practically impossible. Mathematics has an operation like that too.

## The maths version: clock arithmetic

You already know clock arithmetic. On a 12-hour clock, 9 + 5 gives 2, because you go past 12 and start again. Mathematicians call this "working modulo 12". We use the same idea with a different clock size. Ours will have 23 positions, numbered 0 to 22. When a number goes past 22, you take the remainder after dividing by 23.

We also need powers. "5 to the power 3" means 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. On our clock, 125 becomes 125 - (5 × 23) = 125 - 115 = 10. Clock arithmetic doesn't change how powers behave. It only keeps the numbers small.

## The exchange, step by step

**Step 1: agree on public numbers.** Alice and Bob announce two numbers out loud: the clock size, 23, and a "base", 5. Eve hears both. That is fine, because they are the yellow paint.

**Step 2: each picks a private number.**
- Alice secretly picks 6.
- Bob secretly picks 15.

They tell nobody, not even each other.

**Step 3: each computes a public value.** Each raises the base to their private number, then takes the clock remainder.
- Alice works out 5 to the power 6. That is 15,625. Divided by 23 this leaves remainder **8**. She announces 8.
- Bob works out 5 to the power 15 on the clock. The answer is **19**. He announces 19.

Eve hears 8 and 19. These are the orange and greenish tins.

**Step 4: each combines the other's public value with their own secret.**
- Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private 6. On the clock, 19 to the power 6 gives **2**.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private 15. On the clock, 8 to the power 15 gives **2**.

Both arrive at **2**. That shared number is their secret, and it was never spoken aloud.

## Why both get the same answer

Alice's route computes (5^15)^6, which is 5 multiplied by itself 15 × 6 = 90 times. Bob's route computes (5^6)^15, which is 5 multiplied by itself 6 × 15 = 90 times. Multiplication doesn't care about order, so 15 × 6 equals 6 × 15. Both end up at the same place. This is the "add the other person's colour" step: the order of mixing doesn't change the final shade.

The clock arithmetic doesn't break this. You can take remainders at any point along the way and the final answer comes out the same.

## Why Eve is stuck

What does Eve have? She knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret, she needs Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. She has to answer a question like "5 to the power of what gives remainder 8 on a 23-clock?"

With such small numbers she can just try every possibility. She tries powers 1, 2, 3 and so on until she hits 8, and finds 6 within moments. So our example is a toy.

Real systems use a clock with hundreds of digits and private numbers just as large. Going forward is quick, because computers have clever shortcuts for huge powers. Going backward has no known shortcut. Eve would have to try an astronomical number of possibilities, more than all the computers on Earth could check in the age of the universe. Forward is easy and backward is hopeless. That is the unmixable paint.

(Strictly, nobody has proved there is no shortcut. But decades of effort by many clever people have found none that works for well-chosen numbers.)

## Where "public key" comes in

The exchange above is usually called Diffie-Hellman, after two of the people who published it in 1976. The name "public-key cryptography" comes from the pairing of a number you can publish with a number you keep hidden.

- Alice's **private key** is her secret 6.
- Her **public key** is the 8 she announces.

The two are linked mathematically, yet knowing the public one doesn't reveal the private one. Anyone can use the public key, but only the owner of the private key can complete the other half of the trick.

A related family of methods, such as RSA, uses a different hard problem: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but taking the answer and finding the two primes is very hard. With RSA you can lock a message using someone's public key, and only their private key can open it. It is like a padlock you hand out freely: anyone can snap it shut, but only you hold the key.

## What happens after the secret is agreed

The shared number then becomes the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method, in which both sides use the same key to lock and unlock their messages. That fast method is not the clever part. The clever part is the secure handover of the key to strangers over an open line, which used to require meeting in person or sending a courier.

## The catch: knowing who you are talking to

There is one weakness. Imagine Eve does more than listen. She sits in the middle and pretends to be Bob when talking to Alice, and pretends to be Alice when talking to Bob. She does a separate exchange with each, and passes messages along while reading them. Neither victim notices, because each did a perfectly good exchange, just with the wrong person.

The maths cannot fix this alone. It protects against eavesdropping but doesn't prove who is on the other end. So real systems add a way of checking identity. For a website, a trusted organisation vouches that a public key really belongs to that site, the way a passport office vouches for who you are. This is what the padlock symbol in a browser means. Your browser has checked the vouching and then run an exchange like the one above.

## Summary

- Some operations are easy one way and extremely hard to reverse, like mixing paint.
- Alice and Bob each mix a private ingredient into a shared public base and swap the results.
- Each then adds their own private ingredient to what they received. Because the order of mixing doesn't matter, they reach the same final result.
- Eve sees the base and both mixtures, but unmixing them is infeasible, so she can't reconstruct the secret.
- Real systems use enormous numbers, so Eve can't just guess, and they add identity checks to stop an impostor in the middle.

Two strangers, a public line and a bit of clock arithmetic are enough to agree on a secret that nobody else knows.
