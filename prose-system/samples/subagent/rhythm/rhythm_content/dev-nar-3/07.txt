On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, in Boston's North End, a storage tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company burst open and released more than two million gallons of molasses into the streets. The tank stood fifty feet tall and ninety feet across beside Commercial Street, close to the waterfront, and it held molasses that had been shipped up from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a trade that mattered a great deal that winter because Prohibition was about to take effect and the war had created a hunger for munitions. The weather had turned unusually mild after a bitter cold snap, and the warming of the syrup, along with a fresh delivery that had been pumped in only the day before, is generally thought to have added to the strain on a structure that had been built hastily and with thin steel. Residents had long noticed it leaking, and some had watched brown streaks creep down its sides, but the company's answer had been to paint the tank brown so the stains would not show, and nobody with authority had tested whether it could bear its load.

Shortly after noon, with the lunch hour drawing people outdoors, neighbors heard a sound that some compared to a machine gun as the rivets shot loose, and then the tank gave way and a wave of molasses perhaps fifteen feet high and moving at around thirty-five miles an hour rushed down the street. It tore the elevated railway from its supports, lifted a train car off its tracks, knocked a firehouse from its foundation, and flattened wooden houses and warehouses as though they were made of matchsticks, while the air itself was pushed ahead of the wave with enough force to hurl some people backward. Those caught in it were pulled down into a thick, cold mass that grew stickier and more binding as it settled, so that a man or a horse that fell could hardly move at all, and rescuers who waded in found themselves trapped to their knees. Soldiers and sailors from a nearby naval ship, Red Cross workers, and Boston police came to dig out survivors, but in the end twenty-one people died, among them children who had been playing near the tank and a number of workers who had been eating their lunches, and about a hundred and fifty more were injured.

The cleanup took weeks and the smell lingered for years. Firefighters sprayed salt water from the harbor onto the streets, and sand was spread to soak up what remained, but the harbor ran brown for months and the stickiness seemed to cling to every shoe and every doorknob in the neighborhood. The aftermath mattered legally as much as it did practically, because the families of the victims joined together in one of the earliest major class action suits in Massachusetts, and after several years of testimony the court-appointed auditor concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound. The company, which had argued that anarchists had bombed it, was ordered to pay damages that settled out at roughly seven thousand dollars for each death, and the decision is often remembered as an early push toward requiring engineers to sign off on large construction, a change that gradually became standard practice across the country.
