A heat pump does not make heat by burning something, and it does not need the outdoors to be warm. It moves heat that already exists. Every object above absolute zero, including the air on a frosty January morning, contains thermal energy, and a heat pump is a machine for collecting that energy from outside and delivering it indoors. Heat naturally flows from warm to cold, so the machine has to push it the wrong way, and that is where the clever part lies.

The trick is a sealed loop containing a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature, far below freezing. In the outdoor unit, the refrigerant is circulated through a coil at a pressure low enough that it boils even at, say, minus fifteen degrees. Because the outside air is warmer than that, heat flows from the air into the refrigerant, which evaporates into a gas. You can think of it as a fridge running in reverse, drawing warmth out of the cold garden rather than out of your food.

The gas then passes through a compressor, which is the part that uses electricity. Squeezing a gas raises its temperature sharply, in the same way a bicycle pump gets hot when you use it, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor much hotter than your house needs to be, often sixty degrees or more. This hot gas flows through a second coil indoors, where it gives up its heat to your radiators, underfloor heating, or hot water cylinder, and as it cools it condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops its pressure, it turns very cold again, and the cycle repeats.

So the house is warmed by a fluid that is hotter than the rooms, even though the original source of heat was colder than the rooms. The compressor creates that temperature difference, and the electricity it consumes is the price of moving the heat uphill. Nothing in this breaks the laws of physics, because the machine is not creating energy from nothing; it is paying for a transfer.

The reason this matters to you is efficiency. A gas boiler turns each unit of fuel into at most about 0.9 to 0.95 units of heat, because some is always lost up the flue. A heat pump delivers roughly three units of heat for every unit of electricity, and sometimes more in mild weather, because most of the energy comes free from the air. That ratio is called the coefficient of performance. It falls as the weather gets colder, since the machine has to lift heat across a bigger gap, but modern models still manage around two to two and a half at minus ten degrees, which is still far better than a boiler could achieve.

There are practical points to weigh before you commit. Heat pumps deliver water at lower temperatures than a boiler, typically forty-five to fifty-five degrees, so they work best with well-insulated homes and larger radiators or underfloor heating. If your house is draughty or has small old radiators, you may need to improve insulation or swap some emitters, and that adds to the cost. The outdoor unit also needs space and makes a modest hum. Installation usually costs several times more than a new boiler, although grants in many countries narrow that gap.

Running costs depend on the price of electricity relative to gas. Where electricity is expensive, the efficiency advantage can be partly cancelled out, so it is worth asking installers for a heat loss survey and a projected annual bill using your own tariff. If your boiler is near the end of its life, your home is reasonably insulated, and you can find a good installer, a heat pump is usually a sound long-term choice that cuts both emissions and, in many cases, your energy use.
