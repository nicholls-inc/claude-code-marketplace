**Scrap the Parking Mandates**

Every city has a rule that most residents have never read and yet shapes the street outside their window: the minimum parking requirement. It says that before anyone can build an apartment, a café or a clinic, they must also build a set number of parking spaces, whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these rules. Not tweak them, not lower them by a fraction. Repeal them.

Start with what the rules actually do. A parking minimum is not a forecast of demand. It is a legal command, and it overrides the judgment of the person who knows their own project best. A developer who believes tenants will pay more for a building near a train station, with fewer spaces and lower rents, is forbidden from building it. A restaurateur who would happily convert a few spaces into outdoor seating needs a variance. The market is allowed to decide almost everything else about a building, yet we insist on dictating how much free storage space it must provide for private cars.

The numbers behind these mandates are weaker than most people assume. Many codes derive their ratios from the Institute of Transportation Engineers' Parking Generation manual, which compiles observed peak demand at sites that were often suburban, often small samples, and often where parking was already free and plentiful. Planners then treat those peak figures as the minimum to build everywhere, for every day of the year. The result is lots sized for the busiest hour of the busiest day, sitting mostly empty the rest of the time. Anyone who has walked past a half-empty retail lot on an ordinary Tuesday has seen the evidence.

The costs are real, and they land on people who never drive. Structured parking is expensive to build, often tens of thousands of dollars per space, and underground spaces cost far more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, home prices and menu prices. A tenant without a car still pays for the space she doesn't use. This is a regressive hidden tax: lower-income households own fewer cars on average, yet subsidize parking for everyone. In a housing shortage, adding a mandatory expense to every new unit is perverse. Some small projects become impossible entirely, because the required spaces will not fit on the lot, and the homes simply never get built.

Parking minimums also eat land. In many downtowns, surface lots and garages occupy a startling share of the center, space that could hold homes, shops, parks or jobs. Cities that need tax revenue should notice that dense, walkable blocks typically generate far more tax value per acre than a parking lot ever will. Every mandated space is land taken out of productive use and turned into a gap between destinations, which makes the destinations farther apart, which makes driving feel even more necessary. The rule creates the dependence it claims to serve.

The standard objection is spillover: without mandates, buildings will leave residents and customers circling the block, fighting over street spaces. This worry deserves a serious answer, but the answer is not mandates. The answer is pricing and managing the curb. Cities can charge for on-street parking at rates that keep a space or two open on each block, use residential permit districts where needed, and return the revenue to the neighborhoods that generate it. Free curb parking is a scarce resource given away at zero price, and shortages are the predictable result. Fix the price, and the shortage eases.

Repeal also does not mean parking disappears. Here the evidence from cities that have tried it is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and Minneapolis and several other cities have since followed. Developers there still build parking where tenants and lenders want it. Banks, retailers and buyers have their own strong reasons to provide it. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision rather than a legal one. Suburban shopping centers will keep generous lots. A building beside a subway station may build very few. Both outcomes are fine, and both are better matched to reality than a single ratio in a code book.

Finally, consider the stakes beyond housing. Cities across the country say they want lower emissions, safer streets, cheaper housing and more vibrant commercial districts. Parking mandates quietly work against all four. They push up the cost of homes, spread buildings apart, encourage driving, and pave over places where people might otherwise gather. Few single zoning reforms touch so many problems at once, and few cost the public treasury so little. Repeal requires no new subsidy and no new agency; it simply removes a requirement.

Critics will say that cars are not going away, and they are right. But that is an argument for letting people provide parking where it is wanted, not for forcing it everywhere. A city that trusts builders, lenders and residents to decide how much parking a project needs will end up with the right amount in the right places. A city that decrees the number in advance will keep getting too much of it where it hurts most.

Remove the minimums. Manage the curb. Let the buildings follow the people.
