The Pump on Broad Street

In the last days of August 1854, people in Soho began to die. Soho was a crowded, working-class district in the middle of London. The illness came on fast. A tailor or a servant might feel well at breakfast and be dead by evening, after hours of violent diarrhoea and vomiting that drained the body of water until the skin turned grey-blue and wrinkled. The disease was cholera. In the first three days, more than a hundred people living within a few streets of each other died. Families who could leave packed up and fled. Shops closed. Within ten days the death toll passed five hundred.

Most Victorians thought they knew what caused it. The accepted explanation was "miasma": a poisonous vapour rising from rotting rubbish, sewage and filth, carried on the bad-smelling air of poor neighbourhoods. On this view, cholera came from breathing foul air, and the cure was to clean up the stench.

John Snow did not believe it. Snow was a doctor who lived a short walk away on Frith Street, and he was one of the best-known physicians in London. He specialised in the new science of anaesthesia, and the year before he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. For years he had been thinking about cholera, and he had reached an unpopular conclusion. Because the disease attacked the gut rather than the lungs, he reasoned that people must be swallowing it, not breathing it in. He suspected it travelled in water that had been contaminated with waste from people who were already sick. In 1849 he had published this idea in a short pamphlet, and almost nobody had been persuaded.

The Soho outbreak gave him a chance to test it. Snow suspected the trouble centred on a public water pump on Broad Street (today Broadwick Street). In those days most homes had no running water, so people carried buckets from a pump in the street, and the Broad Street pump was popular because its water tasted cool and fresh. Snow took a sample and looked at it. It seemed fairly clean, and that alone could not prove anything. He needed a different kind of evidence.

So he went looking for the dead. From the government office that recorded deaths, he got the names and addresses of everyone in the area who had died of cholera. Then he walked the streets, knocking on doors and talking to grieving relatives and neighbours. He asked one simple question about each victim: where did they get their drinking water?

A pattern appeared quickly. Most of the deaths were clustered tightly around the Broad Street pump. When Snow found victims who lived closer to a different pump, he usually learned that they had still chosen to fetch water from Broad Street, or that their children went to school nearby and drank from it there.

The exceptions told him just as much. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street, which housed more than 500 poor people in cramped conditions, had very few cases. It had its own well. A brewery on Broad Street itself employed about seventy men, and none of them died. The workers were given an allowance of beer, and the brewery also had its own water supply, so they had no reason to visit the pump. If bad air were the cause, these people, breathing the same air as their dying neighbours, should have fallen ill too.

The most striking case was miles away. A widow living in Hampstead, in north London, died of cholera even though there was no outbreak in her area. Snow found out that she had once lived in Soho and liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it delivered to her by cart every day. A niece who visited her and drank the same water also died.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow presented his findings to the local parish officials. They were sceptical, but they agreed to act. The next day the handle was removed from the Broad Street pump, so nobody could draw water from it.

It is often said that removing the handle ended the epidemic. That isn't quite right, and Snow himself said so. By then many residents had fled and the number of new cases was already falling. The real importance of his work was the method. He had shown, by carefully counting and mapping who got sick and who did not, that the disease followed the water and not the air. He later drew a famous map of the neighbourhood, marking each death as a small black bar beside the victim's house, so anyone could see the deaths piling up around the pump.

A local clergyman, Reverend Henry Whitehead, set out to prove Snow wrong and ended up confirming his theory. Whitehead traced the likely first case: a baby girl at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill just before the outbreak. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspit in front of the house. When the cesspit was dug up, it turned out to be leaking into the well beneath the pump, only a few feet away.

Even so, the authorities were slow to accept Snow's explanation, and an official report in 1855 dismissed it. Snow died in 1858, before his ideas were widely accepted. The tiny organism that actually causes cholera wasn't generally recognised until 1883. But in the decades that followed, London built a modern sewer system and cleaner water supplies, and cholera disappeared from the city. Today Snow is remembered as one of the founders of epidemiology, the science of tracking where diseases come from by following the evidence, house by house. A replica pump, without a handle, still stands near the spot.
