**How a heat pump heats a house when it is colder outside**

The puzzle is understandable: heat flows from hot to cold, so how can a house at 20°C draw warmth from air at 2°C? The answer is that a heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat, and it uses a little electricity to push that heat uphill.

**The principle**

Cold is not the absence of heat. Outdoor air at 2°C, or even -10°C, still holds a great deal of thermal energy; it is simply at a lower temperature than your living room. A heat pump captures some of that energy and delivers it indoors.

It does this with a sealed loop of refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature (well below -20°C at normal pressure). The loop has four main parts.

1. **Outdoor coil (evaporator).** Cold liquid refrigerant flows through it. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outside air, heat flows from the air into the refrigerant, which boils into a gas. This works even on a freezing day, as long as the refrigerant is colder than the air.
2. **Compressor.** This electric pump squeezes the gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, much as a bicycle pump warms up when you use it. The gas may now be 50 to 70°C.
3. **Indoor coil (condenser).** The hot gas gives up its heat to your central heating water or indoor air, and condenses back to a liquid. Because the gas is hotter than your radiators' water, heat flows the right way.
4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid's pressure drops, it becomes very cold again, and the cycle repeats.

So the compressor creates the temperature difference needed to move heat from cold to warm. That is the "pump" in heat pump, and it is the same process a fridge uses, run in reverse for your house.

**Why this is efficient**

A gas boiler turns each unit of gas energy into at most about 0.9 to 0.95 units of heat. A heat pump delivers roughly 2.5 to 4 units of heat for each unit of electricity, because most of the energy is simply collected from outside. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance (COP). Over a whole season, the figure is called SCOP, and a typical well-installed system in a temperate climate achieves around 3 to 4.

Efficiency falls as it gets colder, because the compressor must work harder to lift heat across a bigger temperature gap. Modern units still operate down to around -15 to -25°C, though at those extremes the COP may drop to 2 or less.

**What this means for your decision**

- **Running costs.** Electricity usually costs more per unit than gas, so the saving depends on the price ratio. With a COP of 3, a heat pump beats a boiler when electricity costs less than about three times the price of gas. Check current tariffs, and consider heat pump or time-of-use tariffs.
- **Your house matters most.** Heat pumps work best at lower flow temperatures (35 to 50°C), compared with 60 to 75°C for many boilers. Good insulation, draught-proofing and adequately sized radiators or underfloor heating make a large difference. A poorly insulated home with small radiators may need upgrades first, which adds cost.
- **Installation cost.** Expect a higher upfront price than a boiler replacement, often several thousand pounds or dollars more, before any grants. Many countries offer subsidies, so check what applies to you.
- **Space and noise.** You need an outdoor unit with clear airflow, and usually a hot water cylinder indoors. Modern units are fairly quiet, but placement near bedrooms or neighbours is worth planning.
- **Carbon.** Even on a fossil-heavy grid, a heat pump typically emits less carbon than a gas boiler, and the gap widens as the grid gets cleaner.
- **Cooling bonus.** Many models can run in reverse and cool your home in summer.

**A practical next step**

Before deciding, ask two or three accredited installers for a heat loss survey and a quote that states the expected SCOP, flow temperature and running cost for your specific home. If your boiler is near the end of its life, that is the natural moment to switch. If it is still working well and your home is draughty, improving insulation first may give the best return, and makes a later heat pump cheaper to run.
