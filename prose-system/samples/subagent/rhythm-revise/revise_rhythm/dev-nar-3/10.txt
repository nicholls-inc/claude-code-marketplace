On a mild January afternoon in 1919, the 15th, just past noon, with the temperature up to about forty degrees after a bitter cold snap, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Children played near the waterfront, workers ate lunch, and horses and trucks moved along Commercial Street beneath the elevated railway. Looming over it all, on a rise near Copp's Hill, stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, fifty feet tall, nearly ninety feet across, and holding more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be made into industrial alcohol, a business that was growing fast as Prohibition approached. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply in 1915, and its walls were thinner than they should have been, no one had properly tested it, and the company's president had never hired an engineer to check the design. Residents knew something was wrong, because the tank groaned and leaked so badly that brown streaks ran down its sides, and neighbors collected the sticky drips in cans. Instead of repairing it, the company reportedly painted the tank brown to hide the stains.

Then, shortly after twelve thirty, the tank failed. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rattle of popping rivets, followed by a deep rumble. The steel burst apart, and a wave of molasses perhaps fifteen feet high and moving at around thirty-five miles an hour surged into the streets. It crushed buildings, lifted a train off its tracks, and tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports. A firehouse was pushed from its foundation, and men inside were trapped in the wreckage, while others were swept off their feet and carried along as the thick, heavy tide swallowed everything in its path.

The warm temperature made things worse, and as the molasses cooled in the winter air it thickened and became nearly impossible to escape. Rescuers waded in, struggling with every step, and sailors from a nearby ship, police, Red Cross workers, and boys from a local training school searched through the sticky mass for survivors. In all, twenty-one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Some victims were found days later, horses were also lost, and the cleanup was a long ordeal in which crews sprayed saltwater from fireboats and spread sand to cut the syrup, and the harbor ran brown for months. For decades, locals insisted that on hot summer days the neighborhood still smelled faintly sweet.

The disaster led to one of the first major class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts. After years of hearings, an auditor concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, and the company, which had argued that anarchists had bombed the tank though the evidence did not support it, had to pay damages, with families receiving modest settlements. The case helped push the state toward stricter building codes and requirements that engineers and architects sign off on major construction plans. What began as a strange and almost comic image became a lasting lesson in what happens when profit is allowed to outrun safety.
