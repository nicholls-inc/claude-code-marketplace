Suppose two people, Alice and Bob, want to agree on a secret number, and they have never met. They have no shared password and no earlier chance to swap one. All they have is a line that anyone can listen to, such as an internet connection or a shouted conversation across a crowded room. A third person, whom we will call Eve, hears every word they say. It seems that Eve must learn the secret too, because anything Alice tells Bob is heard by Eve as well. Yet it can be done. The method was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, building on ideas from Ralph Merkle. It rests on one fact about arithmetic, which is that some calculations are quick to do and close to impossible to undo.

The arithmetic is the kind you already know from a clock. On a 12-hour clock, if it is 9 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 2 o'clock, because the count wraps round after 12. Mathematicians call this working modulo 12, and the only rule is that you keep the remainder left after dividing by 12. Any number can take the place of 12. We will use 23, so that the count wraps back to 0 after 22. Under that rule 20 plus 6 gives 3, because 26 divided by 23 leaves a remainder of 3. Subtraction, addition and multiplication all work this way, and every answer is a whole number between 0 and 22.

We also need powers. Five to the power of 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. Modulo 23 that becomes 10, because 23 times 5 is 115 and 125 minus 115 is 10. Now look at the first nine powers of 5, counted modulo 23. They are 5, 2, 10, 4, 20, 8, 17, 16 and 11. The list rises and falls with no pattern that you could use to jump ahead. If someone gives you the power, say 6, you can work out the result, 8, in a few steps. If someone gives you only the result, 8, and asks which power produced it, the only general approach is to try 1, then 2, then 3, and so on until one fits. With 23 that takes six tries. With the numbers used in real life it takes far longer than anyone could wait.

Here is the exchange itself. Alice and Bob say aloud two numbers, 23 and 5. Eve hears them, and that does no harm, because they are only the ground rules. Alice then chooses a private number, 6, and tells no one. She works out 5 to the power of 6 modulo 23, which is 8, and sends 8 to Bob. Bob chooses his own private number, 15, and tells no one. He works out 5 to the power of 15 modulo 23, which is 19, and sends 19 to Alice. Each has now sent one number across the open line and kept one number back. The numbers sent are called public, and the ones kept back are called private. That is where the name public-key cryptography comes from.

Next, each of them uses what the other sent. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power of 6, modulo 23. The result is 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private power of 15, modulo 23. The result is also 2. They have arrived at the same number without ever sending it. The reason is plain once you write it out. Bob's 19 is 5 to the power of 15, so Alice has worked out 5 to the power of 15, raised to the power of 6, which is 5 to the power of 90. Bob's 8 is 5 to the power of 6, so Bob has worked out 5 to the power of 6, raised to the power of 15, and that is 5 to the power of 90 as well. Multiplying 6 by 15 gives the same answer in either order, so the two paths meet at the same place.

Consider what Eve holds. She has heard 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the shared secret she needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. She has no direct way to the secret from the public numbers alone. She would have to work backwards from 8 to find the power of 5 that produces it, and that is the slow guessing job described earlier. In this small example she would succeed within six tries, so the example gives no real protection. It is there to show the steps. The protection comes from using numbers that are much larger.

In practice the modulus is a prime number of about 617 digits, which is the size known as 2048 bits. The private numbers run to hundreds of digits as well. Alice and Bob's computers can still do the work, because a method called repeated squaring gets a huge power in a few thousand multiplications rather than by multiplying one number by itself for an unimaginable number of rounds. Eve faces the guessing problem at that size. The best methods published so far need far more time than is practical on any computers that exist. This is the standing belief of the field and not a proof, since nobody has shown that a fast way of undoing the calculation cannot exist. Many systems now use a variant based on points on a curve, which gives the same protection with numbers of around 256 bits.

The secret that Alice and Bob end up with is then used as the key for an ordinary cipher, in which both sides scramble and unscramble messages with the same number. Such ciphers are fast, which is why they carry the actual traffic. Public-key arithmetic is slower, so it is used only for the opening exchange. Your web browser does this each time it connects to a secure site, usually without showing you anything. A second public-key method followed in 1977, devised by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman. It rests on a different hard problem, which is finding the two prime factors of a very large number. With it, anyone can lock a message using a public number, and only the holder of the matching private number can unlock it. Clifford Cocks, working at the British signals agency GCHQ, had found the same idea in 1973, but it was kept secret until 1997.

There is one weakness that the arithmetic does not remove. The exchange gives Alice no proof that the person answering is Bob. Eve could sit in the middle of the line, carry out one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, and pass messages between them, reading each one on the way. Each side would believe it was talking to the other. The usual answer is a certificate, which is a signed statement from an organisation that both sides already trust, saying that a given public number belongs to a given name. The signature is checked with the same kind of mathematics. So the exchange keeps a message private from anyone who is only listening, and the certificates deal with anyone who pretends to be somebody else.
