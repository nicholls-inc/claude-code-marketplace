In late August 1854, a terrible illness, cholera, arrived in Soho, a crowded, working-class district of London, and it killed with frightening speed. A person could be well in the morning, doubled over with violent vomiting and diarrhoea by noon, and dead within a day or two, drained of so much fluid that the skin turned blue and shrivelled. Within about ten days of the outbreak's start, more than five hundred people living within a few streets of one another had died. Many families fled and shops closed, and the neighbourhood around Broad Street, today called Broadwick Street, became a place of mourning and fear.

At the time, almost nobody knew what caused cholera. The dominant belief among doctors and officials was the miasma theory, which held that disease spread through foul-smelling air rising from rotting waste, sewers and swamps. It was a reasonable guess for a city whose streets stank, but it was wrong, since microbes had not yet been accepted as a cause of illness and the bacterium behind cholera would not be identified in any lasting way for decades.

John Snow, a physician living a short walk from Broad Street, had been thinking about cholera for years. He was an unusual man for his profession: a teetotaller and vegetarian, the son of a labourer, and a pioneer in the use of anaesthetics, having given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. During an earlier cholera epidemic in 1848 and 1849, he had come to doubt the miasma theory, noting that cholera attacked the bowels first, not the lungs, which is odd if the cause were something breathed in. He suspected instead that people swallowed a poison from contaminated water or food, and that the sick passed it along through their waste. He had published this idea in 1849, but few were persuaded.

When the Soho outbreak began, Snow saw a chance to test his theory, and rather than simply arguing, he went door to door. He gathered the names and addresses of the dead from the official register of deaths and then visited homes to ask where people had got their drinking water. In that era, there were no taps in most houses, so people fetched water from shared street pumps, and different pumps drew from different sources. Snow's questioning soon revealed a pattern: most of the dead had lived close to the pump on Broad Street, and most of them had drunk its water.

He also noticed the exceptions, which proved more revealing than the rule. A workhouse nearby, home to some five hundred residents, suffered few deaths, and Snow learned that it had its own well. A brewery on the same street had no cases among its workers, who were given free beer and, it seems, drank little else, and it too had its own supply. On the other hand, a woman who lived far from Soho had died of cholera after her family sent for bottles of water from the Broad Street pump, which she had liked the taste of, and her niece, who also drank it, fell ill too. Each of these cases pointed to the same culprit.

Snow also produced a map, marking each death with a small black bar at the address where it occurred, and the bars piled up thickly around the Broad Street pump. Maps of this kind were not entirely new, but his was a persuasive picture of the facts, showing that the disease clustered around one particular water source rather than being scattered across the district as bad air might suggest.

On 7 September, Snow took his findings to the local board of guardians, the officials responsible for the parish, and urged them to remove the handle from the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. The guardians, though sceptical of his theory, agreed to try, and the handle came off the next day. The outbreak was by then already declining, as many residents had fled and others had perhaps changed their habits, so the removal cannot be credited with ending it single-handedly. Still, the gesture became famous.

Snow's work was not finished, and later investigation uncovered the likely source of contamination. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who had initially doubted Snow's ideas, carried out his own careful inquiries and found that the first known victim, a baby girl, had fallen ill shortly before the outbreak. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspit very close to the Broad Street well, and when the pit was examined, its brickwork was found to be decayed, and it was leaking into the ground near the well. Whitehead eventually became one of Snow's strongest supporters.

Official authorities were less convinced. An inquiry concluded that the evidence did not establish Snow's claim, and the miasma theory remained influential for years. Snow died in 1858, aged only forty-five, without seeing his ideas widely accepted, yet in time, as the germ theory of disease took hold and the cholera bacterium was firmly linked to contaminated water, his work was recognised as a milestone. Today he is remembered as a founder of modern epidemiology, the study of how diseases spread through populations, and the Broad Street pump is marked by a replica in the street where it once stood.
