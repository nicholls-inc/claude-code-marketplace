**Keep the Loop: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years, someone declares handwriting dead. Keyboards are faster, screens are everywhere, and the children now in reception classes will write far more with their thumbs than with a pen. Some education systems have already acted on the argument. Finland dropped compulsory cursive in 2016, and a number of American states did the same before several reversed course. The proposal sounds modern and sensible: why spend scarce classroom hours teaching a skill that adults barely use?

I think primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and should teach it properly. The case does not rest on nostalgia, tradition or a belief that neat writing builds character. It rests on three practical claims. Joined-up writing is a better tool for the physical act of writing by hand, which children will still do daily for years. It supports learning, and especially spelling, in ways that are hard to replace. And dropping it would widen an inequality that schools exist to narrow.

**What the argument against gets right**

The best opponents of cursive are not careless, so their case deserves a fair statement. Adults do little extended handwriting. Most of what children produce after school will be typed. Classroom time is finite, and a lesson spent on looped letters is a lesson not spent on reading, number or coding. Some researchers also point out that there is little evidence that cursive is faster or more legible than a well-taught print script, and that some children, especially those with motor difficulties, find joining painful and slow.

All of this is true, and it shapes my argument. I am not claiming cursive is magic or that every child must write a flowing hand by age eight. I am claiming something narrower: that the default school approach should be to teach a fluent, joined script, and that the costs of abandoning it are larger than they look.

**First: joining is a better way to write by hand**

Start with the mechanics. Printing requires the pen to leave the page after almost every letter. Each lift is a small decision, a repositioning and a restart. Joined writing keeps the pen moving through whole words, so a word becomes a single rhythmic gesture rather than a series of separate stamps. That is why most fluent adult handwriting, including the messy kind, drifts toward joins. People who never learned cursive often invent their own semi-joined hybrid as they speed up. The joins are not decoration. They are what handwriting turns into when it has to be quick.

Teaching joins deliberately therefore saves children from an accident. If we teach only print, pupils will still be asked, from about age nine, to write at length in exams, in note-taking and in timed tasks. They will reach for speed and improvise, usually badly. A child taught a consistent joined style from the start develops that fluency with guidance instead of by trial and error. Handwriting speed also matters more than adults tend to remember. A child whose hand cannot keep pace with her thinking loses ideas mid-sentence, writes shorter answers than she is capable of, and is marked down for it. Fluent handwriting frees working memory for content. Labouring over letter formation spends it on the pen.

The usual objection is that keyboards make this irrelevant. But in most primary classrooms, and in nearly all examinations, handwriting remains the dominant mode of output. Until that changes, which will take far longer than the slogans suggest, children need to be good at it.

**Second: it helps children learn**

The more interesting case concerns what handwriting does to the mind. Several lines of research, though not all of it conclusive, suggest that writing by hand engages the brain differently from typing. In brain-imaging work with children and adults, handwriting has been associated with broader activation in regions linked to reading and language than keyboard input. Studies of young children learning letters find that those who practise forming letters by hand often recognise them better than those who only type or trace them on screens. Part of the explanation seems to be that each handwritten letter is produced differently every time, which forces the child to process its essential shape, whereas a key press is identical on every strike.

Joined writing adds something specific to this. Because letters are connected, the child learns that words are units with an internal structure, and that the sequence of letters matters. The hand rehearses common letter patterns such as -ing, -tion and -ough as continuous movements, which builds a kind of motor memory for spelling. Teachers who work with children with dyslexia have long used cursive for exactly this reason: the flow reduces the reversals of b and d, makes it harder to scramble letter order, and gives the child a physical anchor for how a word feels. The evidence here is thinner than the enthusiasts claim, and I would not stake the whole case on it. But it points in one direction, and no comparable evidence points the other way. When the downside of keeping a practice is small and the possible upside is real, the burden of proof falls on those who want to remove it.

Note-taking offers a further example. Students who write notes by hand tend to summarise and rephrase, because they cannot keep up with a lecturer word for word, while laptop users tend to transcribe. The reworking is what produces understanding. A fluent hand makes that approach practical for a child; a slow, laboured one pushes her back toward copying.

**Third: this is an equity question**

The argument that matters most to me is about fairness. When a school stops teaching handwriting explicitly, the skill does not vanish. It becomes something that children acquire, or fail to acquire, at home. Families with time, books, pens and parents who sit beside a child to practise will see their children write well regardless. Children without those things will not. We already know how this pattern works in reading and number, and handwriting is no different, except that it is cheap to teach well in school and nearly impossible to fix later.

The consequences are concrete. Poor handwriting is penalised in marking, often unconsciously, even when the content is strong. Studies of examiners have found that neat scripts receive higher marks than messy ones with identical answers. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly under exam conditions is handicapped for years, and the handicap falls hardest on pupils who already carry others. Teaching a clear joined hand to everyone, in a structured way, removes that variable.

There is also a dignity argument that is easy to dismiss and shouldn't be. Being able to sign your name, write a card, fill in a form or leave a note in your own hand is part of being a capable adult. Children who cannot do these things comfortably feel it, and some feel shame. A school's job is to equip every child, not only those whose homes would have done it anyway.

**Doing it well, and for whom**

None of this means a return to rulers on the knuckles and hours of looped exercises. The evidence favours short, frequent, well-taught sessions: ten minutes a day is better than an hour a week. Schools should teach a simple script with few exit strokes and no ornamental loops, which both reduces the learning burden and tends to produce more legible writing. They should begin joining once letter formation is secure, typically around age six or seven, and not before.

The approach must also be flexible. Children with motor difficulties, dysgraphia or physical disabilities should be offered alternatives, including keyboards, without stigma, and joining should never become a test of worth. The argument is for a strong default, not an inflexible rule. Nor does keeping cursive compete with keyboard skills. Children should learn to type properly too, and the two skills support each other. The choice between them is a false one, and the timetable can hold both.

**Answering the modernisers**

The strongest remaining objection is that we cannot teach everything, and that priorities must follow the future. I agree with the principle and disagree with the application. The future these children face will be full of screens, but it will also still contain pens, forms, exams, whiteboards, notebooks and moments when a handwritten note is the right tool. Basic literacy has always meant being able to produce language by more than one route. Dropping handwriting would not prepare children for the future; it would narrow their options in it.

Notice, too, how little a modest handwriting programme costs. It needs no devices, no licences and no training beyond what teachers already have. Set against the price of a single tablet programme, the investment is trivial. If the benefits are even a fraction of what the research suggests, they come cheaply.

**Conclusion**

Joined-up handwriting deserves its place in the primary curriculum for reasons that have nothing to do with tradition. It is the form handwriting takes when it becomes fluent, it plausibly supports spelling and learning, and teaching it in school guards against a quiet inequality that families, not classrooms, would otherwise decide. The case for abandoning it depends on picturing a world in which nobody writes by hand, and that world does not yet exist and may never arrive. Until it does, we should teach children to write in a way that lets the hand keep up with the mind. Keep the loop, teach it briefly and well, and let every child leave primary school with a hand they can rely on.
