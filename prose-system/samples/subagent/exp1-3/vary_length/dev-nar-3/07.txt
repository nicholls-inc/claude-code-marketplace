On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusual thaw. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit after days of bitter cold, and people were out in the streets. Children played near the waterfront. Workers ate lunch beside the horses and the elevated railway.

Looming over them stood a huge steel tank on Commercial Street. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had come by ship from the Caribbean. It would be turned into industrial alcohol, partly for the munitions that the First World War had made so valuable.

The tank was a poor piece of work. It had been built quickly, with steel too thin for its load and little testing. When it was first filled, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping molasses in pots. Rather than repair it properly, the company painted the tank brown to hide the stains. Residents reported groans and rumbles from the structure. Nobody acted on those warnings.

At about 12:30 that afternoon, the tank failed.

Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, as the rivets shot out of the steel. Then the tank split open. A wave of molasses roughly 25 feet high, by some accounts, surged outward at an estimated 35 miles per hour. It carried a crushing force. It tore buildings from their foundations and bent the girders of the elevated railway. A fire station was knocked off its footing, and its firefighters were thrown into the wreckage.

The cold made things worse. As the molasses spread, it cooled and thickened, and it became a trap. People who had been running found their legs stuck fast. Horses struggled and sank. Rescuers waded in and found that every movement took enormous effort.

Twenty-one people died. Some were drowned in the syrup, and some were crushed by debris. About 150 more were injured. The dead included children, among them a ten-year-old girl who had been collecting firewood. Cleanup crews worked for weeks, using salt water from fireboats and spreading sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown until summer, and locals claimed that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled of molasses for decades.

The disaster led to a landmark lawsuit, among the first major class actions in Massachusetts. The company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, a claim that fell apart under scrutiny. After years of testimony, the court found the company liable and awarded damages to the victims and their families. Settlements came to roughly $600,000 in total, which was a modest sum for so much loss.

The flood also changed the law. Engineers and architects were now expected to prove that structures were safe, and building codes tightened across the state. Plans had to be signed by licensed professionals. A tragedy that sounds almost comic had a serious result, and it left Boston a little safer.
