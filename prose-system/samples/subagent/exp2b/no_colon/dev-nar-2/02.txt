The Long Way Home

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of three earlier flights and was now on his fourth. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was making his first journey into space. The third seat belonged to Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days before. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons, so Swigert, the backup, took his place. The launch was almost flawless, and the crew settled into the quiet routine of a coast through space. They named their command module Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. Neither name could yet suggest how apt it would become.

For two days the mission went so smoothly that the television networks lost interest. A broadcast from the spacecraft on the evening of April 13 went largely uncovered. The crew showed viewers around the cabin, and Lovell signed off with a wish for a pleasant evening. Behind that calm sat a flaw nobody knew about. Before launch, during ground tests, a tank of liquid oxygen in the service module had been drained with the help of a heater. A thermostat in that tank was built for a lower voltage than the one applied, and the heater ran hot enough to melt the insulation off its wiring. The damage was invisible. The tank passed inspection and flew.

About fifty-five hours into the flight, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen tanks, a routine step that agitates the supercold contents so the gauges read properly. Swigert flipped the switch. A few minutes later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Bare wires inside the second tank had sparked in the pure oxygen, and the tank ruptured. The blast blew a panel off the side of the service module and wrecked the neighboring plumbing. Swigert reported to Houston that they had a problem. Lovell repeated it, and added the detail that would matter most. The main electrical bus had lost voltage.

The controllers in Houston first suspected faulty instruments, since the readings seemed too bad to be true. They were true. One oxygen tank was gone and the other was leaking away into space. The service module carried the fuel cells that made electricity and water from that oxygen, and the command module could not run on its own batteries for long. Within hours the crew would be in a dead ship, nearly two hundred thousand miles from home. Lovell, looking out the window, saw a stream of gas venting from the spacecraft. He understood what it meant. The Moon landing was finished, and the question had become whether anyone would get back alive.

The answer was the lunar module. Aquarius was designed to carry two men for two days on the Moon, with power, oxygen and water enough for that job alone. Now it would have to serve as a lifeboat for three men over four days. The crew powered it up, moved in, and shut down Odyssey to save its last reserves. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams in Houston took over the long work of finding a way home. They had to decide how to return the ship and how to make the consumables last.

The first decision concerned the route. The spacecraft was on a path that would loop behind the Moon and return toward Earth, but it would have missed Earth by a wide margin without further action. Engineers chose to fire the lunar module's descent engine after the swing around the Moon, which sped up the trip and aimed it at the Pacific. That burn, about two hours after the closest approach to the Moon, also meant the crew passed farther from Earth than any humans before or since, nearly 250,000 miles away. Lovell later noted that there was little time to enjoy the view. Later, a smaller burn corrected a drift in the course, and the crew aligned the ship by sighting on the Earth's terminator, since the usual navigation systems were off.

Then came the air. Carbon dioxide, which people exhale, poisons a closed cabin if it builds up. Aquarius had canisters of lithium hydroxide to scrub it, but they were sized for two men, and the stock was running low. Odyssey carried plenty more canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. Mission Control assembled a fix from whatever was aboard, which included plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, and a great deal of duct tape. Engineers read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the crew built the adapter in the cabin. The readings of carbon dioxide dropped back to safe levels.

The next enemy was the cold. With the power off, the temperature in the spacecraft sank toward freezing. The men wore thin flight suits and sometimes the boots meant for the Moon. They slept little, and condensation dripped from the walls. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, so everyone grew dehydrated. Haise developed a fever and a kidney infection. The astronauts were exhausted, hungry, chilled and cramped, and yet they were still able to follow the careful procedures required of them.

On the ground, the problem of reentry was just as hard. Odyssey would have to be brought back to life after days without power, and its tiny reserve of battery energy had to cover the final descent. No one had ever written a procedure for that. Ken Mattingly, the astronaut grounded by the measles scare, was brought into a simulator and worked for long hours with the engineers to develop a startup sequence that used the least amount of power. Each step was tested before it was radioed up, and the work saved precious amperes.

As Earth grew in the windows, the crew did one more thing. They separated the damaged service module, which gave them their first clear look at the destruction. A whole side of it had been ripped away, and the engine bell hung at an odd angle. Lovell reported that one entire panel was missing and that debris was everywhere. The photographs helped investigators understand the accident and reassured controllers that the heat shield on the command module had likely survived. They then climbed out of Aquarius, which had saved them, and let it go after a last burst of thanks. Odyssey came back to life, and the instruments woke one by one.

On April 17, the capsule struck the atmosphere at about twenty-five thousand miles per hour. The heat of reentry cut off radio contact, as it always does, and the blackout was expected to last about three minutes. The seconds ticked past, and then the minutes, and Houston heard nothing. It took longer than planned, and the delay was probably caused by the flat angle of the descent. At last Swigert's voice came through, and the control room erupted. Three parachutes opened above the Pacific, and the capsule dropped into the sea near American Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship Iwo Jima. The journey had lasted almost six days.

The investigation that followed redesigned the oxygen tanks, removed the damaging thermostats, and added a third tank kept apart from the others. The Apollo program continued, and six more missions flew, and four of them reached the lunar surface. Apollo 13 never landed, yet it became one of the best-remembered flights of the whole program. Lovell later said that the mission was a successful failure. What it proved was less about the machines than about the people. A few thousand engineers and three tired men, working with improvised tools and very little time, had refused to let a disaster become a tragedy.
