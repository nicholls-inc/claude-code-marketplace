On a mild January afternoon in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with the ordinary noise of a working neighborhood. It was January 15, a few minutes past half past noon. Horses pulled wagons along Commercial Street, children played near the waterfront, and workers took their lunch beside the harbor. Towering over them stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a business that was growing quickly as the country moved toward Prohibition and as the war effort demanded munitions. The tank was about fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, and it had been built quickly and cheaply. Neighbors knew it was troubled. It groaned and leaked so badly that brown syrup ran down its sides, and local children collected the drips in cans. Rather than repair it properly, the company painted it brown so the stains would not show. Nobody had tested it by filling it with water first.

The weather that week made matters worse. The temperature had been bitterly cold, and then it rose sharply. A fresh load of warm molasses had recently been pumped in on top of the cold syrup already inside. Pressure built, and the metal was strained to its limit.

Then the tank failed. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun as the rivets popped, followed by a deep rumble. The tank tore open, and a wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high, by some accounts, surged into the street at around thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed buildings, lifted a train off its tracks, and knocked a firehouse off its foundation. The wave was heavy and dense, and it moved with terrible force. People and horses were caught in it before they could flee.

Twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Rescuers, including sailors from a nearby ship and soldiers, waded into the sticky mess to pull out survivors. The thick syrup clung to everything and made the work agonizingly slow. Some victims could not be found for days. The cleanup took weeks, and Bostonians said the harbor ran brown until summer. For decades afterward, residents claimed that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled faintly sweet.

The aftermath changed American law. Families sued the company in one of the first big class action cases in the state. The company argued that anarchists had blown up the tank, but an auditor spent years hearing testimony and concluded that the tank had been poorly built and that the company was responsible. The company paid out settlements to victims' families. The case also helped push the country toward modern building regulations. Engineers were soon required to sign off on their plans, and the idea that structures must be properly designed and tested became standard.

Today a small plaque marks the spot. The disaster sounds almost comic in retrospect, but it was a genuine tragedy, born of corporate carelessness and ignored warnings.
