A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current, constant voltage, usually shortened to CC-CV. It has two phases, and the second explains why the last part of a charge is slow.

In the first phase, a charger forces a fixed current into the cell, typically between 0.5 and 1 times the cell's capacity per hour. The cell voltage rises steadily as lithium ions leave the cathode, a layered metal oxide, cross the electrolyte and insert themselves between the graphite layers of the anode. This process is called intercalation. The electrons travel through the external circuit, which is where the charger does its work. Most of the capacity, often around 70 to 80 percent, goes in during this phase.

The charger cannot raise the voltage without limit. Each chemistry has an upper cutoff, commonly 4.2 V for a cobalt or nickel based cell. Beyond that the electrolyte oxidizes at the cathode, the cathode structure degrades, and metallic lithium can plate onto the anode. Plating is the most serious hazard because it consumes lithium permanently and can grow dendrites that short the cell. So when the terminal voltage reaches the cutoff, the charger switches to the second phase and holds the voltage constant.

Here is the reason the current then falls. The terminal voltage is not the same as the equilibrium voltage of the electrodes. It is the equilibrium voltage plus the current times the cell's internal resistance, plus polarization terms from slow ion transport. During the constant current phase, the resistive and polarization terms are a significant share of the measured voltage. Once the voltage is clamped, the equilibrium voltage keeps creeping up as the electrodes fill with lithium. With the total fixed, the voltage left over to drive current shrinks, so the current must decay. An engineer can think of it as a capacitor charged through a resistor, where the driving voltage across the resistor diminishes as the capacitor voltage approaches the supply. The decay is roughly exponential.

A second effect compounds this. As the graphite approaches full lithiation, the concentration gradient inside the particles flattens, and solid-state diffusion of lithium slows because there are fewer empty sites to hop into. Less lithium can be accepted per unit time at any given surface potential.

The charger ends the cycle when the current drops to a termination threshold, typically 0.05 to 0.1 times capacity per hour. Pushing past that gains little energy and adds stress. Practical chargers also taper current early when the cell is hot or cold, because plating risk rises at low temperature and degradation accelerates at high temperature. Fast charging therefore means maximizing the constant current phase, since the tail is limited by physics rather than by charger power.
