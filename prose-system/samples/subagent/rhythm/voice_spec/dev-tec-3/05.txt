A lithium-ion cell is normally charged in two stages, known as constant current followed by constant voltage. In the first stage the charger holds the current steady, typically between 0.5C and 1C, where 1C is the current that would fill the rated capacity in one hour. The cell voltage rises as it takes in charge. For a common cobalt-based cell with a graphite negative electrode, the voltage climbs from about 3.0 V when empty towards 4.2 V when full. The charger stops holding current when the terminal voltage reaches that upper limit, and this point is usually reached when the cell is roughly 70 to 80 per cent charged.

In the second stage the charger holds the voltage at 4.2 V and lets the current fall on its own. The charge is ended when the current has dropped to a small fraction of the rated value, often 0.05C to 0.1C. This stage takes much longer per unit of charge than the first, which is why a phone reaches 80 per cent quickly and then needs a further half hour or more for the remainder.

The reason is found in what the voltage measures. During charging, lithium ions leave the positive electrode, cross the electrolyte and insert themselves between the graphite layers of the negative electrode. The voltage at the terminals is the open-circuit voltage of the cell, which depends on how full the electrodes are, plus the current multiplied by the cell's internal resistance. That resistance comes from the electrolyte, the electrode interfaces and the slow diffusion of lithium within the solid particles. At 1C the resistive term might be 50 to 100 mV. As the electrodes fill, the open-circuit voltage rises, and the charger has less room beneath the 4.2 V limit for the resistive term. The current therefore has to fall to keep the terminal voltage in bounds.

Diffusion also limits the rate directly. Lithium enters the surface of each graphite particle faster than it can spread inward, so the surface becomes full while the interior is still partly empty. In the constant-voltage stage the current is largely set by how fast the lithium redistributes within the particles. This process slows as the concentration gradient flattens, so the current decays roughly exponentially.

The voltage limit is a safety and life constraint. Above about 4.2 V, the electrolyte oxidises at the positive electrode and the cell loses capacity faster. At high current and near full charge, the graphite surface can reach the potential at which lithium plates as metal instead of inserting, which can form dendrites and cause short circuits. Tapering the current is how the charger avoids both.
