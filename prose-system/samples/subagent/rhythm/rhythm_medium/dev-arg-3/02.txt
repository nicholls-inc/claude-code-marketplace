Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. The case against it is not foolish, and it deserves a fair hearing before it is answered. Children now grow up surrounded by keyboards and touchscreens, adults rarely write more than a shopping list by hand, and the hours spent drilling loops and exit strokes could, in theory, go to something more useful. Several countries have already acted on this reasoning, moving away from cursive in their curricula and leaving schools to decide for themselves. But the argument rests on a narrow idea of what handwriting is for. Handwriting is not only a way of getting words onto a page. It is a skill that shapes how children think, how they remember, and how comfortably they take part in the life of a classroom, and joined-up writing is the form of it that serves those purposes best.

Begin with the plainest practical point, which is speed and fluency. A child who prints every letter separately lifts the pencil dozens of times in a single sentence, and each lift costs a little time and a little concentration. Joined writing lets the hand move in a continuous flow, so that common letter patterns become single physical gestures rather than strings of separate decisions. Once those gestures are automatic, the child stops thinking about the pencil and starts thinking about the sentence. That shift matters enormously, because a young writer has only so much mental capacity to spend. If most of it goes on forming letters, little is left for choosing words, organising an argument, or noticing that a paragraph has wandered off its subject. Teachers see this constantly in children whose ideas are far better than their written work suggests, and the cause is often a hand that cannot keep up with the mind.

It is true that typing can also become fast, and that many adults type far more quickly than they write. But typing is not available to a seven-year-old in the way that it is to a thirty-year-old, and it does not solve the problem it is usually offered as a solution to. Most primary classrooms do not have a keyboard for every child, and exams in many systems are still written by hand well into the teens. A pupil who has never learned a fluent, legible hand will meet that fact at the worst possible moment, under time pressure, when marks depend on being read. The practical argument for joined writing is therefore partly an argument about fairness, since children from homes without devices, or without much encouragement to practise, rely on school to give them this skill.

The second argument concerns what happens in the brain when a child writes by hand. Research here should be described with some care, because the field is not as settled as enthusiasts sometimes claim. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that forming letters by hand engages the child in a way that tapping a key does not. Studies comparing handwriting with typing in young children have found that handwriting practice tends to improve letter recognition and to leave a stronger trace in memory, probably because the movement itself carries information about the shape of the letter. Studies of older students taking notes have suggested that writing by hand pushes them to summarise rather than transcribe, which tends to produce better understanding. None of this proves that cursive in particular is magic. It does support the more modest claim that the hand is a good teacher, and that a school which takes handwriting seriously is doing more than preserving a tradition.

Why joined-up writing rather than careful print, then? Some defenders of print point out that it is closer to the letters children see in books, that it is easier to learn, and that many adults eventually settle into a half-printed, half-joined style of their own anyway. These points have force, and they explain why the best programmes begin with print in the earliest years. But they do not make the case for stopping there. Joining letters brings real advantages that print lacks. Because the pencil stays on the page, the spaces between words become clearer, and the problem of reversed letters is reduced, since many of the shapes children confuse, such as b and d, are formed with very different movements when written in a joined style. Teachers of children with dyslexia and related difficulties have long argued that the rhythm of a continuous script can help with spelling, because the word is learned as a single motion rather than a heap of parts. This is not true for every child, and some find joining hard, but the benefit is real for enough of them that dropping the teaching altogether would be a loss.

There is also the matter of what we owe to reading. A literate adult needs to be able to read handwriting, not only produce it. Letters, notes from grandparents, comments written in the margin of an essay, historical documents, a teacher's feedback on a piece of work, and the messages people leave on the kitchen table are all handwritten, and a great deal of it is joined. A child who is never taught the joined forms will find all of it harder to decipher. This is a small thing in any one case and a large thing across a lifetime. We would not dream of teaching children to read only the typefaces found on screens, and the same logic applies to the written word as people actually produce it. Cursive is, in the end, a form of literacy, and there is something faintly absurd about a society that treats the ability to read a handwritten letter as optional.

The strongest objection is about opportunity cost. The school day is short, the curriculum is crowded, and every minute spent on handwriting is a minute not spent on reading, mathematics, science, or learning to type properly. This is a serious point, and anyone who defends handwriting should be honest that it has to earn its place. But the cost is smaller than critics suggest, because the best handwriting teaching is brief and frequent rather than long and laborious. Ten minutes a day of focused practice, built into lessons that are already about writing, is enough to bring most children to fluency within a few years. The time is not taken from learning to compose; it is an investment that makes composing easier. And the alternative is not a free lunch. If we stop teaching handwriting, we will still need to teach something to replace it, and keyboard skills take time to learn well. A child who is taught neither properly ends up with the worst of both worlds, writing slowly by hand and typing with two fingers.

It is also worth saying something about the less measurable side of the question. Handwriting is personal in a way that typed text is not. A child's script changes as they grow, and it becomes recognisably theirs, so that parents and teachers can often tell at a glance who wrote a note. Learning to write well, and to take a modest pride in neat work, is part of how children come to feel that their work matters and that care is worth taking over it. This is not sentimentality. Pupils who present work carefully tend to be treated as more capable by the adults who mark it, and those first impressions shape expectations for years. A decent hand is a small piece of cultural capital that costs the school little to provide and costs the child a great deal to lack.

None of this requires a return to the rigid drilling of the past, which was often joyless and sometimes cruel, as anyone who remembers being punished for a blot will agree. Good teaching of joined writing is gentle, short, and attentive to the child in front of the teacher. It allows for variation, since there is no single correct style and the aim is legibility and ease rather than a copperplate ideal. It accepts that some children, including some with motor difficulties, will need adaptations, and that a legible printed hand is a perfectly good outcome for a child who cannot manage more. And it sits alongside keyboard teaching rather than competing with it, since the two skills serve different purposes and a well-educated child should have both.

The real choice, then, is not between the pencil and the screen. It is between a school that gives every child a dependable, fluent, readable way of writing by hand, and one that leaves it to chance and to the circumstances of each home. Joined-up handwriting is quick, it supports thinking, it helps many struggling learners, and it keeps children in touch with the written record of the world they live in. Those are good reasons to keep teaching it, and the burden of proof lies with those who would drop it. Until they can show that something better will take its place, primary schools should carry on putting a pencil in the hand of every seven-year-old and teaching the letters to join.
