In the late summer of 1854, a terrible illness descended on Soho, a crowded district of London. It was cholera. The disease turns the body inside out: violent vomiting, torrents of watery diarrhoea, and a thirst that cannot be quenched. A healthy person could be dead within a day. Within three days of the outbreak's start, more than a hundred people in the streets around Broad Street had died. Within a week, hundreds more.

Nobody knew what caused it. The prevailing belief among doctors and officials was the miasma theory: that diseases spread through "bad air," foul vapours rising from rotting waste and filthy ground. London in 1854 certainly stank. It had no modern sewers, and cesspools overflowed beneath cellars and floorboards. The theory seemed common sense. It was also wrong.

John Snow, a physician who lived a short walk away, did not believe it. Snow was a careful, sober man, a teetotaller and vegetarian who had made his name pioneering anaesthesia, and he had even given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. Years earlier, during an earlier cholera epidemic, he had noticed something that bothered him. The illness attacked the gut first. If people were breathing in poison, why did the symptoms start in the stomach and bowels rather than the lungs? Snow suspected that the disease was swallowed, carried in something people drank or ate, and that the water supply was the likeliest culprit.

Now he had a chance to prove it. He did what few doctors then bothered to do. He went door to door.

Snow obtained the list of the dead from the local registrar and began visiting the families. He asked a simple question over and over: where did you get your water? Most households in the area drew it from a public pump on Broad Street, a hand-operated well that supplied water to hundreds of people. The pattern emerged quickly. Death after death clustered around that pump.

Snow also found the exceptions, and these proved as valuable as the rule. A workhouse nearby housed hundreds of poor residents, yet few of them died. It turned out the workhouse had its own private well. A brewery on Broad Street employed seventy workers, and not one of them fell ill. The brewers were given a daily allowance of beer, and they had little interest in the pump water. There was also a puzzling case far from Soho: a widow in Hampstead, miles to the north, died of cholera. Snow tracked down why. She had once lived in the neighbourhood, and she so loved the taste of the Broad Street water that she had it carried to her home every day in a large container. Her niece, who drank from the same bottle, died too.

Snow drew a map. On a plan of the district, he marked each death with a small black bar stacked at the address where the victim lived. The bars piled up thickest around the Broad Street pump, like a stain spreading outward from a single point. The map did not itself prove the cause, but it made the pattern impossible to ignore. It remains one of the most celebrated images in the history of public health.

On 7 September, Snow took his evidence to the Board of Guardians of St James's Parish, the local authority. He asked for one thing: remove the handle from the pump. Without a handle, no one could draw water. The guardians were sceptical. They were not persuaded by his argument, but the case was strong enough that they agreed to act. The handle came off the next day.

The outbreak was already fading by then, and some have argued that it would have burned out regardless, since many residents had fled the area. Snow himself admitted as much. But he was not finished. Investigating further, he found that a baby living at 40 Broad Street had died of cholera just before the outbreak began. The family's cesspool lay only a few feet from the well, and the mother had washed the infant's soiled nappies in water that then leaked into the ground. The well, it appeared, had been contaminated by sewage from a single sick child.

The authorities still resisted his conclusions. An official inquiry rejected the idea that water spread cholera, and the miasma theory stayed in favour for years. Snow died in 1858, aged only forty-five, never knowing he would be proved right. It took the later work of others, including the discovery of the cholera bacterium, to confirm what he had shown.

Today Snow is considered a founder of modern epidemiology. His method was plain. He counted the dead. He asked questions. He looked for what the unaffected had in common, and for what the affected shared. A small act with a pump handle came to stand for a larger idea: that disease can be traced, and stopped, by paying attention to patterns.
