A lithium-ion cell is charged with a two-stage procedure called constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV), and charging is the reverse of discharge: an external supply forces electrons through the outside circuit into the negative electrode, usually graphite, while charge-balancing lithium ions leave the positive electrode, typically a layered metal oxide, travel through the electrolyte and separator, and insert (intercalate) between the graphite layers. The cell's open-circuit voltage rises with its state of charge, from roughly 3.0 V when empty to 4.2 V when full for common chemistries.

In the first stage the charger holds the current constant, often between 0.5C and 1C, where 1C is the current that would fill the cell in one hour, and it raises its output voltage as needed to push that current through. The cell's terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance, plus polarization terms. Internal resistance comes from ohmic losses in the electrodes, electrolyte and contacts, while polarization comes from charge-transfer kinetics at the particle surfaces and from concentration gradients of lithium in the electrolyte and inside the solid particles. As charge accumulates, the open-circuit voltage climbs and the terminal voltage follows it up, and most of the capacity, around 70 to 80 percent, goes in during this stage.

When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, about 4.2 V, the charger switches to the second stage and holds voltage constant. The limit is firm because higher voltages oxidize the electrolyte, destabilize the cathode, and encourage lithium metal to plate on the anode, which shortens life and creates a safety hazard. With voltage clamped, the current is no longer set by the charger but by the gap between the clamp voltage and the cell's rising open-circuit voltage, divided by the resistance. As the cell fills, that gap shrinks, so the current decays roughly exponentially, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor. Charging is declared finished when the current falls to a cutoff, commonly 0.05C to 0.1C.

The slowdown has a physical cause beyond this circuit picture: near full charge the graphite has few empty sites left near its surface, and lithium must diffuse inward through the solid particles, which is slow, so the surface saturates well before the particle interior does. The surface stoichiometry, which controls the voltage, therefore runs ahead of the bulk average, and the cell reaches its voltage limit while still holding unfilled capacity in the particle cores. The constant-voltage stage lets that lithium redistribute, and the shrinking current reflects diffusion catching up. Charging faster at this point would push the surface beyond its safe potential, so the taper is the cost of staying inside the voltage limit while the last of the capacity fills.
