**Why the world's timekeepers are giving up on the leap second**

A leap second is an extra second occasionally added to the world's official clock time to keep it in step with the Earth's rotation.

The need arises because we keep time in two ways. Traditionally, a day is one turn of the Earth, and a second is a fixed fraction of it. Since the 1960s, however, the second has been defined by atomic clocks, which are steady to within a billionth of a second a day. The Earth is not so reliable. Its spin is gradually slowing, mainly because of the drag of the Moon's tides, and it also speeds up and slows down unpredictably as the planet's molten core, oceans and ice sheets shift.

The result is that atomic time and "Earth time" slowly drift apart. In 1972 it was agreed that whenever the gap approached nine-tenths of a second, an extra second would be inserted into Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the standard from which every country's civil time is set. On those occasions the last minute of 30 June or 31 December lasts 61 seconds, and clocks read 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. This has happened 27 times, most recently at the end of 2016.

For most people, the adjustment passes unnoticed. For computers, it is a recurring hazard. Software generally assumes that every minute has 60 seconds, and leap seconds cannot be programmed in far ahead, because the Earth's behaviour allows only about six months' notice. The 2012 leap second knocked out websites including Reddit and delayed Qantas flights when its booking system failed. Another, in 2016, disrupted part of the internet company Cloudflare's service. Technology firms have invented their own workarounds, such as "smearing" the extra second across several hours, but these differ from company to company, so that systems briefly disagree about the time. Financial markets, telecoms networks and satellite navigation all depend on that agreement.

A new worry sealed the argument. The Earth has lately been spinning slightly faster, raising the prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second would be removed. That has never been tried, and engineers fear much software would not cope.

In November 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body of national governments that oversees the world's measurement units, voted to stop the practice by 2035. The International Telecommunication Union, which regulates the broadcasting of time signals, endorsed the decision the following year. The delay was partly a concession to Russia, whose satellite navigation system is built around leap seconds and needs time to adapt.

Clocks and the Earth will then be allowed to drift apart, probably by about a minute over the next century. Metrologists have yet to decide how, or whether, that larger gap will eventually be corrected.
