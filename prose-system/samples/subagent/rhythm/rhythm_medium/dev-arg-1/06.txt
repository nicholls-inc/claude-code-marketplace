Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon. For most of the last seventy years, planners across the country have told developers exactly how many parking spaces a building must include, based on formulas that few people ever stopped to question. A restaurant needs so many spaces per thousand square feet, an apartment building needs so many per unit, and a medical office needs more still. The numbers look scientific, but they were largely borrowed from one another and loosely tied to surveys of suburban sites at peak demand. The result is a built environment shaped less by what people want than by what a rulebook demands, and the costs of that rulebook are paid by almost everyone.

The most direct cost is money. A single surface parking space can cost several thousand dollars to build, and a space in an underground garage can run to tens of thousands. Developers do not absorb these costs out of generosity. They pass them along in higher rents and higher purchase prices, which means that a tenant who owns no car still pays for parking she will never use. Economists who have studied the question estimate that mandates can raise the cost of housing by a meaningful margin, and the effect is worst for the smallest and cheapest units, which are the ones that struggle to pencil out when every apartment must come with a space attached. If a city says it wants more affordable housing, it is working against itself when it also requires developers to pour concrete for empty cars.

The second cost is land. Every parking space occupies a fixed number of square feet that could have held a shop, a home, a park, or a workshop. In many commercial districts, parking now covers more ground than the buildings it serves, which pushes destinations farther apart and makes walking an unpleasant gamble across open asphalt. Spread-out buildings mean longer trips, and longer trips mean more driving, so the mandate that was supposed to accommodate cars ends up creating the need for them. A downtown that devotes a third of its land to storing vehicles is a downtown with less tax base per acre, and cities that care about their budgets should notice that dense, walkable blocks tend to generate far more revenue than the same acreage covered in parking lots.

The strongest objection is that without mandates, developers will build too little parking and the surrounding streets will fill with cars. This worry deserves a serious answer, but it misunderstands what repeal actually does. Eliminating a minimum does not forbid parking; it simply lets the builder and the market decide how much is worth providing. A developer who is financing a suburban shopping center will still build plenty of spaces, because customers expect them and lenders ask about them. What changes is that a developer near a train station, or building housing for students and seniors, is no longer forced to build spaces that will sit empty. Cities that have already made the change, from Buffalo to Minneapolis to a growing list of others, have not seen the chaos that critics predicted, and most new projects there still include a sensible amount of parking.

Where spillover onto public streets does become a real problem, the better remedy is to manage the curb rather than to force private owners to oversupply. Curb space is a valuable public asset that cities currently give away almost for free, and that is why it is always crowded. Metered pricing that rises with demand, residential permit districts, and time limits can keep a few spaces open on every block without requiring a single mandated stall. Several cities have shown that when the price is set well, drivers adapt, and the revenue can be returned to the neighborhood in the form of better sidewalks and transit. This approach treats parking as a service to be priced and managed, which is how we treat almost every other scarce resource.

There is also a matter of fairness and freedom. Minimum requirements quietly assume that everyone owns a car and wants to pay for its storage, when a large share of households, especially lower-income ones, do not. They also freeze the use of old buildings, since a corner shop or a small apartment conversion that could never meet modern ratios is simply illegal to open. Many of the most beloved streets in older cities would be impossible to build today, and their charm exists precisely because they predate the formulas. Removing mandates lets neighborhoods grow and adapt the way they once did, one modest project at a time.

None of this means cars are the enemy or that parking has no place. It means the decision about how much to build should rest with those who pay for it and use it, not with a table in a zoning code written for a different era. Cities that repeal their minimums lose very little, since the market will keep providing parking where it is truly wanted. What they gain is cheaper housing, more flexible land, and streets that are shaped by people rather than by ratios. That is a trade worth making, and the sooner it is made, the sooner those benefits begin to compound.
