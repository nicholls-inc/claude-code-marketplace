Apollo 13: Failure and Return

On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 2:13 in the afternoon, Eastern time. It was the third mission meant to land people on the Moon, and by then the public had grown a little bored with the idea. Two crews had already walked on the lunar surface, and the networks had declined to broadcast the new crew's television transmissions. The men aboard were Commander James Lovell, on his fourth spaceflight, Command Module Pilot Jack Swigert, and Lunar Module Pilot Fred Haise. Swigert was a late addition. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been grounded just days before launch after exposure to German measles, and Swigert, the backup, took his place after a frantic few days of training.

The first two days passed without trouble. The crew coasted toward the Moon in the combined spacecraft: the Command Module, which they called Odyssey, and the Lunar Module, named Aquarius, which was to carry two of them to the surface. Behind them both sat the Service Module, a cylindrical equipment section that supplied the Command Module with electrical power, oxygen, and water.

On the evening of April 13, about 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks in the Service Module. The tanks held supercooled liquid oxygen and hydrogen, and stirring prevented the contents from stratifying and giving inaccurate readings. Swigert flipped the switch at 9:07 p.m. Houston time. Roughly 90 seconds later, there was a loud bang, and the spacecraft shuddered.

Lovell heard it and felt it. Swigert reported, in words that became famous in slightly altered form, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it. Warning lights flickered. Voltage on one of the main electrical buses had dropped, and the pressure in one oxygen tank read zero. Soon the second tank was leaking too. Through a window, Lovell saw gas venting into space, a pale stream flowing out from the side of the Service Module.

The cause, established later, lay in events years earlier. The oxygen tank had been dropped slightly during handling at the factory, damaging an internal tube. Then a testing procedure on the launch pad had left heater thermostats fused shut, so when the stirring fans and heaters ran in flight, wiring with damaged insulation sparked inside a tank full of pure oxygen. The resulting fire burst the tank and blew a panel off the side of the Service Module, wrecking the neighboring tank as well.

In Houston, flight controllers watched their data in confusion at first, suspecting instrument faults. Flight Director Gene Kranz and his team slowly realized the readings were real. The Command Module was losing its oxygen and, with it, its fuel cells, which produced electricity and drinking water by combining oxygen with hydrogen. Without them, Odyssey had only its batteries, meant for the final descent. The mission to land on the Moon was over. The question now was whether the men could come home at all.

Mission Control made a decision that changed everything: use the Lunar Module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, power, and water, intended to keep two men alive for two days on the Moon. Now it had to sustain three men for about four. The crew moved into it and shut down Odyssey almost completely to save its batteries for reentry.

Next came the problem of trajectory. Apollo 13 was on a "free return" path that would loop around the Moon and bring it back toward Earth, but it had been adjusted for a lunar orbit, and the current course would miss Earth by a wide margin. Controllers weighed options, including turning around immediately, but the damaged Service Module's main engine could not be trusted. Instead they used Aquarius's descent engine, built for landing on the Moon, to speed the return. The first burn, after the craft had swung around the far side of the Moon, shortened the trip home. A second, later burn corrected the course further. Lovell, Haise, and Swigert, passing farther from Earth than any humans before or since, saw the Moon's far side up close and had little time to appreciate it.

Then the conditions inside the spacecraft turned miserable. To conserve power, nearly everything was switched off, including the heat. The cabin temperature fell to near freezing, and condensation beaded on the walls. The crew slept little. Haise developed a serious kidney infection. They drank very little water, rationed to save the supply needed to cool Aquarius's electronics, and Haise and Lovell lost significant weight.

A more urgent problem was carbon dioxide. The Lunar Module's scrubbers used lithium hydroxide canisters to remove the gas from the air, but they were designed for two men for a short stay. As the crew breathed, carbon dioxide climbed toward dangerous levels. The Command Module had spare canisters, but they were square, and the Lunar Module's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers gathered a pile of the same materials the crew had aboard: cardboard, plastic bags, duct tape, a spacesuit hose. They built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew, who assembled it. The carbon dioxide level dropped. The improvisation is often called the mailbox.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the grounded pilot, worked in a simulator at Houston with a team to devise a procedure for restarting Odyssey from its near-dead state. The spacecraft had never been meant to power up after a shutdown like this, using so few amp-hours. Their written procedure, tested and refined on the ground, was read to the crew as they approached Earth.

On April 17, the crew prepared for reentry. First they jettisoned the Service Module. As it drifted away, they saw for the first time how much damage there was: an entire panel missing and the side of the module scorched and ripped open. Photographs went to the ground. Then they powered up Odyssey, left Aquarius behind, and released the Lunar Module, which had saved their lives, to burn up in the atmosphere.

Reentry brought a blackout in radio communications, normally about three minutes long. This time it stretched past that, and Houston waited, calling the crew and hearing nothing. The silence lasted longer than expected, because the trajectory was a little shallower than usual. Four minutes. Then, at last, Swigert's voice came through: "Okay, Joe." Parachutes bloomed on the television pictures from recovery ships. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific, near Samoa, at 12:07 p.m. Houston time on April 17, and the crew was picked up by the USS Iwo Jima.

The investigation that followed, led by a review board, traced the failure to the oxygen tank and the design and testing history behind it. NASA redesigned the tanks, removed the fragile wiring, and added a third oxygen tank on later missions. The Apollo program continued, and several more crews landed on the Moon.

Apollo 13 has endured in memory partly because of what it was not: a failure with a tragic end. NASA's own phrase for it was "a successful failure." The mission did not reach the Moon, but it showed what a disciplined team on the ground, working with three calm men in a crippled spacecraft, could do under pressure. Within days, a public that had lost interest in spaceflight was watching with its breath held.
