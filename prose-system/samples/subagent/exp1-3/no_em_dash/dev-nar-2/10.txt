The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. It was the third lunar landing attempt, and by then the public had grown a little bored. Apollo 11 had made the whole idea seem routine. Television networks did not even bother to carry the crew's broadcast live on the night that mattered most.

The commander was Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights and the man who had circled the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise, a rookie in space, would pilot the lunar module. The third seat had been meant for Ken Mattingly, but days before launch doctors worried he had been exposed to German measles, and the backup, Jack Swigert, took his place on short notice. Swigert had trained for the role of understudy and now found himself in the starring one. The command module was named Odyssey. The lunar module, the fragile craft built to carry two men to the surface, was named Aquarius.

For two days the flight went smoothly. On the evening of April 13, the crew finished a television broadcast, showing viewers around the cabin, and signed off. About ten minutes of normal work remained before they could rest. Mission Control asked Swigert to flip a routine switch to stir the supercold liquid oxygen in the tanks of the service module, the cylindrical section behind the command module that held its power and air supply.

He flipped the switch. Moments later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Warning lights lit up. Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas streaming into space. "Houston, we've had a problem," he said, in one of the most famous understatements in history.

The cause lay in the past. Oxygen tank number two had been dropped slightly during handling at the factory, years earlier. Later, during ground testing, its heater had been run at a voltage it was never meant to handle, which cooked the wire insulation without anyone noticing. When Swigert stirred the tank, a spark leapt across the bare wires and the oxygen caught fire. The tank burst, and the blast tore away a panel on the side of the service module and crippled the neighboring tank. The spacecraft was about 200,000 miles from Earth, and it was leaking the oxygen that fed both its air supply and its electricity.

Within minutes it was clear that the command module was dying. Its fuel cells needed oxygen to make power, and the oxygen was vanishing. Landing on the Moon was no longer a question. The only question was whether the three men could get home at all.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz gathered his teams and set a tone that matched the crisis: keep calm, think carefully, and work the problem. The idea that saved the crew was bold. Aquarius, the lunar module, was a complete spacecraft in its own right, with its own power, oxygen, and engine. If the astronauts moved into it and used it as a lifeboat, they might survive until Earth drew near. No one had ever planned for the lunar module to support three men for four days. It had been built for two men and about two days.

The crew shut down Odyssey to save its remaining battery power for the final descent, then crowded into the lunar module. Then came the matter of the path. The spacecraft was on a trajectory that would have swung it around the Moon and returned it to a point far from Earth's atmosphere, but not close enough to hit the narrow window for reentry. Controllers decided to continue around the Moon, using its gravity as a slingshot, and fire the lunar module's descent engine afterward to speed up the return. The burn went well. As they passed behind the Moon, out of radio contact, the crew looked down on the lunar surface they would not walk on. Later they would reach a distance of roughly 248,000 miles from Earth, farther than any human beings had traveled before or since.

Surviving the trip was a separate struggle. The most urgent danger was invisible. Three men breathing in a craft meant for two caused carbon dioxide to build up in the cabin, and the lunar module's filters began to fill. Spare filters existed in the command module, but they were square, while the lunar module's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the same sort of items the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard, a spacesuit hose, and duct tape, and worked out how to join them into a makeshift adapter. They read the instructions to the astronauts over the radio, step by step. The crew built it, fitted it, and the carbon dioxide level fell.

Then came the cold. With power shut down to stretch the batteries, the cabin temperature dropped toward freezing. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and the men grew dehydrated. Condensation dripped from the walls. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. Sleep was close to impossible. Lovell later described the lunar module as a refrigerator that they lived in for days.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the man who had been grounded, was working in a simulator on the ground. His task was to devise a way to bring Odyssey back to life from a state of near total shutdown, using as little power as possible. A normal startup would drain the battery long before the spacecraft touched the ocean. He and the engineers wrote a new procedure, tested it, and read it to the crew when the time came.

On the way home the spacecraft drifted slightly off course, and a final correction was needed. With the guidance systems powered down and the windows fogged by debris, the crew had no easy way to aim. Lovell lined up the Earth in the window using the line between its day and night sides, and Haise ran the burn by hand. It worked.

About four and a half hours before landing, the crew prepared to separate. First they discarded the service module, and as it drifted away they saw what the explosion had done. An entire panel was gone, and the side of the module was torn open, trailing debris. Lovell reported that a whole side of the spacecraft was missing. The crew now knew exactly how close they had come.

Then they moved back into Odyssey, climbed into the cold cabin, and powered it up using the new procedure. They cast off Aquarius, which had served as their lifeboat, and watched it fall away. Nobody could be sure that the heat shield of the command module had survived the blast. If it had cracked, there was no way to know until reentry.

On April 17, the capsule plunged into the atmosphere. Communications blackout during reentry normally lasts about three minutes. This time it stretched longer, and the silence in Mission Control grew heavier with each second. Then, after nearly a minute beyond the expected time, Swigert's voice came through. Parachutes bloomed against the sky, and Odyssey settled into the Pacific Ocean near Samoa. The crew was picked up by the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima, and Lovell, Swigert, and Haise were home, tired, thin, and alive.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, and NASA later called it a successful failure. The mission was a reminder that the skill of the people on the ground, and the calm of the people in the cabin, could turn a disaster into a rescue. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tanks and their wiring before the next flight, and Apollo continued, with six more missions, five of them landing on the Moon. But the story that people remember is the one in which three men in a cold, crowded lunar module, guided by thousands of voices and a roll of duct tape, found their way back.
