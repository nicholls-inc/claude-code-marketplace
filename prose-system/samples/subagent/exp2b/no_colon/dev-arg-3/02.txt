**Keep the Joins**

Every few years a familiar proposal returns. Typing has won, the argument goes, so primary schools should stop teaching children to join their letters. Finland dropped compulsory cursive in 2016. Several American states have wavered and then reversed course. Teachers are told that time is short and that the curriculum is crowded. I think schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case does not rest on nostalgia. It rests on what the skill does for children's hands, their thinking and their access to the written world, and on how little it costs to teach well.

I will start with the strongest version of the opposing view, because it deserves a fair hearing. Most adults now write very little by hand. Children will spend their working lives at keyboards and on touchscreens, and a school day is finite. Every minute spent looping an "l" is a minute not spent on reading, number work or coding. Many adults abandon cursive anyway and settle into a private hybrid of print and scrawl. If the end state is a personal mess, why spend years on a formal model?

The first answer is that the end state is not the point. Learning a formal model is how a child arrives at any fluent hand at all. Children who are taught no joined style do not magically develop a fast, legible alternative. Many of them stay slow, stopping and restarting the pen for every letter. Print is made of separate strokes, each one a fresh start, and each lift of the pen is a small interruption. A joined hand turns a word into a single movement. The pen travels forward, the rhythm of the word is built into the motion, and speed comes from the flow rather than from rushing. A child who writes fluently has spare attention for what the sentence is saying. A child who struggles to form each letter spends that attention on the pencil.

This matters because writing is where children first learn to organise thought. Research on handwriting and composition consistently finds that when letter formation becomes automatic, the quality and length of children's writing improve. The mechanical load falls, and working memory is freed for ideas, vocabulary and structure. Joined writing is not the only route to automaticity, but it is a well-tested one, and it offers a particular advantage. It makes the speed-and-legibility trade-off easier to win. Fast print tends to collapse into a jumble of disconnected marks, while fast joined writing tends to stay recognisable because the connections hold the letters in order.

There is also evidence that writing by hand does something typing does not. Studies comparing note-taking by pen with note-taking on laptops have repeatedly suggested that handwriters process and condense what they hear, while typists transcribe. Work with young children has found that drawing letters by hand activates reading-related regions of the brain more strongly than typing or tracing does. Some of this research is contested, and I would not claim that cursive in particular unlocks the brain. But the broad pattern is hard to dismiss. The hand is not just a slow keyboard. Forming a letter requires a child to recall its shape from memory rather than recognise it among a row of keys, and that act of recall supports learning to read and spell. Joined writing adds a further benefit for spelling, because words are written as units. A child who has written "ing" as one continuous gesture a thousand times has a motor memory of the pattern that a typist, pressing three separate keys, does not.

Children with certain learning differences are often the strongest argument for teaching joined writing. Many teachers who work with dyslexic pupils favour a continuous hand because it reduces reversals. In print, "b" and "d" are mirror images that differ only by which side the bowl sits on, and a child who muddles them can do so for years. In a joined style the entry and exit strokes give each letter a distinct start and a built-in direction, so the two shapes feel different in the hand. The same logic helps children with motor-planning difficulties, for whom a single fluid movement can be easier to organise than a series of precise stops. If we drop joined writing, we remove a tool that some of our most vulnerable learners find useful, and we do it on behalf of a majority who would manage without.

Then there is the plain matter of access. A child who cannot read cursive cannot read a grandparent's birthday card, a handwritten recipe, a teacher's marginal comment or a historical document. This is not a trivial loss. Archives, family letters, census returns and wartime diaries are written in joined script, and a generation that cannot decipher them will depend on transcribers. Teaching children to write cursive is the simplest way to teach them to read it, because the two skills reinforce each other. Reading without writing would mean treating joined script as a foreign alphabet, to be learned later or never, and that is a strange thing to do to a living part of our written culture.

The practical objection is time, and here I think the critics overstate their case. Teaching joined handwriting well does not require hours a day. A short, regular session in the early years, say ten minutes a few times a week, is enough to introduce the joins and build the habit. The cost is small compared with the work schools already spend on spelling, grammar and phonics, and the payoff compounds across every subject in which a child has to write. The real waste is the opposite one. Schools that teach no consistent style, or teach it in an unstructured way, often leave children to invent a hand for themselves in the later years, when bad habits have already set. Fixing a clumsy pencil grip at eleven is far harder than teaching a good one at six.

I should also address the worry that cursive is an old-fashioned imposition that serves tradition more than children. It is true that joined handwriting has a history of being taught as a mark of good character, with rap on the knuckles for blots. That history is a reason to teach it kindly, not to abandon it. Modern practice does not demand copperplate. Most schools that teach joined writing now favour a simple, semi-joined or entry-stroke style that is quick to learn and quick to write. Children are not required to produce calligraphy. They are asked to develop a legible, personal, reasonably speedy hand, and to know how to join their letters when it helps.

Nor is this an argument against keyboards. Children should learn to touch-type, and they should learn it properly, with the same seriousness we give to any other tool they will use every day. The two skills are not rivals. Fluency with a pen supports fluency with a keyboard, because both depend on automatic recall of letter patterns, and a child who writes comfortably is more likely to compose comfortably in any medium. The real choice is not between handwriting and the future. It is between giving children one more way to express themselves and taking one away.

There is something else worth saying about the pen itself. Handwriting is the one form of writing that does not need a battery, a signal or a licence. It works in an exam hall, at a bedside, on a form in a doctor's waiting room and on a note left on a kitchen table. It is also the way most of us first leave a mark that says we were here. A signature, a shopping list, a condolence card, a line scribbled in the margin of a book, all depend on a personal hand that a child had to learn from someone. Schools are the only place where every child, regardless of home circumstances, can be sure of learning it.

So I would keep joined-up handwriting in the primary curriculum, teach it early and briefly, and teach it with care rather than rigidity. It builds fluency, supports spelling and composition, helps children who struggle with letter reversals, and opens the door to a century of handwritten history. The cost is a few minutes a week. The loss, if we abandon it, would be quiet and slow to notice, and by the time we noticed we would find it hard to bring back. A skill that is cheap to teach, useful to learn and difficult to recover is exactly the kind a school should protect.
