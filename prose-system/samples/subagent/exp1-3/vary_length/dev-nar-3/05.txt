On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter afternoon. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, a welcome relief after days of bitter cold. Children played in the streets. Workers ate lunch near the waterfront. Nobody was thinking about the enormous steel tank that loomed over Commercial Street.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It would be turned into industrial alcohol, a use that mattered a great deal in 1919, when munitions needed it and Prohibition was only days from becoming law.

The tank had never been trusted. It leaked from the day it was filled, and neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups and buckets. Rather than repair the seams, the company painted the tank brown so the stains wouldn't show. Nobody tested it properly. Nobody seems to have asked an engineer whether the steel was thick enough.

At about 12:40 p.m., the tank failed.

Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rapid popping of rivets shooting loose, followed by a low rumble. Then the walls split open. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged into the neighborhood at around thirty-five miles an hour. It was cold, thick, and heavier than water. It tore the elevated railway from its supports. It lifted a firehouse off its foundation. It crushed wooden houses as if they were boxes of matches.

Those caught in the flood had almost no chance. The molasses pinned people where they stood, and as the temperature dropped again it thickened, trapping them in place. Rescuers waded in up to their knees and found that every step cost them. Soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship joined the Boston police, the Red Cross, and local residents, digging for survivors through the sticky wreckage. Doctors treated the injured on the spot, and a makeshift hospital opened close by. It took days to recover all the bodies. Twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Among the dead were children, a teenager, and several city workers who had stopped to eat lunch.

The cleanup was grim and slow. Crews sprayed salt water from fireboats, then covered the streets in sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown until summer. For decades afterward, people said the North End smelled of molasses on hot days.

The aftermath mattered as much as the disaster. The company blamed anarchists, claiming a bomb had destroyed its tank. But a lawsuit, one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts, dragged on for years, and an auditor appointed by the court heard from hundreds of witnesses and engineering experts. His conclusion was blunt: the tank was structurally unsound. The company paid roughly $600,000 in settlements, a modest sum even then.

The consequences reached far beyond Boston. The case helped push states toward requiring that engineers and architects sign off on construction plans, and it strengthened the idea that companies must answer for the safety of what they build.
