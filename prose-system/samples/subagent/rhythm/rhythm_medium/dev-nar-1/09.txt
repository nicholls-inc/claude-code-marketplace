In late August 1854, a terrible outbreak of cholera struck Soho, a crowded and poor district of London. Cholera is an illness that causes violent diarrhea and vomiting, and it can kill a healthy adult within a day or two by draining the body of fluid. In those days almost no one knew what caused it. The prevailing opinion among doctors and officials was that disease spread through "miasma," a foul-smelling bad air rising from rotting waste, filthy streets and swampy ground. The idea of microscopic germs carrying disease from one person to another had not yet been accepted.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked nearby, did not believe the miasma theory. He had already spent years thinking about cholera, and he had published an argument in 1849 that the disease was passed on by something people swallowed, rather than by something they breathed. Snow was a respected man, known for his pioneering work in giving anesthesia to patients, including Queen Victoria during childbirth. Yet his view of cholera was considered a minority opinion at best, because he could not point to the actual cause, and he lacked decisive proof.

The outbreak around Broad Street gave him his chance. Within about ten days, hundreds of people living within a few streets of one another had died. Families fled the area, shops closed, and the neighborhood fell into dread. Snow knew the district well, and he set out to find out exactly who had been struck down and where they had lived. He went door to door, asking questions of grieving relatives and neighbors, and he obtained the list of deaths that was kept by the official registrar. Using this information, he began to see a pattern that the miasma theory could not easily explain.

Snow drew a map of the neighborhood and marked each death with a small black bar at the address where it had occurred. When the bars were stacked up along the streets, they clustered thickly around one spot, the public water pump on Broad Street. The pump was a source of drinking water for the people of the area, who drew their water by hand and carried it home. Snow noticed that most of the deaths occurred closer to this pump than to any other in the district. This was striking, but he knew that a map alone might be dismissed as coincidence, so he looked for the cases that did not fit the pattern and tried to understand them.

Some of the exceptions turned out to confirm his idea. A workhouse nearby housed more than five hundred inmates, yet very few of them died. Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well on the premises, so the residents had no need to use the Broad Street pump. Likewise, a brewery on the same street had almost no cases among its workers. The men there were given free beer as part of their pay, and the brewery also had its own well, so they seldom drank the water from the pump at all. These details suggested that the water, and not the air, was responsible.

The most haunting case was that of a woman who lived far away in Hampstead, outside the affected district. She and her niece had both died of cholera, though no outbreak existed where they lived. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and had so liked the taste of the Broad Street water that she had it delivered to her by cart every day. Her niece had drunk some of it as well. This was exactly the sort of link that Snow was looking for.

On September 7, Snow brought his evidence to the local board of guardians, the men who ran the parish. He urged them to remove the handle of the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. They were not wholly convinced, but they agreed to try it, and the handle was taken off the next day. It is often said that this action ended the epidemic, but the truth is more complicated. The outbreak had already been declining quickly, as many residents had fled or died, and the number of new cases was falling before the handle was removed. Even so, Snow's evidence made a strong case that the pump had been the source.

A local clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who had at first doubted Snow, went on to make an important contribution. Whitehead knew his neighbors and carried out his own careful inquiries. He eventually traced the beginning of the outbreak to a baby girl who had fallen ill with cholera just before it started. Her family lived at a house on Broad Street, and her soiled diapers had been washed into a cesspool that was found to be leaking close to the pump's well. Contaminated waste had apparently seeped into the water that the neighbors were drinking.

The authorities were slow to accept all this. An official inquiry decided that the evidence did not prove that the pump was to blame, and the cesspool was quietly covered over. Snow died in 1858, still not widely believed. Yet over the following years, as understanding of germs grew, his work was recognized as a landmark. He is now regarded as a founder of modern epidemiology, which is the study of how diseases spread through populations. His map and his patient reasoning showed that careful observation of ordinary facts can reveal the hidden cause of a deadly disease.
