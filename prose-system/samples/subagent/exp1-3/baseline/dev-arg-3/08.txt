**Keep the Join: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years someone declares that handwriting is finished. The keyboard has won, the argument goes, so teachers should stop spending scarce hours on looped letters and use them for coding or critical thinking. I think this is wrong, and I think the reasons for keeping joined-up handwriting in primary school are better than its defenders usually make them sound. The case does not rest on nostalgia, or on the idea that neat writing builds character. It rests on three plain claims. Handwriting is a cognitive tool that typing does not replace. Fluent joined writing is a practical skill that pays off for years. And the usual arguments against teaching it are weaker than they look.

**What the argument is, and is not, about**

First, a clarification. Nobody serious is proposing that children should stop typing. Keyboard skills belong in the primary curriculum too, and the two skills are not rivals for a fixed pool of minutes. Nor am I arguing that every child must end up with beautiful copperplate. The question is narrower: should schools explicitly teach children to join their letters, and expect most of them to write that way by the end of primary school? I say yes.

**Writing by hand does something typing does not**

The strongest argument is about learning, not appearance. When a child forms a letter by hand, she has to recall its shape from memory and then produce it through a sequence of controlled movements. Pressing a key does none of that. Every letter 'a' on a keyboard is the same press of the same key, and the motor act carries no information about the letter.

Research on this is not unanimous, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. But the pattern is consistent enough to take seriously. Studies of young children, including work by Karin James and colleagues, have found that children who practised writing letters by hand showed more adult-like brain activation when later seeing those letters than children who typed or traced them. A Norwegian study of brain activity during handwriting versus typing found richer connectivity patterns in the handwriting condition. Virginia Berninger and others have found that children who write fluently by hand tend to produce longer and better-organised compositions, partly because letter formation has become automatic and no longer eats up working memory. Some of the well-known findings on note-taking, such as the Mueller and Oppenheimer study suggesting that longhand notes aid understanding, have been challenged in replications, and I would not hang my whole case on them. But I do not need to. The claim is modest and well supported: early handwriting practice helps children learn letters and supports composition.

None of this says joined writing specifically beats printing for learning. That is a fair objection, and I will come back to it. The point for now is that the broader category, handwriting, deserves its place in the curriculum, and that makes a deliberate handwriting programme sensible. The only live question is what that programme should teach children to do.

**Why joining, specifically**

Here is where the case for the join becomes concrete. Print-style writing, where each letter is separate, is a fine start for five-year-olds. But it has a ceiling. A child who lifts her pen after every letter makes many more starts and stops, and each one is a small interruption. As the demands of school writing grow, with longer pieces, timed assessments and note-taking, the speed and ease of the writing hand start to matter.

A joined hand reduces those interruptions. Common letter strings, such as 'ing', 'th', 'ed' and 'ou', become single flowing movements and not four or five separate acts. The pen stays on the page and the hand develops rhythm. In practice, children who move to a fluent joined style often write faster without writing worse. This is the main reason the argument is practical and not sentimental: by around age eight or nine, handwriting speed starts to limit what a child can get down on paper. A pupil who writes slowly and laboriously will tend to write less, to choose simpler words she can spell and form easily, and to find extended writing tiring. That is a hidden tax on every subject that requires writing, which is nearly all of them.

The evidence that joined writing is faster than careful print is mixed, and I will not overstate it. Some studies find little speed difference, and plenty of adults write quickly in a semi-printed hybrid. But what children usually move towards, if left alone, is exactly that hybrid, and it tends to be a personal compromise worked out by trial and error. Teaching joins explicitly gives every child a reasoned starting point, and it means the child who would never have worked out an efficient hand on her own still gets one. That is an equity argument, and it matters. Children from homes full of books, pens and patient adults will pick up good habits regardless. Children from homes without them depend on school.

**Reading as well as writing**

There is a further reason that tends to get lost: joined writing is something children will need to read. Handwritten notes from teachers, family letters, historical documents, archives, a grandparent's recipe card, a signature on a form. A great deal of the written record, and a good part of everyday life, is in cursive or a cursive-influenced hand. A child who has never been taught to join letters can find it hard to decipher them. Literacy in the full sense includes reading what other people actually write, and not only what a printer produces.

This is a small point, but it is not trivial. When some US states dropped cursive from the curriculum, teachers and archivists started to notice students who could not read primary sources written in cursive. Several states have since restored it, and California made cursive instruction a requirement again in 2023. That reversal is telling. It suggests that the cost of dropping it only became clear after the fact.

**The objections**

*"It's obsolete. Adults barely write by hand."* This is the most common objection, and it contains a half-truth. Adults do type more than they write. But they still write all the time: shopping lists, forms, quick notes, cards, annotations, exam scripts. In England, public examinations are still handwritten, which means that for most young people, fluent handwriting remains a requirement of getting qualifications. And the obsolescence argument proves too much. We still teach children to do arithmetic by hand even though calculators exist, because doing it builds understanding that you then rely on. The existence of a tool does not by itself settle what should be taught.

*"The time could be better spent."* Handwriting practice does take time, but not a great deal. Short, regular sessions of ten or fifteen minutes beat occasional long ones, and the time pays back: fluent writers spend less effort on the mechanics of every later task. It is also worth asking what the alternative use of that time actually is. If the answer is more screen time, that deserves scepticism of its own.

*"Some children find joining very hard, especially those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties."* This is a real concern, and it is the objection I take most seriously. But the right response is to support those children, not to remove the skill from everyone. A good school will let a pupil who finds joining painful use print, or a keyboard, or whatever form of writing she can do fluently. The default should still be that children are taught to join, and the exceptions should be handled as exceptions. We do not abolish PE because some children are clumsy.

*"There's no proof joined writing is better than print."* Largely true, and I have already conceded it. But absence of proof of superiority is not proof of equivalence, and the practical and reading arguments do not depend on a clean experimental result. Schools make plenty of sensible decisions on the basis of reasoned judgment plus consistent weak evidence, and this is one of them.

**What good teaching looks like**

If schools are to teach joined writing, they should do it well, and that means avoiding two old mistakes. The first is rigid perfectionism: marking children down for slope or loop shapes that make no difference to legibility. The second is starting too early, before small hands have the strength and control for it. A sensible approach teaches print first, introduces joins once letter formation is secure, usually around age six or seven, and keeps the goal clear: a legible, fluent, comfortable hand. A simple, consistent style with straightforward exit strokes is better than an ornate one. Children should be allowed to develop a personal style on top of that foundation, as nearly every adult does.

**Conclusion**

The case for teaching joined-up handwriting does not depend on pretending that typing is bad or that the past was better. It depends on three modest things: handwriting helps children learn and compose, a fluent joined hand removes a bottleneck that otherwise grows as school demands increase, and cursive remains part of the written world children must be able to read. Against these stand objections that are either partly true but overstated, such as obsolescence, or real but better answered by individual support than by dropping the skill for everyone.

Primary school is where children acquire the basic tools they will use for the rest of their education. A fluent, comfortable hand is one of those tools. It costs relatively little to teach, and it is difficult to pick up reliably without being taught. Schools that drop it will save a few minutes a week, and some children, especially those with the least support at home, will pay for it later. We should keep teaching the join.
