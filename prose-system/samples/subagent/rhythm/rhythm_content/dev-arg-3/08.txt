Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it with a clearer sense of what it is for than many of them currently have, because the case rests on fluency and not on the old moral association between neat script and good character. Every few years a headline announces that cursive is dying, that children will be taught to type instead, and that the loops and flourishes of the copybook are about to join the abacus in the museum cupboard, and the usual evidence is Finland's decision in 2016 to stop requiring cursive in its national curriculum together with the American retreat that followed the Common Core standards of 2010, which left it out. The retreat has been less complete than the headlines suggest, since a number of American states have since written cursive back into their standards and England's national curriculum still expects children to begin joining their letters in Year 2. Even so, the question deserves a real answer rather than nostalgia, because time in a primary classroom is scarce and every minute spent on one skill is taken from another, and my answer is that this skill still earns its minutes.

The place to begin is with whether handwriting itself matters, since if it does not then the argument about its style is idle. A fair reading of the research says that it does, though the strongest findings are narrower than enthusiasts like to claim. Studies by Karin James and her colleagues, in which young children who had not yet learned to read either drew letters by hand or typed them, found that the handwriting group showed more activity in the brain regions later involved in reading when they saw those letters again, which suggests that forming a letter with the hand teaches the eye something that pressing a key does not. Virginia Berninger's work with school-age children found that those composing by hand often wrote longer and more fluent texts than those at a keyboard, the likely explanation being that the effort of forming a letter ties the writer's attention to the act of making language. The much-quoted finding that students taking notes longhand remember lectures better than those typing has been harder to replicate than its popularity implies, and I would not rest anything on it. What survives the scepticism is a modest and plausible claim, that writing by hand in the early years does cognitive work worth having, and that claim is enough to justify taking handwriting seriously as a subject and not a decoration.

Nobody who takes that view is obliged to defend joined writing specifically, because print is also handwriting, and this is the objection that the sceptics press hardest. If what matters is the movement of the hand and the formation of letters, then a child who prints clearly has received the benefit, and the time spent teaching the join looks like an indulgence. The honest reply begins by conceding that the evidence for cursive over print is thinner than the evidence for handwriting over typing, and that anyone who tells you that joined writing makes children cleverer is selling something. The reply continues with the plainer observation that writing is a physical activity done under time pressure, and that fluent writers, left to themselves, join. Print demands that the pen leave the page after almost every letter and find its place again, whereas a join lets the hand travel through a word in a single movement, and while the measured speed advantage is modest and not uniform across studies, it points the same way as the habit of nearly every adult who writes a great deal. The letter-by-letter rhythm of print, which is perfectly sensible for a five-year-old, is slow for a fourteen-year-old facing a three-hour examination, and most adolescents solve the problem by improvising joins of their own.

That improvisation is the heart of the matter, and it is the strongest practical argument for teaching the join at all. Almost everyone ends up with a personal hybrid of print and cursive, which is natural and nothing to lament, but a hybrid built on a sound model differs greatly from one invented under pressure by a sixteen-year-old with no guidance. A child who was shown at seven how an e connects to an r, and why the join leaves the baseline at a particular angle, has a standard to deviate from and a clear sense of what a legible compromise looks like. A child who was never shown has only habit, and habit formed by copying the quickest classmates tends to produce the cramped, slanting, half-joined scrawl that examiners complain about every summer. Legibility is not a trivial matter when the reader of a script holds the author's mark in their hands, and the schools that give a few weeks of regular practice to the join are buying insurance against a problem that costs pupils marks later.

This argument gains force from the fact that handwriting is not going away from the places where children are judged. The great majority of GCSE and A-level papers in England are still written by hand, as are examinations at many universities, and although technology may eventually change this, assessment systems move slowly and a child who starts school today will be sitting those papers in about a decade. A school that treats handwriting as obsolete is therefore betting against its own pupils' immediate interests, and the bet is not a safe one, since the children most likely to lose it are those who find writing hard to begin with. For them the case for joining is stronger still. Many specialists who work with dyslexic and dyspraxic children favour a continuous, joined style, partly because the unbroken movement gives the hand a memory of the whole word and so supports spelling, and partly because letters that begin with a common lead-in stroke are less easily reversed, so that the notorious b and d confusion has fewer chances to occur. The controlled trials behind these claims are small and I would not call the matter settled, but the practitioners' experience is consistent enough that a school dropping joined writing should at least be able to say what it proposes to do for those children instead.

The strongest argument against all of this concerns keyboards, and it should be met directly rather than waved away. Children will spend their working lives typing, and a primary school that sends them out unable to touch-type is failing them as surely as one that sends them out unable to form a legible letter. Finland's reasoning in 2016 was not foolish, and the proposal that typing deserves a more serious place in the primary curriculum seems to me correct. What does not follow is that the two skills compete in any way that requires a choice. Handwriting is learned best at five to seven, when the small muscles of the hand are developing and when the letters are being learned for the first time, whereas touch-typing is learned better a little later, at eight to ten, when hands are larger and the keyboard no longer fights the child's reach. A timetable that allows an hour a week for typing and fifteen minutes a day for handwriting is not an impossible one, and it is the sort of timetable that most good schools already manage. There is also an equity point that rarely gets made, which is that a pen and paper work in every home, whereas a keyboard and a reliable device do not, and a child who has been taught only to type has been taught a skill that some of their classmates can practise at home and they cannot.

Defenders of joined writing weaken their own case when they defend it for the wrong reasons, and the manner of teaching matters as much as the decision to teach. A regime in which seven-year-olds are marked down for the shape of their loops, or kept in at playtime until their capital G resembles the one in the scheme, produces resentment and the hands of children who write as little as they can. The sensible approach, which several English schools have adopted, is to teach the lead-in and exit strokes from the very start, so that the join arrives as an extension of letters the child already knows and not as a second alphabet to be learned in Year 3, and to keep the practice short and frequent, with ten minutes a day being worth more than an hour on Friday afternoon. It also helps to say plainly to the pupils that the goal is a hand that is quick and that others can read, so that a child who writes quickly and clearly in a style slightly different from the model is not corrected for the sake of conformity. Beauty can be left to those who enjoy it, and calligraphy clubs are a fine thing, but they belong to the school's enrichment and not to its core.

There remain a few smaller points that I would not rest the case on but that deserve mention. A child who cannot read cursive cannot read a grandparent's letter, a note left by a teacher on a margin, or the handwritten archives in which most of recorded history is kept, and a school that teaches children to read the screen but not the page has narrowed their inheritance in a way that is easy to overlook and slow to repair. A signature, too, is still the mark by which adults commit themselves to contracts and to the cheque and the form, and children who have been taught to produce a fluent, personal, joined name have something more reliable than an improvised scribble. These are real but secondary benefits, and I mention them chiefly to show that the skill leaves traces in later life that the sceptics tend to undercount.

The conclusion I reach is a modest one, and I hold it with the confidence of a position that does not depend on any single contested study. The evidence that handwriting aids early literacy is reasonably good, the evidence that fluent writers join is strong, the cost of teaching the join is small when it is taught well, and the alternative is a generation improvising its own method at the moment when speed and legibility matter most. Schools should therefore go on teaching joined-up handwriting, begin it early and gently, keep it short, judge it by whether the result can be read at speed, and leave room beside it for the keyboard, which will take its own rightful share of the child's working life without needing to be bought at the pen's expense.
