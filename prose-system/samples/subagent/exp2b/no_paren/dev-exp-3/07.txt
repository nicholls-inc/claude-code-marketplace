How Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

The puzzle

Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met. They want to talk privately, but the only line between them is one that anyone can listen to. Every word they say is overheard by a third person, Eve. It sounds hopeless. If a secret is only a secret when nobody else knows it, how can two people create one while Eve hears everything?

For most of history the answer was that they could not. To share a secret code, two people had to meet first, in a safe place, and agree on it. Armies carried codebooks. Banks sent couriers. That works for a few people, but it fails completely for the modern world, where your phone must set up a private conversation with a shop it has never dealt with, in under a second, over a network that anyone can tap.

The trick that solved this is called public-key cryptography. It rests on one simple idea: some jobs are easy to do but extremely hard to undo. Once you see that idea, the rest follows.

Easy one way, hard the other way

Think of mixing paint. If you mix yellow and blue you get green, and it takes a second. But if someone hands you the green and asks you to separate it back into the exact yellow and blue you started with, you cannot. The mixing cannot be reversed.

Now suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow. Everyone hears this, including Eve. Alice secretly picks her own private colour, perhaps red, and mixes a big batch of it with the yellow. She sends that mixture to Bob. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, perhaps blue, mixes it with the yellow, and sends his mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures travel past, but she cannot unmix them to find the private colours.

Now comes the clever step. Alice takes the mixture she got from Bob and adds her own private red. Bob takes the mixture he got from Alice and adds his own private blue. Both end up with the same final colour, because both have blended yellow, red and blue, and the order of mixing does not matter. Eve holds only the two half-mixed batches. She is missing a private colour, so she cannot reach the final shade.

Paint is only a picture. Computers cannot send paint, so they use numbers. The numbers need an operation that is easy to do and hard to undo. Here is the one that is used.

Clock arithmetic

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You count round and round, and keep only the leftover. Mathematicians call this working with a remainder. The remainder of 15 when you divide by 12 is 3.

We will use a clock with 23 hours instead of 12. Any number gets squashed onto this clock by dividing by 23 and keeping the remainder. For example, 25 lands on 2, because 25 is 23 plus 2.

The easy operation is repeated multiplication. Take the number 5 and multiply it by itself 6 times. This is written as 5 to the power of 6, and it equals 15625. Divide 15625 by 23 and you get 679 with a remainder of 8. So on our clock, 5 to the power of 6 lands on 8.

The hard direction is working backwards. If I tell you that 5 multiplied by itself some number of times lands on 8 on the 23 hour clock, and ask how many times, you have no shortcut. The answers jump around in a pattern that looks random. You would have to try 1 time, 2 times, 3 times, and so on until you hit it.

The exchange, step by step

Here is the whole conversation, with real numbers.

Step one. Alice and Bob announce two public numbers: the base, 5, and the clock size, 23. Eve hears both. Nothing is hidden yet.

Step two. Alice secretly chooses the number 6. She tells no one. Bob secretly chooses 15, and also tells no one.

Step three. Alice computes 5 to the power of 6 on the clock, which is 8, as we saw. She sends 8 to Bob. Bob computes 5 to the power of 15 on the clock, which works out to 19. He sends 19 to Alice. Eve hears both 8 and 19.

Step four. Alice takes Bob's number, 19, and multiplies it by itself 6 times, which is her secret number, keeping only the clock remainder. She gets 2. Bob takes Alice's number, 8, and multiplies it by itself 15 times, which is his secret number, again keeping the remainder. He also gets 2.

They have both arrived at 2, and neither ever said it aloud. The reason it matches is the same reason the paint matched. Alice's final number amounts to 5 multiplied by itself 15 times and then 6 times, while Bob's is 5 multiplied by itself 6 times and then 15 times. Both are 5 multiplied by itself 90 times. The order does not matter.

What does Eve have? She knows 5, 23, 8 and 19. To find the shared number she needs one of the private numbers, 6 or 15. To get one, she must work backwards from the clock result, which is the hard direction. With numbers this small she can simply try them all, and she would find the answer in moments. That is why this example is only a demonstration.

Why real versions are safe

Real systems use the same steps with enormous numbers, hundreds of digits long. A number with 600 digits has more possibilities than there are atoms in the known universe. Trying them all is not just slow, it is impossible, even if every computer on Earth worked together for longer than the age of the universe. Meanwhile Alice and Bob's own computers do their easy forward work in a fraction of a second, because there are clever ways to do repeated multiplication in a small number of steps.

Notice what this means. The strength does not come from a hidden method. The method is published in textbooks. Eve knows exactly what Alice and Bob are doing. The security comes only from the gap between easy and hard.

Why it is called public and private keys

The numbers that travel openly, 8 and 19 in our example, play the role of public keys. They are safe to announce to the entire world. The numbers kept at home, 6 and 15, are private keys, and they never leave the person who chose them.

There is a second use of the same idea. Instead of agreeing on a shared secret, you can publish a public key that works like an open padlock. Anyone can snap the padlock shut around a message, but only the owner of the matching private key can open it. A famous method called RSA works this way. Its easy but hard to undo job is multiplying two huge prime numbers together. Multiplying them is quick, but given only the answer, finding the two original primes is hopelessly slow.

What happens next

Once Alice and Bob share their secret number, they use it as the key for a fast, ordinary scrambling method, and then they chat freely. The slow, clever number work is used just once, at the start, to get the key across safely. Every time your browser shows a padlock, a conversation much like this one has already taken place in the blink of an eye.

The lesson is a surprising one. Privacy between strangers does not need a secret meeting. It needs a task that is easy to do and ruinously hard to undo, and a little cleverness about the order of mixing.
