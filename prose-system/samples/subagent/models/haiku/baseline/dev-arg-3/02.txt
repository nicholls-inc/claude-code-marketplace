Keep Teaching the Join

Every September, somewhere in a primary school classroom, a child with a newly sharpened pencil copies a row of loops and humps from the board. Most of those children will be typing within a few years. So the question people keep asking is fair: why spend scarce lesson time on joined-up handwriting when a keyboard will do the job? My answer is that primary schools should keep teaching it. The case rests less on nostalgia than on what handwriting does for a developing mind, and on what we lose when we stop.

Start with the most practical objection. Adults rarely write by hand for long stretches. When they do, it is usually a signature or a note to self. But the skill is not really about the output. It is about the process it trains. A joined hand teaches a child to produce a word as a continuous movement rather than a string of separate marks. The pen does not lift between most letters, so the child learns that letters belong to words, that a word has a shape, and that the shape is worth recognising. Anyone who has watched a child struggle with spelling will recognise the problem. A child who must stop and construct each letter often loses the word while still forming its first half.

This is not only teacher folklore. Virginia Berninger and her colleagues at the University of Washington have spent decades studying how handwriting fluency relates to writing. Their work suggests that when handwriting feels effortful, the strain competes with the higher-level work of composing sentences and developing ideas. A child who has to think about every letter has less attention for what the sentence means. Fluency frees capacity. A child with fast, automatic letter production can put more thought into content. The research is not unanimous, and the size of the effect varies with age and task. But the direction is consistent enough that dropping instruction on the grounds of irrelevance looks premature.

Second, consider reading. Karin James and colleagues at Indiana University used brain imaging to compare young children who practised new letters by hand with children who only typed them or looked at them. The handwriting group showed stronger activation in regions associated with reading. Letters learned through the hand appeared to be encoded more richly than letters seen or pressed on a key. These are small studies, and nobody should build national policy on one scanner. But the findings fit a wider intuition: motor practice helps children attach a shape to a sound, and the more of the body involved in learning a letter, the more firmly it sticks.

The skeptic will say: then teach print. Print letters are simpler, and several education systems teach them first. This is a fair point, and I concede it. Early on, a child should learn letter formation in whatever form is most legible. But print is not the destination. Print is a series of separate strokes that must be re-sequenced for every letter, and it does not teach the rhythm that connected writing demands. The leap from print to cursive is where many children stall, and it is exactly the moment that schools abandoning joins tend to lose them. If we teach joined writing only after children have formed habits in print, we make the task harder. The better approach is to introduce joins early, in a clear and consistent model, so that no child builds a second habit to unlearn later.

Third, fine motor development. Handwriting depends on small, precise movements of the hand and wrist, the same capacities used for buttons, scissors and tools. Children who practise letter formation daily are also practising control, pressure and posture. Occupational therapists use handwriting exercises for exactly this reason. Removing the lesson removes one of the few structured occasions in a school day when a child is asked to slow down and move carefully, with attention to every stroke.

Fourth, legibility and fairness. A student who writes a fluent, readable hand can get through exams, forms and everyday notes without strain. A student who writes slowly and illegibly is judged more harshly by markers, employers and classmates than their ideas deserve. Handwriting is a visible form of competence, and schools owe children a real chance to master it. Leaving it to chance at home produces the same unfair pattern we see across education. Children from households with time, calm and a parent who writes neatly get the skill. The rest do not, and the gap widens without anyone deciding that it should.

Now the counterargument deserves its strongest form. Time is finite. A primary curriculum is crowded, and every minute spent on letter joins is a minute not spent on phonics, mathematics or reading for pleasure. Some argue that keyboard skills are more useful for children who will spend their working lives on screens. This is a real trade-off, and I do not wave it away. But it is smaller than it first appears. Handwriting instruction in primary school is typically short and daily, perhaps ten to fifteen minutes. Keyboarding cannot replace it, because typing a letter does not train the motor sequence that links letters into words, and young children are not ready to type fluently anyway. Children will need the keyboard eventually, and schools can teach that too. Nothing about joined writing excludes typing. The claim is only that the first skill is worth keeping.

There is also a cultural argument. It is weaker than the others, but it is real. Handwriting is how many children first see that writing is something a person makes with their own body, not something a machine dispenses. Letters from grandparents, old diaries, parish registers and handwritten notes are part of how a literate person relates to the past and to other people. A child who cannot read cursive at all is cut off from a surprising amount of that record. This is not a reason to make cursive mandatory forever. It is a reason to think carefully about what happens when a generation grows up unable to read a handwritten sentence without help.

So what should schools actually do? Keep joined-up handwriting as a core part of the primary writing curriculum. Introduce letter formation early, using a consistent model that brings in joins as soon as children can form the basic letters reliably. Keep sessions short and regular rather than occasional and rushed. Pair handwriting practice with real writing, so children use the skill to produce sentences rather than copying endless rows of loops. Assess for legibility and fluency, not for perfection. The goal is a hand that serves thinking, not a calligraphy prize.

Teaching joined-up handwriting will not fix literacy by itself, and nobody should claim it will. But it sits in a useful place in the curriculum. It connects motor learning, reading, spelling and composition, and it gives children a skill they will use in some form for life. Removing it saves some time and loses something that is hard to recover once it is gone. Primary schools should keep it, and they should teach it well.
