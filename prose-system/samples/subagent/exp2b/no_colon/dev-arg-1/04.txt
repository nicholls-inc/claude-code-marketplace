Let Builders Decide How Much Parking Is Enough

Every city has a rule it never voted on in any meaningful sense. Somewhere in the zoning code sits a table that says a restaurant must provide one space per hundred square feet, an apartment building must provide two spaces per unit, and a medical office must provide one for every two hundred. Cities should delete that table. Minimum parking requirements are a mistake dressed as common sense, and the sooner they go, the sooner cities can become cheaper, greener and more flexible places to live.

Start with the premise behind the rules. Planners assumed that if a building did not supply enough parking, its visitors would clog the surrounding streets. That sounds plausible until you ask who knows best how much parking a building needs. The builder who risks money on the project has every incentive to get it right. A developer who builds too few spaces loses tenants. A developer who builds too many wastes capital that could have gone into homes or shops. Private actors answer to this tradeoff every day. A zoning table answers to nothing. It was copied from handbooks that drew on suburban survey data, often from a handful of sites with free parking, and then hardened into law.

The costs are real and they land on people who never asked for a parking space. A single structured space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one costs far more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents and purchase prices, so a household without a car still pays for a space it will never use. Researchers who have studied this find that bundling parking into housing raises rents noticeably and pushes lower income households toward cheaper, more crowded or more distant options. A rule that claims to serve drivers ends up taxing everyone, and it taxes the poorest hardest.

Then there is the land. Parking eats lots that could hold apartments, groceries, clinics and offices. In many downtowns the surface area devoted to storing empty cars rivals the area devoted to people. That is not just ugly. It spreads destinations farther apart, which makes walking and transit less workable and makes driving more necessary, which in turn seems to justify more parking. The requirement creates the very dependence it claims to respond to. Small businesses suffer in particular. A corner lot that could support a bakery cannot clear the parking hurdle, so the bakery never opens, or a chain with deep pockets and a big lot gets the site instead.

Defenders raise fair worries, and they deserve straight answers. The first is spillover. If a new building provides little parking, will its residents fill the street in front of the neighbors' homes? Sometimes, yes, and that is a pricing problem rather than a building problem. Cities that underprice curb space invite scarcity. The better tool is to manage the curb directly, with residential permits, metered rates that rise with demand and time limits where turnover matters. Several cities have shown that modest pricing keeps a block or two open at nearly all times. Forcing every developer to overbuild in order to protect a free public resource gets the logic backward.

The second worry is that removing minimums will leave people stranded. It will not, because removal does not ban parking. It simply lets the market supply it. Buffalo scrapped its citywide minimums in 2017 and saw developers keep building plenty of parking where it made sense, while projects that were once blocked became feasible. Minneapolis, Austin, and a growing list of other places have followed with similar changes. What these cities report is not a wave of carless chaos. It is more varied projects, more infill on odd lots, and more adaptive reuse of old buildings that could never meet modern ratios. A historic storefront can finally become a home or a cafe without a demolished neighbor to make room for spaces.

The third worry is fairness to existing residents who drive. That concern is honest, and the answer is time and choice. Most buyers and renters who want a car will still be able to find a building that offers one, and those buildings will price the space openly. Someone who values a spot can pay for it. Someone who does not can stop subsidizing it. That is a fairer deal than the current arrangement, where everyone pays the same hidden fee whatever they drive.

There is also a climate argument that needs little embellishment. Transportation is among the largest sources of emissions in most wealthy countries. Policies that mandate cheap, abundant parking tilt millions of daily decisions toward driving. Removing minimums will not end car use, but it stops the law from putting a thumb on the scale.

Reform does not need to be reckless. A city can eliminate minimums while keeping accessibility spaces required by law, setting maximums in the densest districts if it wishes, and pairing the change with better curb management and transit investment. But the default should flip. Instead of asking builders to justify building fewer spaces, cities should let them decide, and let the public see the true price.

Parking is a service like any other. Treat it as one. Cities that stop dictating how much of it everyone must buy will find that homes get cheaper, streets get livelier, and the cars that remain have somewhere to go.
