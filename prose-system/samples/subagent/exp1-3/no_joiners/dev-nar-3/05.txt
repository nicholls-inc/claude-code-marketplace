On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusual thaw. After days of bitter cold, the temperature had climbed above forty degrees. Workers ate lunch near the waterfront. Children played in the streets near Commercial Street. Towering above them stood a huge steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The tank had always been a worry. Built in 1915 in a hurry, it stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet wide. It leaked from the start, and neighbors collected the sticky drips in cans to take home. Rather than fix the problem, the company painted the tank brown so the stains would not show. The structure was never properly tested, and no qualified engineer had reviewed its design. The molasses inside was bound for distilleries that made industrial alcohol, which was used for munitions in the war and would soon be needed for liquor before Prohibition took hold.

Shortly after noon, residents heard a low rumble and a sound like machine gun fire. That was the rivets popping loose. Then the tank burst. A wave of molasses roughly twenty five feet high rushed through the neighborhood at about thirty five miles an hour. It carried a force that no one on the street could have imagined from something so ordinary.

The wave smashed buildings and flung wagons and horses aside. It tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports and nearly toppled a passing train. A firehouse was crushed, and one of the firemen inside was killed. People were swept off their feet and trapped in the thick, cold syrup, which grew stickier and heavier as it settled. Rescuers waded in but struggled to free anyone, because every movement drew them deeper. In the end twenty one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Among the dead were children, and some victims were found only days later.

The cleanup lasted for weeks. Crews hosed the streets with salt water pumped from the harbor and scattered sand and sawdust. The harbor itself ran brown until summer, and for years afterward some residents swore that the area smelled sweet on hot days.

Then came the question of blame. The company's owner, United States Industrial Alcohol, insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, a claim that suited the nervous mood after the war. Survivors and families sued, and the case became one of the first major class action suits in Massachusetts. A court appointed auditor listened to thousands of witnesses over several years. Engineers testified that the tank had been dangerously weak, with walls too thin for the load. In 1925 the auditor rejected the bomb story, and the company agreed to pay damages to the families.

The disaster left a lasting mark on how buildings are made. It helped push Massachusetts toward requiring that architects and engineers be licensed and that their plans be reviewed before construction. A sweet, strange tragedy had taught a hard lesson about corners that should never be cut.
