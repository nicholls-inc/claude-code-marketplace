A heat pump does not make heat by burning something; it moves heat that already exists, which is why it can warm your house when the air outside is colder than the air inside, and it is the main thing to understand before you compare it with a gas boiler.

Heat is not a thing that only exists above room temperature. Outdoor air at 0°C still holds a great deal of thermal energy, because it is far above absolute zero. Left alone, heat flows from hot to cold, so it will never travel from cold outdoor air into a warm house by itself. A heat pump gets round this by using a machine that pushes it the other way, in the same way a fridge pushes heat out of its cold interior into your warm kitchen. A heat pump is essentially a fridge run in reverse, with the "inside" being the outdoors and the "kitchen" being your home.

The trick is a sealed loop of refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, well below freezing. Outside, a fan pulls air across a coil containing this fluid. Because the refrigerant is colder than the air, even on a frosty day, heat flows into it and it evaporates into a gas. A compressor then squeezes that gas, and since compressing a gas heats it up sharply, the refrigerant leaves the compressor much hotter than your radiators need to be, typically 40 to 60°C. That hot gas passes through a coil inside the house, hands its heat to your heating water or indoor air, and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops its pressure, which chills it again, and the cycle repeats.

The compressor needs electricity, but it uses far less energy than the heat it delivers, a ratio engineers express as the coefficient of performance. A typical modern air-source heat pump might deliver three units of heat for every one unit of electricity, and over a whole winter a well-installed system often averages somewhere between 2.5 and 4. A gas boiler, even a good condensing one, cannot exceed about 90 to 95 percent efficiency, because it can only turn the energy in the fuel into at most the same amount of heat. A heat pump comfortably beats that because most of its output is borrowed from the outdoor air.

There are limits, and you should know them. Efficiency falls as it gets colder, because the pump has to lift heat across a bigger temperature gap; modern units keep working well below minus 10°C, but they use more electricity per unit of heat when they do. Efficiency also falls when you ask for hotter water. A heat pump is happiest producing warm water at around 35 to 50°C, which suits underfloor heating or large radiators, and less happy at the 70°C or more that older boiler systems often run at. Many homes need some radiators upsized and the building made less leaky, so a heat loss survey before you commit is worthwhile.

For your decision, the practical questions are these. How well insulated is your home? Are your radiators big enough to heat the house at lower water temperatures? What is the price of electricity compared with gas where you live, since the running cost advantage depends on that ratio against the efficiency of roughly three? And what grants or subsidies are available toward the upfront cost, which is usually higher than a new boiler? If the house is reasonably insulated, the radiators are adequate and electricity is not wildly more expensive than gas per unit, a heat pump will usually heat it comfortably and cheaply, and it will cut your carbon emissions as the grid gets cleaner. If the house is draughty and the pipework is old, fix those first, and get quotes from installers who will do a proper heat loss calculation.
