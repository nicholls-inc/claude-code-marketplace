The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of four earlier spaceflights. Fred Haise was a rookie flying as lunar module pilot. The third seat should have gone to Ken Mattingly, but days before launch doctors learned he had been exposed to German measles, and the backup, Jack Swigert, took his place. The mission was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. By then the public had grown a little bored with the idea. Networks skipped the crew's early television broadcasts. That would change within two days.

The command module was named Odyssey and the lunar module Aquarius. For the first two days everything went so smoothly that Lovell later described the flight as almost dull. The crew had left Earth orbit, docked with the lunar lander, and settled into the long coast toward the Moon. Then, on the evening of April 13, roughly fifty-six hours into the flight, Mission Control asked Swigert to flip a switch that stirred the supercold oxygen and hydrogen stored in the service module. The tanks needed regular stirring so that the readings stayed accurate.

Swigert threw the switch. Moments later the crew heard a bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights lit across the panel. Swigert reported the trouble, and Lovell repeated it in the words that became famous: "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause lay in a chain of small errors made years earlier. Oxygen tank number two had been dropped slightly during factory handling. During a ground test before launch, the tank would not drain properly, and technicians used heaters to boil off the oxygen. The heaters ran far hotter than the tank's design allowed, and the thermostat switches meant to protect them had been built for a different voltage and fused shut. The wiring insulation inside the tank was scorched without anyone noticing. When Swigert stirred the tank in space, a spark ignited that damaged insulation in the pure oxygen. Pressure rose until the tank burst, and the blast tore away a panel of the service module and crippled the neighboring tank.

At first the crew and the controllers in Houston thought they were looking at faulty instruments. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space. The oxygen was draining away, and with it went the fuel cells that made the command module's electricity and water. Within a few hours the module would be dead. The Moon landing was abandoned almost at once. The only question was how to bring three men home.

The answer was to treat the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had been built to carry two men for two days on the Moon's surface. Now it had to keep three alive for four days. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams worked in shifts around the clock. Swigert and Haise moved into the lander, and Lovell shut down Odyssey, powering it off to save what little battery power remained for the return to Earth.

Getting home was a problem of trajectory as much as survival. The ship was on a path that would have taken it past the Moon and into a high orbit around the Earth, not directly back. The controllers decided to swing the craft around the Moon, using lunar gravity as a sling, and then fire the lander's descent engine to speed the return. That burn took place about two hours after the closest approach to the Moon. Behind the Moon, at the far point of the loop, the three men were about four hundred thousand kilometers from home, farther than any human beings have ever been. The crew photographed the lunar surface passing below, a view they were meant to be studying for a landing they would never make.

Other problems followed. The lander was designed to scrub carbon dioxide from the air using filters shaped for its own system. With three people aboard, the filters began to fill, and the gas levels crept toward dangerous. The spare filters from the command module were square, and the lander's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers gathered a heap of the same items the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, hoses from spacesuits, and duct tape. They devised an adapter and read the instructions to the crew over the radio. Lovell, Haise and Swigert built it as described, and the carbon dioxide readings fell. Later the engineers nicknamed the device the mailbox.

The cold was almost as dangerous. With the power shut off, cabin temperatures sank to a few degrees above freezing. Condensation beaded on the walls. The astronauts slept little and drank little, because water had to be rationed to keep the lander's electronics cool. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. All three lost weight and grew dehydrated. They stayed in the lander, bundled in what clothing they had, and kept working.

Meanwhile, in Houston, Mattingly and a team of engineers wrestled with the reverse problem. Odyssey would need to be brought back to life before reentry, using only the small reserve of battery power, and no one had ever tried to power up the command module from a cold start in flight. The team worked through the procedure in a simulator, rewriting it again and again until it fit within the power budget, then sent the final steps up to the crew.

Another challenge came late in the flight. The ship needed a final correction to line up with the narrow corridor through which it had to enter the atmosphere. The computer guidance was shut down to save power, and the usual alignment stars were hidden by a cloud of debris around the ship. The crew steered by hand, using the sunlit edge of the Earth as a reference and a count of seconds to time the burn. The thruster firing lasted about fourteen seconds, and it worked.

On April 17, the crew separated the damaged service module. For the first time they saw how much had been lost, a whole side of the vehicle missing and wreckage trailing from it. Lovell described it in a radio call as a very sobering sight. They then moved back into Odyssey, powered it up, and released Aquarius, which had saved their lives, to burn up in the atmosphere. The command module had one more worry. No one was sure that the heat shield had survived the explosion, and the cold-soaked capsule might have problems with its parachutes.

Reentry brought a long silence. The hot plasma around the capsule blocks all radio contact, and it normally lasts about three minutes. This time it ran longer, because the ship had entered at a slightly different angle. Controllers listened to static and wondered. Then, at last, the voice of Swigert crackled through. The parachutes opened, and Odyssey settled into the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted almost six days. The crew were thin, weary and chilled, but unhurt.

Investigators later traced the accident to the tank and its heaters, and the design was changed so that no wiring would be exposed in oxygen tanks again. Lovell never walked on the Moon, but he often said that the mission was a different kind of success. It showed what teams on the ground and in space could do when everything went wrong. Apollo 13 is sometimes called a successful failure, and for good reason. Three men left for the Moon, and three men came home.
