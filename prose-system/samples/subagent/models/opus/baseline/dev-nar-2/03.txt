**The Long Way Home: Apollo 13**

By April 1970, the country had mostly stopped watching moon landings. Apollo 11 had landed the summer before and Apollo 12 had followed in November, and the third landing was starting to look like routine work. When Apollo 13 lifted off from Cape Canaveral at 1:13 p.m. Houston time on April 11, the television networks gave it modest coverage. Two days later none of them carried the crew's live broadcast from space.

There were three men aboard. Jim Lovell, the commander, was flying his fourth mission and had already circled the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise would fly the lunar module down to the surface with Lovell. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only three days before launch. Ken Mattingly, the original pilot, had been exposed to German measles, and flight surgeons were unwilling to risk him falling ill while alone in lunar orbit. Their spacecraft came in two parts. The command and service module, called Odyssey, would carry them to the Moon and back. The lunar module, Aquarius, would land at Fra Mauro, a hilly region that geologists hoped would yield rocks from deep in the Moon's past.

For two days the flight went so smoothly that the crew joked about being bored. On the evening of April 13 they finished a television tour of the spacecraft. Then, at 55 hours and 54 minutes into the mission and about 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked Swigert to run a routine chore: switch on the fans that stirred the supercritical oxygen in the service module's tanks. He flipped the switch, and a few seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder.

"Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. When the controllers asked him to repeat, Lovell answered: "Houston, we've had a problem." Warning lights came on across the panel. One of the main electrical buses lost voltage. The gauges on oxygen tank 2 read zero, and tank 1 was draining. Lovell looked out a window and saw a white cloud of gas spraying into space around the ship. "We are venting something out into the space," he reported.

The problem was worse than a gauge failure. The service module held the oxygen that the crew breathed and that fed the three fuel cells making the ship's electricity and water. Within the hour two fuel cells were dead and the third was failing. Odyssey was going to die, and it was the only part of the spacecraft with a heat shield, the only part that could bring anyone home through Earth's atmosphere.

In Mission Control, flight director Gene Kranz and his team stopped trying to save the landing and started trying to save the crew. They decided to use the lunar module as a lifeboat, an idea that had been talked about in training but never planned in detail. Lovell and Haise hurried through the tunnel into Aquarius and powered it up. In Odyssey, Swigert shut down the command module before its last reserves drained away, keeping its batteries for reentry days later. Before the computer went dark, the crew copied its navigation alignment by hand and did the arithmetic to transfer it to the lunar module's guidance system.

Aquarius had been built to keep two men alive for about two days. Now it had to keep three alive for nearly four, and it had to push both spacecraft, which together weighed far more than its engine was designed to move. The first question was the route home. Earlier in the flight Apollo 13 had left its "free return" path, the trajectory that would have swung it around the Moon and back to Earth automatically. Without a correction, the spacecraft would miss Earth altogether. Five and a half hours after the explosion, the crew fired Aquarius's descent engine, the one meant to land them on the Moon, for about half a minute to put the ship back on a course for home.

Early on April 15, Apollo 13 passed behind the Moon. For a while the crew had no radio contact, and the three men were farther from Earth than any humans before or since, about 248,000 miles away. Haise and Swigert, who had never seen the far side, took photographs of the landing site they would not reach. Lovell, who had seen it before, was thinking about the next engine burn. Two hours after they passed the Moon, the descent engine fired again for more than four minutes. The burn cut roughly ten hours off the trip and moved the splashdown point into the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships were waiting.

The trip home brought new problems. With almost every system turned off to save power, the cabin grew cold, eventually reaching about 38 degrees Fahrenheit. Water was so tightly rationed that the men became dehydrated, and Haise developed a urinary infection and a fever. They hardly slept. Frost formed on the windows and moisture collected on the walls and instrument panels, which worried everyone, since the command module's electronics would need to work when it was switched back on.

The most immediate danger was the air itself. Each breath added carbon dioxide to the cabin, and the lunar module's filters, canisters of lithium hydroxide, were not enough for three men over so many days. Odyssey carried plenty of spare canisters, but they were square and Aquarius's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground worked out an adapter from materials they knew were on board: plastic bags, the cardboard covers of flight manuals, socks, hoses from the spacesuits and a great deal of duct tape. They read the instructions up to the crew one step at a time. The improvised box, which the astronauts called "the mailbox," worked, and the carbon dioxide readings dropped back to safe levels.

There was still steering to do. The ship was drifting slightly off course, and with the guidance system unreliable, the crew had to correct it by hand. Lovell held the line between day and night on Earth, which they could see through the window, steady in his sights while the engine fired for about fourteen seconds. The method was old-fashioned, and it put them back on target.

Meanwhile, Ken Mattingly, the pilot who had been left behind, spent long hours in a simulator in Houston working out how to restart a cold, damp command module without using more electricity than its batteries held. Nobody had ever planned to shut down the command module during a flight and then bring it back. He and the engineers wrote the procedure step by step, and on the last day Swigert copied it down and carried it out.

On April 17, a few hours before reentry, the crew jettisoned the service module. As it drifted away they saw the damage for the first time: an entire side panel had been blown off. Haise said that one whole side of the spacecraft was missing. They moved back into Odyssey, which came back to life, and about an hour before entry they released Aquarius. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," a controller said.

During reentry the radio always went silent for a few minutes while the capsule plunged through a sheath of superheated air. This time the blackout lasted longer than expected, and Mission Control waited for well over a minute past the usual time with no word. Then Swigert's voice came through. Three parachutes opened, and at 12:07 p.m. Houston time Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima.

An investigation later traced the accident to oxygen tank 2. The tank had been dropped slightly at the factory, and its heater switches had been designed for the spacecraft's 28-volt power supply, not the 65 volts used by the ground equipment at the launch pad. When technicians had trouble emptying the tank after a prelaunch test, they ran its heaters for hours. The switches welded shut, temperatures inside rose far beyond the safe limit, and the insulation on the wires was badly damaged. When Swigert turned on the fans, bare wires sparked inside a tank of pure oxygen.

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon. NASA nonetheless came to call it a "successful failure," because the crew, the flight controllers and the engineers had taken a mission that was falling apart two hundred thousand miles from home and brought all three men back alive.
