In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell upon Soho, a crowded and poor district of London. The illness was cholera, which causes violent vomiting and diarrhea, drains the body of fluid, and can kill a healthy adult within a day or two. Within about ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few streets of one another had died. Many families fled. Shops closed. The living carried the dead out of houses where whole households had been taken.

At that time, almost everyone believed that cholera spread through bad air, known as miasma. The theory held that foul smells rising from rotting waste and filthy ground carried the disease. It was a sensible-sounding idea in a city that stank, but it was wrong.

One doctor doubted it. John Snow, a physician who lived and worked not far from Broad Street, had been thinking about cholera for years. He had pioneered the use of anaesthetic gas in surgery and even given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He was a careful man who liked to measure things. Snow noticed that cholera began with symptoms of the gut, not the lungs. If bad air were the cause, he reasoned, victims would first suffer in their breathing. Instead, the sickness seemed to attack the stomach and bowels, which suggested that people were swallowing something. He had published this idea in 1849, but few took it seriously.

The Broad Street outbreak gave him a chance to test it. Snow knew the neighbourhood, and he went door to door. He asked who had died, where they lived, and what they had been drinking. In those days, most Londoners had no water pipes in their homes. They collected water from public pumps, and the quality of that water varied widely. Snow began to mark each death on a street map, drawing a small black bar at the address of every victim. As the bars piled up, a pattern emerged. The deaths clustered thickly around the pump on Broad Street.

Snow did not stop at the pattern. A cluster could be a coincidence, and he knew that critics would say so. He looked for the exceptions, which often teach more than the rule. There was a workhouse nearby with hundreds of inmates, yet very few of them died. Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped almost untouched. Its workers were given free beer, and they also had their own supply of water. They simply never drank from the pump.

Then there were the strange cases of people who lived far from Broad Street but still died. One was a widow in Hampstead, several miles away. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and loved the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it sent to her every day. Her niece, who visited and drank the same water, died too. Both had no other link to the outbreak.

By the evening of the seventh of September, Snow was confident enough to go to the local board of guardians, the officials who ran the parish. He urged them to remove the handle from the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. They agreed, and the handle was taken off the next day. The pump stood useless, and the neighbours had to walk further for water.

It is tempting to say that this single act ended the epidemic, but the truth is more complicated. The outbreak had already been declining before the handle was removed, partly because so many people had died or fled. Snow himself acknowledged this. Still, his evidence went far beyond one gesture with a pump handle.

The cause was found later. A Reverend Henry Whitehead, who at first doubted Snow, did his own investigation of the neighbourhood. He discovered that a baby living at number 40 Broad Street had died of what looked like cholera shortly before the outbreak. The mother had washed the infant's soiled cloths and thrown the dirty water into a cesspool just a few feet from the pump's well. The cesspool was leaking. The well had been dug close by, and its water was contaminated. Whitehead's discovery helped confirm Snow's argument.

Even so, the official bodies did not at once accept the idea. An inquiry concluded there was not enough proof, and many clung to miasma for years. Snow died in 1858, never knowing how completely he would be vindicated. Later in the century, scientists identified the cholera bacterium, and the water-borne explanation became the foundation of modern public health.

Snow's lasting contribution was a way of thinking. He counted cases, mapped them, asked questions of ordinary people, and followed the evidence even when it contradicted what the experts believed. That method, now called epidemiology, still guides how we trace and stop outbreaks today.
