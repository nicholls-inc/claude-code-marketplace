Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and who share no password, trying to agree on a secret while a stranger named Eve listens to every word they exchange. At first this sounds impossible, because anything Alice tells Bob, Eve hears as well. For most of history the only way around the problem was for the two people to meet beforehand and swap a secret key in person, which is how spies and armies operated for centuries. Public-key cryptography, discovered in the 1970s, removed that need by using a clever trick of mathematics: some operations are easy to do but extremely hard to undo, and two people can use such an operation to build a shared secret in plain view.

The easiest way to feel the idea is with paint. Suppose Alice and Bob each have a private colour that they never reveal, and they also agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow, which Eve can see too. Alice mixes a generous amount of her private colour into the yellow and sends the resulting jar to Bob, while Bob does the same with his own private colour and sends his jar to Alice. Eve sees both jars travel past, but mixed paint cannot be separated back into its ingredients, so she cannot tell what private colours went in. Now Alice takes the jar she received from Bob and adds her private colour to it, and Bob takes the jar he received from Alice and adds his. Both end up with yellow plus Alice's colour plus Bob's colour, and since the order of mixing does not change the final shade, their two jars match exactly. Eve, who holds only the two half-mixed jars and the plain yellow, has no way to reach that final shade without one of the private colours.

Real cryptography replaces paint with arithmetic, and the part of school mathematics that matters most here is the remainder you get after division. Think of a clock face. If it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, the clock reads three, because you wrap around after twelve. Mathematicians call this clock arithmetic, and the key move is to do multiplication and powers in the same wrapping-around way. To raise a number to a power, you multiply it by itself that many times, and then you keep only the remainder after dividing by a fixed number, which we will call the modulus. The crucial fact is that working forwards is quick, while working backwards is painfully slow. If I tell you that five multiplied by itself six times, with remainders taken at each step, leaves a certain value, you can check my claim in a moment. But if I give you only the final value and ask how many times five was multiplied, you have no shortcut and must try the possibilities one by one.

Let us run through an example with small numbers, so that you can follow every step. Alice and Bob publicly agree on a modulus of 23 and a base number of 5. These are not secrets, and Eve is welcome to know them. Alice privately chooses the number 6, and Bob privately chooses the number 15. Alice works out five raised to the power six, which is 15,625, and then takes the remainder after dividing by 23, which turns out to be 8. She sends the number 8 to Bob across the open line. Bob works out five raised to the power fifteen in the same wrapping-around way and gets 19, which he sends back to Alice. At this point Eve has seen the numbers 23, 5, 8 and 19, and nothing else.

Now comes the surprising step. Alice takes the 19 that Bob sent her and raises it to her private power of six, again keeping only the remainder after dividing by 23, and she arrives at 2. Bob takes the 8 that Alice sent him and raises it to his private power of fifteen, and he also arrives at 2. They have independently reached the same number, and it was never transmitted. The reason is that both calculations amount to multiplying five by itself six times fifteen times in total, just grouped in a different order, and the order of that grouping does not affect the answer. Alice and Bob now share the secret number 2, which they can use as the key for an ordinary scrambling method that both of their computers know.

You may wonder why Eve cannot do the same thing. To get the secret she would need either Alice's private six or Bob's private fifteen, and the only information she has to find them is the numbers 8 and 19. With a modulus as small as 23 she could simply try every possibility in a few seconds, which is why this example is only a demonstration. Real systems use a modulus with hundreds of digits, and the private numbers are equally enormous. At that size the number of possibilities exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe, and the fastest methods anyone has found for working backwards would take longer than the age of the universe on today's computers. Nobody has proved that no clever shortcut exists, but generations of mathematicians have searched without finding one, and that long record of failure is what the security rests upon.

The same one-way idea supports a second use of public keys, which is sending a secret message directly. Here each person holds a pair of linked numbers, one made public and one kept private. You can picture the public number as an open padlock that Bob hands out to anyone who asks. Alice snaps the padlock shut around her message, and from that moment only Bob, who alone holds the matching key, can open it. Not even Alice can undo what she has done. The most famous system of this kind multiplies together two huge prime numbers to make the public number, and it relies on the fact that multiplying two primes is trivial while splitting the product back into those primes is enormously hard when the primes are large. Whichever way it is done, the public half can safely be shouted from the rooftops, because it only locks things.

There is one weakness that the mathematics alone does not remove. Eve could do more than listen, because she might sit in the middle of the line and pretend to be Bob when talking to Alice, and pretend to be Alice when talking to Bob. Each of them would then agree a secret with Eve instead of with each other, and Eve could read and pass along everything. To prevent this, people also need a way to confirm that a public number really belongs to the person claiming it. Websites handle this with certificates, which are digital statements, vouched for by trusted organisations, that tie a public number to a particular name, and a signature trick that runs the padlock idea in reverse lets anyone check that such a statement is genuine.

When your browser shows a small padlock beside a web address, a version of this whole conversation has just taken place in a fraction of a second. Your device and the website have agreed on a shared secret across a line that anyone could have been watching, and everything you send afterwards is scrambled with it. The remarkable thing is how little is required, which is no more than a handful of ordinary ideas from school arithmetic, applied to numbers large enough that going backwards becomes hopeless.
