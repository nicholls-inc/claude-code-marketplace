On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise. It was to be NASA's third landing on the Moon, and by then the public had begun to treat such flights as routine. Lovell, the commander, was the most experienced astronaut in the world, and this was his fourth trip into space. Haise was to pilot the lunar module with him on the surface, at a highland site called Fra Mauro. Swigert was a late substitute. Ken Mattingly had been grounded just days earlier because of possible exposure to German measles, and Swigert took his place at the command module controls after only a few days of intensive training.

The launch had its own small alarm. The center engine of the Saturn V's second stage shut down early, and the remaining engines burned longer to make up the difference. The crew reached orbit anyway, left Earth for the Moon, and settled into the unhurried rhythm of the outbound journey. The command and service module, named Odyssey, was joined to the lunar module, named Aquarius. Two days passed without trouble.

On the evening of April 13, about 200,000 miles from home, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine chore meant to keep the supercold contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later, at 9:08 p.m. Houston time, the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert said the words that became famous in a slightly altered form: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, adding that they had a main bus B undervolt.

The cause lay in the oxygen tank number two. Years earlier, during ground testing, the tank had been dropped slightly and later damaged during a draining procedure. The thermostatic switches inside had been built for a lower voltage than the spacecraft was later given, and during testing they had fused shut, a fault nobody noticed. When Swigert turned on the fans, wiring with burned insulation sparked inside the tank, and the pure oxygen ignited. The pressure rose until the tank ruptured, blowing off a panel of the service module and damaging the neighboring tank as well.

In the first minutes, nobody understood how bad it was. Instruments flickered, thrusters fired on their own, and the crew saw a gas venting into space outside the window. Lovell reported that something was escaping, and when the flight controllers saw the oxygen pressure falling toward zero, the truth sank in. The fuel cells that made electricity and water for the command module depended on that oxygen. Within about two hours the command module would be dead, and with it the only machine built to survive reentry.

The only lifeboat was the lunar module. Aquarius was designed to carry two men for two days, and only to the surface and back to lunar orbit. Now it had to keep three men alive for four. The crew powered it up, moved in, and shut down Odyssey to save what little battery power remained in it for the final descent. Flight Director Gene Kranz told his controllers that failure was not an option, a line later attributed to him through the film, though the spirit of it was real enough in the room.

The first decision was how to get home. A direct abort, turning around at once, would have required firing the service module engine, which no one trusted after the explosion. Instead, controllers chose to swing the spacecraft around the Moon and let lunar gravity sling it back toward Earth, using the lunar module's descent engine to adjust the path. The crew fired that engine shortly after passing the Moon, speeding their return, and fired it again later to correct the trajectory.

Then came the long cold. To conserve power and water, nearly everything was turned off. The cabin temperature fell to around forty degrees Fahrenheit, and condensation beaded on the walls. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection after drinking too little. The men slept poorly, huddled in the cramped spaces, and rationed their water to a few ounces each per day.

A second, quieter danger grew beside them. Carbon dioxide from the men's breathing was building up in the lunar module, whose lithium hydroxide canisters, designed for two people for two days, were nearly saturated. The command module had spare canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the same items the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard, a suit hose and duct tape, and devised an adapter. They read the instructions to the crew step by step, and the astronauts built it. The carbon dioxide readings dropped back to safe levels.

Meanwhile, another problem waited. The command module had to be restarted in orbit, in a state nobody had planned for, with its batteries drained. Engineers worked through the night to write a new power-up procedure, a checklist that would normally have taken months, and Mattingly, the man who had been bumped from the flight, helped test it in a simulator. Near the end, the crew separated the damaged service module and, for the first time, saw the extent of the harm. A whole side panel was gone, and the wreckage trailed wires and debris. Lovell described the sight with evident shock, and photographs were taken for the investigators. They then released the lunar module that had saved them, and watched Aquarius fall away.

Reentry, on April 17, carried one last uncertainty. Because of the damage and the unusual flight path, no one could be sure the heat shield was intact. Communications blackout during reentry normally lasts about three minutes, and this time it stretched beyond that, long enough for the tension in Houston to become nearly unbearable. Then, at last, Swigert's voice came through, and parachutes were seen blossoming above the South Pacific. Odyssey splashed down near the recovery ship Iwo Jima, a few miles from its target, after nearly six days in space.

The crew were thin and tired, and Haise was ill, but all three were alive. The mission never reached the Moon's surface, yet many consider it NASA's finest hour. A review board traced the failure to the tank and the chain of small, overlooked errors in its history, and the oxygen tanks were redesigned for later flights. The episode reminded people that spaceflight is risky, and that the people on the ground and in the spacecraft, working together calmly under pressure, can sometimes turn a disaster into a rescue.
