The Earth does not spin at a perfectly steady rate, and a leap second is the small patch that keeps our clocks in step with it. Since 1972, the world's official time, known as Coordinated Universal Time, has been kept by atomic clocks that count out seconds with extraordinary regularity, while the planet itself, which is the original timekeeper from which days and nights are measured, wobbles and slows because of tidal friction, shifting oceans, movements in its molten core and even the melting of ice. Left alone, the two would drift apart, so whenever the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation threatened to exceed 0.9 of a second, international timekeepers inserted an extra second at the end of June or December, and on those occasions a clock would read 23:59:60 before ticking over into the new day. There have been 27 of them, the most recent at the end of 2016.

For most people a leap second is invisible, but for the computer systems that run telecommunications, finance, power grids and satellite navigation it has been a recurring headache. Software generally assumes that every minute has sixty seconds, and a minute with sixty-one breaks that assumption in ways that are hard to predict, which has led to crashed servers, scrambled timestamps and outages at major websites. Because the insertions are announced only a few months ahead and come at irregular intervals, engineers cannot simply schedule around them, and large technology companies have resorted to "smearing" the extra second across many hours, a workaround that different firms carry out differently and so introduces its own disagreements about what time it is.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body of national governments that oversees the international system of units, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035, and the decision rests on a change in the Earth's behaviour as well as on the software problem. The planet's rotation has recently been speeding up slightly, which raises the prospect of a first-ever negative leap second, in which a second would be removed, and that is an operation almost nobody has tested and which many experts fear could cause worse failures than adding one does. Under the new arrangement, atomic time will be allowed to wander from the Sun-based day by a larger margin, perhaps a minute or more, over a period of decades or centuries, and a different correction will be devised if it ever becomes necessary. Astronomers who track the sky will need to adjust their instruments, but for everyone else the sun will still rise and set on schedule, and the practical gain is that the world's clocks will run smoothly without the occasional extra tick that engineers have long dreaded.
