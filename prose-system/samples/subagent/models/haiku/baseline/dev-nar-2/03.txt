**Apollo 13: Two Days to Get Home**

On April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert, and Fred Haise toward the Moon. Lovell was on his fourth spaceflight. Swigert was a last-minute replacement: Ken Mattingly, the command module pilot assigned to the mission, had been exposed to rubella days before launch and was pulled from the crew. The plan was a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands, the third lunar landing attempt after Apollo 11 had made the first a year earlier.

The first two days went smoothly. Then, about 55 hours into the flight, on the evening of April 13, the crew carried out a routine stirring of the cryogenic oxygen in the service module's tanks. A fan inside one tank had damaged wiring that was not built for the strain, and when the fan switched on, the wiring sparked. The tank ruptured. Swigert radioed Houston, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space. The blast had also damaged the second oxygen tank, and within the hour the service module had lost its oxygen supply and most of its electrical power.

Flight Director Gene Kranz and the controllers in Houston had a grim problem. The command module's fuel cells, which produced both electricity and drinking water, depended on that oxygen. The crew would have to abandon the Moon landing and come home, and they would have to do it using the lunar module, *Aquarius*, as a lifeboat.

The lunar module was built to support two men for about 45 hours. Now it had to support three men for roughly four days. The crew shut down the command module to save its remaining batteries and moved into *Aquarius*, which was cramped, cold, and short on everything. Mission Control chose a free-return trajectory, a path that would swing the spacecraft around the far side of the Moon and bring it back to Earth using gravity rather than a large engine burn. The lunar module's engine would provide several correction burns along the way.

The next problem was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's air scrubbers used lithium hydroxide canisters, but the canisters from the command module were a different shape and did not fit its sockets. Carbon dioxide levels inside *Aquarius* began to climb toward dangerous levels. In Houston, a team of engineers led by Ed Smylie worked out an adapter using the materials that were on board: plastic bags, duct tape, the cardboard cover of a flight plan checklist, and spare hoses. Mission Control read the procedure to the crew, and Lovell, Swigert, and Haise assembled the device and ran air through it. It worked.

Other hardships followed. The spacecraft was cold, and the crew saw their breath in the lunar module. They rationed water and drank little, because they needed to conserve what they had for the return. Haise had a painful kidney infection during the return, adding to the strain on the crew. Lovell and Haise steered the spacecraft by hand and kept an eye on its temperature while it rotated slowly to keep one side from overheating in the sun.

On April 15, the spacecraft swung around the far side of the Moon. At its farthest point, Apollo 13 was about 400,000 kilometers from Earth, the greatest distance any humans had traveled from home. Then the crew lost radio contact for a time as the Moon blocked the line of sight, and when they came back into range, the trip home was underway.

The return was far from easy. The engineers had to plan and test new procedures for powering up the command module after days of being off. They needed a precise entry angle. Too steep, and the capsule would burn up; too shallow, and it would skip off the atmosphere and into space. Mission Control and the crew checked and rechecked the numbers.

On April 17, the crew separated the command module from the damaged service module about four hours before reentry. Through the windows they could see the wreckage, including the side of the service module that had been blown open. Then the command module re-entered the atmosphere, cutting off radio contact during the blackout caused by the heat of reentry. Anxious controllers and families waited through the silence before the parachutes opened.

Apollo 13 splashed down in the Pacific Ocean at 1:07 p.m. Eastern Time on April 17, about four miles from the recovery ship USS *Iwo Jima*. The crew were safe. The mission had not reached the Moon, but it had survived the most dangerous emergency of the Apollo program, and it had done so through the work of people on the ground and in the spacecraft.

The mission's legacy is more complicated than the phrase "Houston, we have a problem" suggests. Apollo 14 flew the landing in February 1971, touching down in the Fra Mauro highlands that Apollo 13 had been meant to reach. The famous line "Failure is not an option" is often attributed to Gene Kranz, but it came from the 1995 film *Apollo 13*, directed by Ron Howard, not from the mission itself. The real story is quieter and more impressive: a crew that kept their heads, controllers who improvised solutions in hours, and a spacecraft that was never designed for the job it had to do.
