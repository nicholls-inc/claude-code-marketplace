# How Two Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

## The problem

Imagine you want to agree a secret number with someone you have never met, but the only way to reach them is a line that anyone can listen to. Every word you say, the eavesdropper hears. You have no shared codebook, no password agreed in advance, and no chance to whisper in a corner.

Until the 1970s, most people thought this was impossible. Secrets, the thinking went, needed a secret to start from: you had to meet first, swap a key, and only then talk safely. Public-key cryptography showed otherwise. Two people can build a shared secret entirely in the open, and an eavesdropper who hears everything still cannot work it out.

This essay explains how, using nothing beyond school arithmetic.

## The key idea: easy one way, hard the other

The whole trick rests on a kind of calculation that is easy to do forwards and extremely hard to undo.

Everyday life has plenty of these. Mixing paint is easy; unmixing it is not. Smashing a plate takes a second; reassembling it takes hours. Multiplying 7,919 by 7,927 takes a minute with a pencil. But if I give you the answer, 62,775,... and ask which two prime numbers multiplied to make it, you would be stuck guessing.

Mathematicians call these "one-way" operations. Public-key cryptography is built from one that suits our purposes very well.

## The paint analogy

Before any numbers, here is the idea with paint.

Alice and Bob agree, openly, on a starting colour: yellow. The eavesdropper, Eve, hears this too.

1. Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red. Bob secretly picks his own, say blue. Neither tells anyone.
2. Alice mixes her red into the yellow and sends the resulting orange to Bob. Bob mixes his blue into the yellow and sends the green to Alice. Eve sees both pots travel past.
3. Alice takes Bob's green pot and adds her private red. Bob takes Alice's orange pot and adds his private blue.

Both now hold yellow + red + blue, a muddy brown. The order of mixing does not matter, so their browns match exactly. Eve has yellow, orange and green, but she is missing the private colours. Separating orange back into yellow and red is the "unmixing" that cannot be done.

Now we replace paint with arithmetic.

## Clock arithmetic

We need a mixing operation that is easy forwards and hard backwards. The one used in practice is arithmetic on a clock face.

On a clock with 12 hours, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, not 15. You wrap around. Mathematicians write this as "mod": 15 mod 12 = 3, meaning "the remainder after dividing by 12".

We will use a clock with 23 positions (0 to 22). Adding or multiplying works as usual, then you wrap around by taking the remainder after dividing by 23. For example, 25 mod 23 = 2.

We also need powers. "5 to the power 3" means 5 x 5 x 5 = 125, and 125 mod 23 = 10, because 23 x 5 = 115 and 125 - 115 = 10.

## The exchange, with real numbers

Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers:

- the clock size, 23
- a base number, 5

Everyone hears these. They are not secret.

**Step 1: private numbers.** Alice secretly picks 6. Bob secretly picks 15. These never leave their heads.

**Step 2: public mixes.**

Alice computes 5 to the power 6, then wraps around the clock of 23.
5^6 = 15,625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 with remainder 8. So Alice sends **8**.

Bob computes 5^15 mod 23. Working it out step by step gives **19**. Bob sends **19**.

Eve hears 8 and 19.

**Step 3: the final mix.**

Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power: 19^6 mod 23. The answer is **2**.

Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private power: 8^15 mod 23. The answer is also **2**.

They both arrive at 2, which is their shared secret. Neither ever sent it.

## Why do the answers match?

Look at what each person actually computed. Alice's final number is (5^15)^6, and Bob's is (5^6)^15. Both are 5 multiplied by itself 6 x 15 = 90 times, wrapped around the clock. Since 6 x 15 equals 15 x 6, the results must agree. This is the same fact as "the order of mixing paint does not matter", written in algebra.

## Why can't Eve do the same?

Eve knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To reach the secret she needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. Her only route is to ask: "5 to what power gives 8 on this clock?"

With a clock of 23 she can simply try everything: 5, then 5^2 = 2, then 5^3 = 10, 5^4 = 4, 5^5 = 20, 5^6 = 8. Found it, after six tries. So this toy example is not secure, and it is not meant to be.

The point is how the difficulty grows. Powers on a clock behave erratically: as the exponent climbs, the answers jump around the clock with no pattern that points back to the exponent. There is no known quick shortcut for working backwards, so Eve has to try exponents one by one. With a clock of 23 positions, that is trivial. With a clock whose size has 600 digits, and secret numbers just as large, the number of tries exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. Meanwhile Alice and Bob can still do their forward calculation quickly, using a clever repeated-squaring method that avoids multiplying 5 by itself billions of times.

That asymmetry is the whole security story: forwards takes seconds, backwards takes longer than the age of the universe.

## From a shared number to private messages

The shared secret is only a number, but that is enough. Alice and Bob feed it into an ordinary scrambling method, one where the same key locks and unlocks a message. From then on, everything they send looks like noise to Eve, while Alice and Bob read it easily. This is what happens when your browser shows a padlock: your computer and the website run an exchange like this in a fraction of a second, then use the resulting secret to protect your passwords and card details.

## The "public key" in the name

The exchange above is the simplest form, known as Diffie-Hellman after its inventors. The name "public-key" comes from a closely related idea. Imagine a padlock. You can make thousands of copies of an open padlock and hand them out to anyone, and anyone can snap one shut around a box. But only you, holding the one key, can open it.

In the same way, each person publishes a "public key" that anyone can use to lock a message for them, while a matching "private key", kept to themselves, is the only thing that unlocks it. The most famous scheme, RSA, gets its one-way trick from multiplication: multiplying two huge primes is easy, but factoring the result back into those primes is not. Same principle, different difficult sum.

## What to take away

- The secret is never sent. Only half-mixed ingredients travel, and the final mix happens privately at each end.
- Security comes from an arithmetic asymmetry: powers on a clock are quick to compute and very slow to reverse.
- The numbers in real use are enormous, so guessing is hopeless, even though our toy clock of 23 could be cracked in seconds.
- Nothing needs to be agreed in advance. Two strangers with an open line can end up sharing a secret that no listener can reconstruct.

That is why the maths of wrapping around a clock quietly protects almost everything you do online.
