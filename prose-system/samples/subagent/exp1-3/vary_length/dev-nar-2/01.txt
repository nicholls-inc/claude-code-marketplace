The Dark Side of the Moon

On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 2:13 in the afternoon, Houston time. It carried three men: Jim Lovell, the commander, on his fourth spaceflight and veteran of Apollo 8; Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, who had joined the crew only days earlier; and Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, who was making his first trip to space. Swigert was a late substitute. Ken Mattingly, the original command module pilot, had been grounded after exposure to German measles. The mission was meant to land in the Fra Mauro highlands of the Moon. By then, two crews had already walked on the surface, and the public had begun to treat lunar flight as routine. Television networks declined to broadcast the crew's early transmissions.

The launch had one flaw. The center engine of the Saturn V's second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the other engines burned longer to compensate. The crew reached orbit anyway. After circling Earth, they fired the third stage, left for the Moon, and pulled the command module free to dock with the lunar lander, which they called Aquarius. The command module was named Odyssey. For two days nothing much happened. The ship coasted. The crew ate, slept, and answered questions from the ground.

The trouble began, unseen, long before. Years earlier, the oxygen tanks for the service module had been redesigned to run on a higher voltage, but the heater thermostats inside them were never upgraded to match. During a ground test before launch, technicians had trouble draining tank number two. They overrode the problem by running its heaters for a long stretch, and the thermostat switches welded shut. Nobody noticed. The insulation on the wires inside the tank was left scorched and cracked. The tank looked fine. It was a bomb with the fuse already burning.

On the evening of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the flight, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that keeps the contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. Ninety-odd seconds later, a loud bang shook the spacecraft. Lovell, in the left seat, thought at first that Haise had played a prank. Then the master alarm sounded, the lights flickered, and the voltage on the main electrical buses dropped. Swigert said the words that became famous in a slightly altered form: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, adding that there had been a main B bus undervolt.

In the tank, the stirring fans had thrown a spark across the damaged wires. The insulation burned, and the pressure inside the tank climbed until the tank ruptured. The blast blew off a panel on the side of the service module and damaged the neighboring tank. Within a few hours, both tanks would be empty. Oxygen was not only air for the crew. It fed the fuel cells that produced the ship's electricity and water. Without it, the command module was dying.

For a while, nobody on the ground grasped how bad it was. Flight controllers stared at instruments that disagreed with each other and suspected a faulty sensor. Then Lovell, looking out the window, reported that something was venting into space. A gas was streaming from the service module. That settled it. In the control room, Flight Director Gene Kranz told his team to be tough and competent, and to work the problem. Mission Control did not think of the Moon any more. The only goal now was getting the men home alive.

The plan to land was scrapped within hours. The crew powered down the command module to save what little battery life it had, and climbed into Aquarius, the lunar lander. It was built to support two men for two days on the Moon's surface. Now it had to keep three alive for four days. Lovell and Haise worked to bring it to life, and Swigert, who had been the junior man on the mission, helped. Aquarius became a lifeboat.

The flight path made things harder. Apollo 13 was on a free-return trajectory, which would swing it around the Moon and back toward Earth, but it would arrive too late and in the wrong place. Controllers decided to burn Aquarius's descent engine to speed the return and pull the landing point into the Pacific. That first burn, a few hours after the Moon's far side slid beneath them, worked. Later, another burn corrected the course. The crew had to align the ship using the Sun, since the usual guidance platform had been shut down, and Lovell steered by the terminator of the Earth.

Then came the cold, and the air. The lander's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrub carbon dioxide from the cabin, filled up fast with three men breathing. Spare canisters in the command module were the wrong shape, square where Aquarius needed round. On the ground, engineers dumped a pile of the same hardware on a table: cardboard, plastic bags, a sock, duct tape, the cover of a flight manual. They built an adapter and read the instructions to the crew over the radio. Lovell and Swigert assembled it in orbit, and the carbon dioxide readings fell. It was one of the most celebrated improvisations in the history of spaceflight.

The temperature dropped to near freezing. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and Haise developed a fever and a kidney infection. The men slept little. Condensation ran down the walls. Meanwhile, in Houston, Mattingly, the man the measles had kept off the flight, worked in a simulator to find a way to restart the command module from nearly nothing, using the tiny reserves of power the ship still had. His procedure, tested on the ground and then dictated to the crew, would be essential.

On April 17, the crew prepared for the end. Hours before arrival, they jettisoned the damaged service module and saw, for the first time, the extent of the harm: a whole panel gone, wires hanging, the engine bell dented. Lovell reported that it was a bad one. Then they powered up Odyssey, letting its flight computer wake in the cold, and the instruments held. Finally they released Aquarius, which had saved them, and watched it fall away.

Reentry carried one more hazard. Because of the long cold, some worried that the heat shield had been damaged, and the ordinary radio blackout that comes with reentry would stretch past its usual three minutes. It did. The silence lasted longer than expected, about a minute and a half more, until controllers feared the worst. Then the capsule's signal returned. Three parachutes bloomed. At 12:07 in the afternoon, Houston time, on April 17, Odyssey settled into the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship, the Iwo Jima.

The crew were thin, tired, and alive. Haise's fever eased, and a few days later the doctors cleared him. Within the year, investigators traced the failure to the thermostats and the tank, and engineers redesigned the system, putting in stronger wiring, a third oxygen tank, and a separate battery. Apollo went on. Three more missions reached the Moon.

Apollo 13 is remembered as a successful failure, a phrase that is often repeated and only half true. The mission failed. Its goals were lost, and the accident came close to killing three men. What succeeded was the response: thousands of people, in a control room and in a spacecraft, who refused to give up and who solved, one problem at a time, a set of problems that nobody had planned for. The men came home. For many, that was the real triumph.
