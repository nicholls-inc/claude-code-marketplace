The Pump on Broad Street

In the last days of August 1854, cholera struck the crowded London district of Soho with a violence that shocked even a city accustomed to epidemics. People who were well at breakfast were dead by nightfall. Within three days, more than a hundred residents of the streets around Broad Street had died; within ten days, the toll passed five hundred. Families who could leave fled, and within a week most of the neighbourhood's houses stood shut and empty.

Cholera is a brutal disease. It drains the body of water through violent diarrhoea and vomiting, and in the nineteenth century it killed roughly half of those who caught it. Nobody knew what caused it. The prevailing belief among doctors and officials was the "miasma" theory: that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting waste, cesspools and overcrowded graveyards. It was a reasonable guess. Poor districts smelled terrible, and poor districts suffered most.

John Snow did not believe it. A physician in his early forties, the son of a Yorkshire labourer, Snow had made his name as a pioneer of anaesthesia; he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth the year before. He was a quiet, methodical man who had been studying cholera for years, and he had noticed something the miasma theory could not explain. Cholera attacked the gut, not the lungs. If the poison were breathed in, why did it not begin in the chest? Snow suspected that people swallowed it, and that it travelled in water contaminated by the waste of earlier victims. He had published this idea in 1849, and it had been largely ignored.

Snow lived only a few minutes' walk from Broad Street. When the outbreak began, he went straight to the scene. His suspicion fell quickly on a public pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street, a popular one whose water was known locally for its good taste. He examined a sample and found it looked fairly clear, which proved nothing either way. The microscopes of the day could not identify a germ nobody yet knew to look for. So Snow turned to a different kind of evidence: the pattern of the deaths themselves.

He obtained a list of the dead from the General Register Office and went from house to house, asking surviving relatives and neighbours a simple question: where had the victim got their drinking water? The answers pointed again and again to the same place. Nearly all the deaths had occurred in homes closer to the Broad Street pump than to any other. Of the deaths nearer to a different pump, several turned out to involve people who preferred the Broad Street water, or children who drank from it on their way to school.

The exceptions were even more telling than the rule. A workhouse close to the pump housed more than five hundred inmates, yet only a handful died; it had its own well. At a brewery on Broad Street itself, none of the seventy or so workers died. The men were given an allowance of beer and the brewery also had its own well, so they never touched the pump. And then there was a widow who lived miles away in Hampstead, a district untouched by cholera. She died of it, as did a niece who had visited her. Snow learned that the widow had once lived on Broad Street, liked the taste of its water, and had a bottle carted out to her every day. Her last delivery had been drawn on 31 August.

On 7 September, Snow presented his findings to the local parish officials, the Board of Guardians of St James's. They were not convinced by his theory, but the evidence was hard to dismiss and they had little to lose. The next day they ordered the pump's handle removed so that no one could draw water from it.

The outbreak ended, although Snow himself was careful not to claim too much. The deaths had already been falling for several days, largely because so many residents had fled. He could not say whether the well was still dangerous when the handle came off.

The full story emerged months later. A local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, had set out to disprove Snow, believing the epidemic to be an act of God. His own patient interviews changed his mind, and he found the missing piece: a baby at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with cholera just before the outbreak began. Her mother had washed the soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspool at the front of the house. When the cesspool was dug up, its brick lining was found to be decayed, and it sat less than three feet from the well.

Snow later drew a map marking each death as a small black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars cluster thickly around the pump, and the map became one of the most famous images in the history of medicine.

Recognition came slowly. Officials continued to favour bad air for years, and Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, before his theory was accepted. It was only in the following decades, as London built its great sewer system and scientists identified the cholera bacterium, that he was proved right. Today he is remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the science of tracing how disease spreads through a population. A replica pump, without a handle, stands in Soho on what is now Broadwick Street, close to a pub that bears his name.
