Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, carrying three men toward what was meant to be America's third Moon landing. The commander, Jim Lovell, was on his fourth spaceflight and had already orbited the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise would fly the lunar module down to the surface with him. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only days earlier, after the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, was exposed to German measles and grounded by the flight surgeons.

For two days the flight was so smooth that the television networks did not bother to carry the crew's evening broadcast on April 13. The astronauts gave viewers a tour of their two joined spacecraft: the command module Odyssey, which would carry them home, and the lunar module Aquarius, the spindly lander built to work only in the vacuum of space. They signed off about 200,000 miles from Earth.

Minutes later, Mission Control in Houston asked for a routine chore. The service module, the cylinder behind Odyssey that held its main engine and supplies, carried tanks of super-cold oxygen and hydrogen. These fed three fuel cells that produced the ship's electricity and water, and the oxygen was also what the crew breathed. The tanks needed an occasional stir from internal fans to give accurate readings. Swigert flipped the switches.

Inside oxygen tank 2, damaged wiring sparked. The insulation caught fire, the pressure soared, and the tank burst. The crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights lit up across the instrument panel. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Asked to repeat, Lovell said it again: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first, controllers hoped it was an instrument fault, because the readings seemed too bad to be true. Two of the three fuel cells were dead. One oxygen tank read empty and the other was falling. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming into space. It was the ship's oxygen. The explosion had damaged the second tank too, and when it emptied, Odyssey would have no power, no water and no air beyond a small reserve set aside for re-entry.

The landing was abandoned. The only question now was whether the crew could get home alive. Flight director Gene Kranz and his team made the decision that saved them: the astronauts would move into Aquarius and use it as a lifeboat. It had its own oxygen, batteries and engine. But it was designed to keep two men alive for about two days, and it would now have to support three men for four.

With roughly fifteen minutes of power left in Odyssey, Swigert shut the command module down completely, saving its batteries for the final plunge through the atmosphere, while Lovell and Haise hurried to bring the lander to life.

There was no quick way to turn around. Nobody trusted the main engine after the blast, so the ship would continue to the Moon, swing around its far side and let lunar gravity sling it back toward Earth. Apollo 13 had earlier left the path that would have returned it home automatically, so the crew fired the lander's descent engine, never intended for this job, to get back onto it. As they passed behind the Moon, they travelled farther from Earth than any humans before or since, about 248,000 miles. Haise and Swigert took photographs of the surface they would never walk on. Two hours later a second, longer burn sped up the return by ten hours and aimed the ship at the Pacific Ocean.

Then came the long coast home, and it was miserable. To stretch the lander's batteries, nearly everything was switched off, including the heaters and the guidance computer. The cabin temperature fell to a few degrees above freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men slept badly, if at all. Water was needed to cool the lander's electronics, so each astronaut was limited to about six ounces a day, a fraction of what the body needs. They became badly dehydrated, and Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever.

A more urgent danger was building in the air itself. Each breath the men exhaled added carbon dioxide to the cabin, and the lander's filter canisters, which scrubbed it out, were being used up by three men instead of two. Odyssey had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lander's fittings were round. In Houston, engineers led by Ed Smylie gathered only the items they knew were on board: plastic bags, cardboard covers from the flight plan, a spacesuit hose and grey duct tape. In about a day they had designed an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew, step by step. The astronauts built the contraption, which they called the mailbox, and watched the carbon dioxide level begin to fall.

Meanwhile the ship was drifting off course, and a correction was needed without the help of the guidance computer. Lovell lined up the Earth in his window, keeping the line between its day and night sides against the crosshairs of his sight, while Haise steered on the other axis and Swigert timed the burn with his wristwatch. Fourteen seconds later they were back on track.

One large problem remained. Odyssey had been shut down cold in deep space, something never planned for, and it had to be woken up on almost no electricity. Any mistake in the sequence could drain the batteries before splashdown. Teams in Houston, including the grounded Mattingly working in a simulator, spent days writing and testing a new procedure. When Swigert finally worked through it, he was throwing switches on panels covered in water droplets, with everyone worrying about a short circuit. None came.

A few hours before re-entry, the crew cast off the damaged service module and saw it for the first time. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. An entire panel had been blown off, from the base to the engine. The sight raised a new fear: the heat shield on Odyssey had sat right beside the explosion, and no one could know whether it had cracked.

Then the crew climbed back into the command module and let Aquarius go. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," Mission Control radioed.

On April 17, Odyssey hit the atmosphere at nearly 25,000 miles per hour. The heat of re-entry always cuts off radio contact, normally for about four minutes. This time four minutes passed with no word, and then five. In Mission Control and in front of televisions around the world, people waited in silence. After about six minutes, Swigert's voice came through the static. Soon the capsule appeared beneath three orange and white parachutes, and it splashed down in the South Pacific within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima.

The three men were tired, thin and cold, but alive. Investigators later traced the explosion to a chain of small oversights: a tank that had been jolted during handling years before, and heater switches that were never upgraded for higher voltage and welded shut during a ground test, letting the wiring inside overheat. The tanks were redesigned, and Apollo flew to the Moon four more times.

Apollo 13 never reached the lunar surface. NASA called it a successful failure, because the mission was lost and the crew was saved.
