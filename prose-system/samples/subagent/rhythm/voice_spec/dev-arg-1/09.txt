Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. A minimum parking requirement tells a developer how many spaces a building must include, usually through a formula tied to floor area, bedrooms or seats. A typical code asks for one or two spaces per home, one for every 300 square feet of shop floor, and one for every few seats in a restaurant. The numbers were rarely drawn from local evidence. Many came from manuals that surveyed a handful of suburban sites with plenty of free parking, and the figures were then copied from one city's code to the next.

The first problem is cost. A surface space takes about 300 square feet once the aisles are counted, which is more room than many city flats give a person to live in. A space in a parking structure commonly costs somewhere between $25,000 and $40,000 to build in the United States, and an underground space costs more. A builder has to recover that money through rents or sale prices, whether or not the tenant owns a car. Donald Shoup, who wrote The High Cost of Free Parking in 2005, has argued that this spreads the cost of parking across everyone, including households without cars, who end up subsidising the drivers around them. In New York City roughly half of households do not own a car. I think Shoup is right, and the evidence is on his side.

The second problem is what the rules do to buildings. On a small urban lot the requirement can decide what gets built. A site that could hold twelve flats may hold eight once a ramp and the required spaces are drawn in, or the project may not go ahead at all. Older main streets are affected in particular. A bakery that wants to move into a former shop may have to show it can supply spaces the building never had, and so the shop stays empty. Many of the older neighbourhoods that people value most would be illegal to build today for this reason.

The third problem is land. Parking takes up room that could hold homes, shops or trees. In some American city centres, parking covers a large share of the developed land. The result is that destinations sit farther apart and the streets between them are less pleasant to walk along, which is a view I hold from watching how such places work, though it is hard to put a single figure on it.

Several cities have tried the alternative. Auckland removed its minimums in 2016. Buffalo, New York did so in 2017 and was the first large American city to do it citywide. Minneapolis followed in 2021 and Austin in 2023. In these places developers have continued to build spaces where they judge that buyers and tenants want them. What changed is who makes the decision. It moved from the planning office to the people paying for the building, and lenders, who want a project that is easy to fill, remain a check on anyone who ignores demand.

The strongest objection is that without a requirement, new buildings will push their cars onto neighbouring streets, and existing residents will find it harder to park outside their own homes. The worry is a fair one and deserves a direct answer. The cause is free kerb space, not the lack of a minimum. Where street parking is unpriced and unlimited, someone will use it. A city can manage this with residents' permits or with metered rates set so that one or two spaces on each block stay free, and the revenue can be spent on the street itself. A city that scraps its minimums and leaves the kerb unmanaged will see the problem. The sensible response is to manage the kerb, not to require every builder to guess at demand years in advance.

A second objection is that some people need to drive, such as disabled residents, tradespeople, and families in places with poor transit. That is true, and removing minimums does not change it. Accessible spaces can still be required under separate rules, and nothing stops a developer from offering ample parking at a suburban site where buyers expect it. A minimum applies the same formula to the person who needs a car and the person who does not, and the second person pays for it too.

Removing the rule is a modest step. It does not ban parking or set a maximum. It leaves the question of how many spaces a building needs to the people who finance, build and use it, who know more about a particular site than a formula in a municipal code can. Where there is demand, parking will be provided and priced. Where there is not, the land and the money can go to something else.

Cities that are short of homes, and many are, have few measures that cost the public purse nothing and make building easier. Repealing parking minimums is one of them. The rule was written on a guess about demand, and the bill has been paid by renters, small businesses and neighbourhoods that can no longer build what they once did. It should go.
