Keep Teaching Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years a country announces that it will stop teaching cursive in its primary schools. The reasoning is always tidy. Children type more than they write, exams are drifting onto laptops, and an hour spent on looping letters is an hour not spent on coding or maths. It sounds modern and practical. I think it is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it properly rather than as an afterthought squeezed in before the bell.

I will start with what I am not claiming. I am not arguing that cursive is a sacred cultural inheritance that must be preserved for its own sake. Nostalgia is a weak reason to fill a timetable. Nor am I claiming that typing is a lesser skill. Children need keyboards, and they should learn to use them well. My claim is narrower. Joined-up handwriting does real work in a child's development, it costs little, and the alternatives on offer do not replace what it gives.

The first argument concerns the hand and the brain working together. Writing by hand is a complex motor act. A child has to form a letter shape from memory, control pressure, keep a steady line, and plan the next movement while finishing the current one. Printing letters one at a time asks for all of this, but it asks for it in a stop-and-start rhythm. Each letter is a separate event, with the pen lifted and repositioned dozens of times in a single sentence. Joined writing turns the word into one continuous gesture. That continuity is harder to learn at first, but it is also the reason it pays off. A fluent cursive writer is not thinking about individual letters at all. The word comes out as a unit, and attention is freed for what the sentence is meant to say.

This matters because the central problem in early writing is cognitive load. A seven-year-old composing a sentence is juggling spelling, grammar, vocabulary, the shape of letters and the physical effort of holding a pencil. Whatever can be automated frees up capacity for the rest. Research on handwriting fluency has repeatedly found that children who write quickly and legibly produce longer and better composed texts, and the link holds even when the content of the writing is compared fairly. Fluency is not decoration. It is infrastructure for thought. A joined style, taught well and practised until it is automatic, is one of the most efficient routes to that fluency.

The second argument is about spelling. Because cursive links letters into whole words, the hand learns the typical sequences of English. The movement for "ing" or "tion" becomes a single memorised pattern, which many teachers report helps children who struggle with spelling, including some with dyslexia. This is not a universal claim, and the evidence is more mixed than enthusiasts admit. Some dyslexic children find joined writing difficult and do better with a simpler print style, and a good school will make allowances. But as a default for the whole class, a joined style supports the link between how a word sounds, how it looks and how it feels to write. Typing, by contrast, uses the same finger motion for very different letters and gives no comparable motor memory of a word's shape.

Third, there is the matter of reversals and separation. Print letters such as b, d, p and q are mirror images or rotations of one another, and young children confuse them constantly. In a joined script, each of these letters begins with an entry stroke and is formed in a distinct way, which reduces the chance of flipping them. Because letters in a word are written without lifting the pen, word boundaries also become clearer on the page, and the spaces between words become more deliberate. Again, this is not magic. A child who prints carefully will also learn to tell b from d. But the claim that print is simpler and cursive is an unnecessary complication ignores the places where cursive is actually more forgiving.

Now consider the objection that handwriting is obsolete. It is true that adults write less by hand than they once did. It is also true that nearly everyone still does it daily, on forms, in notes, on whiteboards, in birthday cards, in a margin while thinking something through. Studies comparing note-taking by hand with note-taking on a laptop have often found that handwriting leads to better conceptual understanding, because the slower pace forces students to summarise rather than transcribe. The size of that effect has been debated, and some replications have been weaker than the original claim, so I would not build the whole case on it. But the broader pattern is consistent. Writing by hand engages the mind differently from typing, and there is no good reason to let that capacity wither in the first years when it is easiest to build.

The objection about time is the strongest one, so it deserves a direct answer. Primary timetables are crowded, and every subject has its advocates. But handwriting does not need a large amount of time. Short, frequent, well-structured practice is more effective than long sessions. Ten minutes a day, built into literacy lessons, is enough for most children to become fluent over a couple of years. The cost is small compared with the benefit, and it is far smaller than the time schools already devote to remedial support for children who reach secondary school unable to write legibly at speed. The argument that we cannot afford cursive looks much weaker once we count the cost of illegible and exhausting handwriting in later years, when exam candidates are marked down because examiners cannot read them.

That brings up a practical point that critics tend to skip. Children are still assessed by hand in most countries, and they will be for the foreseeable future. A pupil who cannot write quickly and clearly is disadvantaged on the day that matters. Teaching a fluent, efficient script early is a matter of fairness as much as pedagogy. Children from homes with plenty of books, pens and patient adults tend to pick up good handwriting regardless. Children from homes with fewer of these things depend on school to teach it. If schools drop the subject, those who lose most are the ones with least to fall back on.

Then there is the question of reading. Cursive is not a dead script. Letters from grandparents, historical documents, old recipes and teachers' comments on homework are written in it. A child who cannot read joined writing is cut off from a whole layer of ordinary human communication, and from the primary sources that history teachers still love to show. Teaching children to write cursive is the most natural way to teach them to read it. Asking them to decipher it without ever having formed it is like asking someone to understand a language they have never spoken.

I want to be fair to the opposing view, because it has a serious version. Some countries, Finland most famously, have moved toward print and keyboard skills and have not seen their literacy results collapse. The lesson I take from that is that the choice of script matters less than the quality of teaching. A school that teaches print well will beat a school that teaches cursive badly. But that is an argument for teaching handwriting seriously, not for dropping the joined style. And the Finnish example is only one case, in a system with many other strengths that make it hard to isolate any single decision.

What should good teaching look like? It should start with print in the earliest years, because small children need simple letter shapes while they are learning to read. It should introduce joins gradually, once the basic letters are secure, rather than as a sudden switch. It should focus on legibility and speed rather than ornament, since no child needs to master a flourish for a capital Q. It should allow flexibility, so that a child who finds a particular join awkward can adapt it, and so that a child with a motor difficulty gets tailored help. And it should treat handwriting as a skill to be practised briefly and often, with feedback, rather than as a penalty for messy work.

The case for keeping cursive therefore does not depend on a romantic view of the past. It rests on a handful of modest, practical claims. Joined writing builds fluency, and fluency supports composition. It reinforces spelling patterns for many children. It reduces some common letter confusions. It prepares pupils for handwritten assessment and for reading the handwriting of others. And it costs only a few minutes a day. Against this, the argument for dropping it amounts to a hunch that the time could be better spent, with no clear account of what would replace the benefits.

A sensible education policy does not discard a cheap, useful skill simply because a newer tool exists beside it. Children can and should learn to type. They can and should also learn to write by hand in a way that is quick, clear and comfortable. Joined-up handwriting is the best-established way we have to achieve the second goal, and primary schools should keep teaching it.
