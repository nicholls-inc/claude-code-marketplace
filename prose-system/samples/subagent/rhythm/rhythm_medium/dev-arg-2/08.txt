Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, at least in the form most of them practice today, and the sooner they admit it the better their work will go.

Consider what an estimate actually is in most organizations. It begins as an honest guess made by someone who knows the least they will ever know about the problem, and within a day it hardens into a commitment that other people plan around. The engineer who said "about three weeks, if nothing surprises us" is later reminded only of the three weeks. The caveat evaporates, the date goes onto a roadmap, and the roadmap goes to a customer. When the surprise arrives, as it always does, the team is not rewarded for discovering it early but punished for being late.

The deeper problem is that estimates are wrong in a predictable direction. Work that looks simple turns out to hide a decade of accumulated decisions, and the cost of understanding it is paid only after you start. Decades of research on planning, and most engineers' own memories, point to the same result: we are optimistic, and our errors are skewed toward overruns that are far larger than any underruns. Averaging a lot of bad guesses does not produce a good forecast. It produces a confident-looking spreadsheet.

Meanwhile the estimating itself is expensive. Teams spend hours in planning meetings debating whether a task is a five or an eight, arguments that change nothing about how long the work takes. Those hours are real, and they buy a number nobody should trust. Worse, the number distorts behavior. People pad their guesses, cut corners to hit dates, or quietly avoid the uncertain and valuable work in favor of the predictable and trivial.

Managers often reply that the business needs dates, and that is fair. But a business needs decisions, and decisions can be made without per-task guesses. Slice the work into small pieces, ship them continuously, and measure how many pieces actually finish each week. That observed throughput is a far better forecast than anyone's intuition, because it reflects your real team, your real codebase, and your real interruptions. If the question is whether a project can land by the end of the quarter, count the remaining pieces and look at the history. If the answer is no, you learn it in week two rather than week eleven.

A second tool is to fix the time and flex the scope. Decide what the work is worth, say six weeks of a small team, and then ask what the best thing is that can be built in that window. This turns the conversation from "how long will this take" into "what are we willing to pay," which is the question a manager is supposed to be answering anyway. It also gives engineers a legitimate way to say that part of the plan will not fit.

Nobody is arguing that teams should stop thinking about size. A rough sense of whether something is days or months is useful, and breaking a problem down is often the best way to understand it. The thing to give up is the pretense of precision, and the ritual of turning a guess into a promise. Teams that give it up spend less time in meetings, surface bad news sooner, and ship in smaller steps. Those are better outcomes than any estimate has ever delivered.
