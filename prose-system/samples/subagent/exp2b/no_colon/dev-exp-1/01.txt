A heat pump can warm a house on a cold day because it does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists. Even when the air outside is near freezing, it still holds a lot of thermal energy. Absolute zero is around minus 273 degrees Celsius, so air at minus 5 is far from empty of heat. A heat pump collects some of that energy, concentrates it, and delivers it indoors.

The principle is the same as in your fridge, run in the opposite direction. A fridge pulls heat out of its interior and dumps it into your kitchen. A heat pump pulls heat out of the outdoor air and dumps it into your home.

Here is how it works. A sealed loop contains a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, well below zero. The loop has four main parts.

First, the outdoor unit has a coil with a fan blowing air across it. The refrigerant in the coil is colder than the outside air, so heat flows from the air into the refrigerant. This warms it enough to turn it from a liquid into a gas.

Second, a compressor squeezes that gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, in the same way a bicycle pump gets hot when you use it. The gas may now be around 50 to 60 degrees Celsius, which is hotter than your radiators or underfloor system need to be for a comfortable home.

Third, that hot gas passes through a coil, or heat exchanger, inside your home. It gives up its heat to your heating water or indoor air, and in doing so it condenses back into a liquid.

Fourth, an expansion valve lets the liquid pressure drop suddenly. This makes it very cold again, and the cycle repeats.

The compressor is the only part that needs significant electricity. That is the key to the economics. A heat pump typically delivers three to four units of heat for every one unit of electricity it uses. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance, or COP. A gas boiler, by comparison, can never deliver more than about 0.9 to 0.95 units of heat per unit of gas, because some heat always escapes up the flue.

The efficiency does fall as it gets colder outside, because the pump must lift heat across a bigger temperature gap. On a mild day the COP might be 4 or higher. On a very cold day it might drop to 2 or 2.5. That is still better than a boiler in energy terms, though whether it is cheaper to run depends on your electricity price compared with your gas price. Modern units keep working in temperatures well below minus 10, and many are rated down to minus 25.

What does this mean for your decision? Several things matter.

Your house needs to hold its heat. Heat pumps work best at lower flow temperatures than boilers, so a well insulated home with decent draught proofing gets the most from one. A poorly insulated house may need upgrades first, or larger radiators, so the system can stay efficient.

Emitters matter. Underfloor heating suits heat pumps very well. Existing radiators may need replacing with larger ones, because the water runs cooler than the boiler water you are used to.

Running costs depend on your tariffs. Where electricity is much more expensive than gas per unit, the saving shrinks. A time-of-use electricity tariff, or solar panels, can improve the result considerably.

Installation costs are higher than for a new boiler, though grants or subsidies are available in many places. Ask for a heat loss calculation from the installer, not a rule of thumb, and make sure the unit is sized to your home.

Finally, expect a different feel. A heat pump tends to run gently and for long periods, keeping rooms steady rather than blasting hot water in short bursts. Many people find this more comfortable once they adjust, though it can take a season to tune the settings.

In short, a heat pump heats a house in winter by moving existing heat from outdoors to indoors, using electricity to do the moving rather than to create the heat. That is why it can beat a boiler on efficiency. Whether it beats one on cost for you depends on your insulation, your radiators, and your local energy prices, so get a proper survey before you decide.
