A heat pump does not make heat in the way a boiler does. It moves heat from one place to another, and it can do this even when the place it takes heat from is colder than the place it delivers it to. The idea sounds odd until you notice that "cold" is relative. Air at 0°C still holds a large amount of thermal energy. Absolute zero is about minus 273°C, so outdoor air on a frosty morning is a long way above the point where no heat can be extracted at all.

The machine works with a sealed circuit of refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at very low temperatures. Outside the house, a fan blows air across a coil, the evaporator, that holds the refrigerant at a temperature below that of the outdoor air. Heat flows from the air into the refrigerant, which is enough to make it boil into a gas. This happens at around minus 10°C or lower, so the air can be well below freezing and the process still works.

The gas then passes through a compressor, which is an electric pump. Compressing a gas raises its temperature, the same effect you feel when a bicycle pump gets warm. The refrigerant leaves the compressor at perhaps 50 to 70°C, which is hotter than the water in your radiators needs to be. It flows through a second coil, the condenser, where it gives up its heat to the water in your heating system and turns back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops its pressure and temperature sharply, and the cycle starts again.

The compressor is what lets heat move from cold to warm, and it needs electricity to do so. The useful point is how much heat you get per unit of electricity. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance, or COP. A typical air-source heat pump has a COP of about 3 in mild weather, so one kilowatt-hour of electricity delivers about three kilowatt-hours of heat, with two of those coming from the outdoor air. At minus 5°C the figure falls, often to between 2 and 2.5, because the compressor has to work harder over a bigger temperature gap. Over a full year, installers usually quote a seasonal figure of 2.8 to 3.5 for a well-designed system in the UK.

A gas boiler, by comparison, converts at most about 90 to 94 per cent of the energy in the gas into heat, so you get less than one unit of heat for each unit of fuel. That is why a heat pump can use less energy overall even though it is working against the cold.

Cost is a separate question, because electricity per kilowatt-hour has usually been priced at three to four times the price of gas in Britain. With a COP of 3, the running costs of the two come out close to each other, and a heat pump looks better when electricity is cheaper, for example on a time-of-use tariff. Where a heat pump is installed alongside solar panels, the saving can be larger.

For a homeowner, the practical conditions matter more than the physics. Heat pumps deliver water at lower temperatures than boilers, commonly 35 to 50°C against 65 to 80°C, so they suit well-insulated houses and larger radiators or underfloor heating. A poorly insulated house with small radiators may need work before a heat pump will keep it comfortable, and that work adds to the cost. The installed price of an air-source unit is typically £10,000 to £15,000 before any government grant, which in England and Wales has been £7,500 under the Boiler Upgrade Scheme. In my view, the strongest case is a house that is already reasonably insulated and whose boiler is due for replacement, since the extra upfront cost is then easiest to recover. A survey by a qualified installer, who will calculate the heat loss of each room, is the sensible next step before deciding.
