A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two different ways of measuring time. One is astronomical: a day is the time the planet takes to spin once, and the Sun returns to the same spot in the sky. The other is atomic: since the 1960s, the second has been defined by the vibrations of caesium atoms, which tick with extraordinary regularity. The trouble is that the Earth is a less dependable timekeeper than an atom. Tides, shifting oceans and movements of the planet's molten core slow and speed its rotation in ways that are hard to predict, so the length of a day drifts slightly from the tidy 86,400 seconds that atomic clocks count.

To stop the two systems pulling apart, international authorities introduced Coordinated Universal Time in 1972, along with the leap second. When the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation grew close to a second, an extra second was inserted at the end of June or December, so that a minute briefly contained 61 seconds. Between 1972 and 2016, this was done 27 times. The most recent was at the end of 2016, and none has been added since, because the Earth's spin has recently been running a little faster than expected.

The practice has become a headache for the digital world. Computers, telephone networks, financial exchanges and satellite systems depend on timestamps that are precise to tiny fractions of a second, and most were designed on the assumption that every minute lasts exactly 60 seconds. An unexpected 61st second can cause software to crash, repeat events or record them in the wrong order. Several well-known outages over the years have been blamed on leap seconds, and technology companies have invented their own workarounds, such as spreading the extra second across a whole day, which means that different systems do not always agree on the time. Because leap seconds cannot be predicted far ahead, engineers must also be told only a few months in advance.

For these reasons, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of the second, voted in 2022 to phase out leap seconds by 2035. After that date the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation will be allowed to grow much larger before any correction is made, perhaps a minute or more, and the exact approach is still being worked out. Ordinary people will not notice the change. Sunrise and sunset will not move. But the world's clocks will become simpler and steadier, and the slight divorce between the clock and the Sun will be left for future generations to resolve.
