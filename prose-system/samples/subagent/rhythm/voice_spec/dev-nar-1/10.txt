Cholera reached Soho, a crowded district of central London, at the end of August 1854. The disease causes severe vomiting and diarrhoea, and a patient can lose so much fluid that they die within a day or two. London had already been through epidemics in 1832 and 1848, and most doctors held that the cause was miasma, a poisonous vapour thought to rise from rotting waste and damp ground. Within ten days of the first cases, about five hundred people living in a few streets around Broad Street had died. The final count for the outbreak was 616.

John Snow lived a short walk away in Sackville Street. He was a physician, born in York in 1813, and was known chiefly for his work on anaesthesia. He had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during two of her confinements. For several years he had also held a different view of cholera. In 1849 he had published a pamphlet arguing that the disease was taken in by mouth, through water or food contaminated by the discharges of earlier patients, and that it was not breathed in from the air. Few of his colleagues accepted this.

Snow had already tested the idea on a larger scale. In south London, two water companies supplied houses on the same streets, and both had drawn their water from the Thames below the city's sewage outfalls. In 1852 the Lambeth company moved its intake upstream to Thames Ditton, where the river was cleaner. During the 1854 epidemic Snow counted cholera deaths house by house. Homes supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall company had 315 deaths for every 10,000 houses, and homes supplied by Lambeth had 37.

When the Soho outbreak began he used the same method. He obtained the addresses of the dead from the General Register Office and went from door to door asking where each household got its drinking water. He marked the deaths on a map of the streets. They gathered around the public pump in Broad Street, near the corner with Cambridge Street, and almost every household he questioned had used it. In several cases the dead had lived nearer to another pump but were known to prefer the water from Broad Street.

Snow also looked for people who should have been affected and were not. The workhouse in Poland Street held about 535 inmates, and five of them died. It had its own well and did not rely on the Broad Street pump. The Lion Brewery, on Broad Street itself, employed about seventy men, and none of them died of cholera. They had a well on the premises and were given malt liquor to drink. A widow who had once lived in Broad Street died in Hampstead, far from the outbreak. She had asked for water from the Broad Street pump to be carried to her each day because she liked it, and a niece who was visiting her drank some as well. Both women died.

On 7 September Snow took his findings to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish. The next day the handle of the pump was removed, so that nobody could draw water from it. The outbreak was already declining by then, because many residents had left the district, and Snow said so himself. The removal of the handle therefore cannot be shown to have ended the outbreak. He regarded the pattern of the deaths as stronger evidence than the timing.

Henry Whitehead, the curate of St Luke's in Berwick Street, began as a sceptic and set out to disprove Snow's view. He knew the neighbourhood and many of its families, and he questioned them closely. He found that a baby girl at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with diarrhoea on 28 August and had died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool at the front of the house. When the cesspool and the well were examined, the cesspool lay only a few feet from the well, and the brickwork of both was found to be decayed, so that sewage could have seeped into the drinking water. Whitehead came to agree with Snow, and the two worked together afterwards.

Official bodies were slower to agree. A committee of the parish and an inquiry for the General Board of Health both looked at the outbreak in 1855, and neither accepted that contaminated water was the cause. Snow republished his work that year with a map on which each death was shown as a black bar at the address where it occurred. He died in 1858, at the age of 45, after a stroke. By then the Italian anatomist Filippo Pacini had described the cholera bacillus in 1854, though his paper was little noticed, and Robert Koch identified it again in 1883. In the 1860s Joseph Bazalgette's new sewers carried London's waste away from the Thames below the city, and when cholera returned to the East End in 1866 it was traced to a single water company. Snow's account of Broad Street is now treated as a founding example of epidemiology, the study of how disease is distributed in a population and why.
