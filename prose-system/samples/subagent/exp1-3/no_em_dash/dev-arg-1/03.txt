Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet subsidy hiding in plain sight. It is the parking lot. Most of those spaces exist not because a developer or a shop owner decided they were worth building, but because a rule said they had to. Minimum parking requirements, the zoning provisions that force every new building to include a set number of spaces, are among the most consequential and least examined policies in urban life. Cities should remove them.

Start with how the numbers get set. A typical code might demand one space per 300 square feet of retail, two per apartment, or one for every four seats in a restaurant. These ratios rarely come from careful local study. Many trace back to manuals produced by traffic engineers who sampled a handful of suburban sites with free parking, measured peak demand, and treated that peak as a universal need. Planners copied the figures from neighboring towns, who had copied them from somewhere else. The result is a rule built on borrowed guesses and applied to every lot in the city, whether it sits beside a subway station or at the edge of a highway.

The cost of those guesses is large and lands on everyone. A single surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count aisles and entrances. A structured space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one often costs more. That money does not vanish. It is folded into the rent of the apartment above, the price of the sandwich at the corner cafe, and the sticker on the goods in the store. Because the cost is bundled into everything, people who do not own cars pay for parking anyway. Households with no vehicle, who skew poorer, end up subsidizing the storage of other people's cars. A rule sold as a public convenience works as a regressive tax.

Housing is where the damage is clearest. Every required space competes with a home for room on the lot. On a small parcel, a parking mandate can be the difference between a building that pencils out and one that never gets built. Developers respond by building fewer units, larger units, or nothing at all. Cities struggling with affordability often wring their hands over high rents while enforcing a rule that makes new apartments more expensive and less numerous. You cannot credibly claim to want more housing while demanding that each home come with a mandatory garage.

The urban fabric suffers as well. Parking requirements spread buildings apart, set them back behind seas of asphalt, and make walking between destinations unpleasant and slow. Old main streets, with shops pressed against the sidewalk, would be illegal to build today in many places. Small businesses are hit hardest. A family wanting to convert a vacant storefront into a bakery may discover the building cannot be reused because it lacks the spaces the code now requires. Buildings sit empty, and the street dies, to protect a supply of parking that nobody asked for.

The usual objection is that without mandates, developers will build no parking and the streets will overflow. This worries many residents, and it deserves a straight answer. First, removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers and lenders still know that most tenants and customers drive, and they will build what the market needs. The difference is that they will build the amount that makes financial sense, tailored to the site, rather than a number handed down by an ordinance. A building near transit might provide little, while one in a car-dependent area will provide plenty.

Cities that have tried this offer evidence. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and others have followed, and many more have loosened rules near transit. Studies of these reforms have found that developers frequently still build parking, but often less of it, and that new housing and commercial projects become easier to finance. The sky has not fallen. The feared chaos of spillover parking has been manageable where cities pair reform with sensible tools.

Those tools matter, and they are the honest answer to the street-overflow worry. If curb space is scarce, the solution is to manage it, not to force every private building to oversupply its own. Pricing on-street spaces according to demand, issuing residential permits where needed, and sharing underused lots between neighbors all make better use of what exists. A well-priced curb leaves a few spaces open, which is exactly what drivers want. Free curb space and mandatory private lots together produce the opposite: too many cars chasing a resource treated as costless.

Nothing here is hostile to drivers. Plenty of people will always need cars, and plenty of buildings will always include garages. The point is about who decides. Today a city council decides, using a formula, with the costs hidden. Without mandates, owners, lenders, and tenants decide, using real prices and real knowledge of the site. That is a better way to allocate land, one of the scarcest resources a city has.

Cities already spend enormous effort trying to build housing, revive downtowns, cut emissions, and keep rents in reach. Removing parking minimums advances every one of those goals while costing the public treasury nothing. It is rare to find a reform that is this cheap, this effective, and this overdue. Repeal the requirements, manage the curb, and let people build what their neighborhoods actually need.
