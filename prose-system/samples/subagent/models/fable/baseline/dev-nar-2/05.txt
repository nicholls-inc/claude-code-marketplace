Apollo 13: The Long Way Home

Apollo 13 lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, carrying three men toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was making his fourth spaceflight and had already orbited the Moon once on Apollo 8. Fred Haise was to land with him in the lunar module. Jack Swigert, who would stay in orbit aboard the command module, had joined the crew only days before launch, after the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, was exposed to German measles and grounded.

For two days the flight was so smooth that controllers in Houston joked about being bored. On the evening of April 13, the crew gave a television tour of their spacecraft, which the American networks did not bother to broadcast. Moon flights had already come to seem routine.

A few minutes after the broadcast ended, with the spacecraft about 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked Swigert to switch on the fans that stirred the oxygen tanks in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the crew cabin that held the ship's main engine, power, and air. It was a routine chore. Seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights came on. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Asked to repeat, Lovell said it again: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first, controllers suspected faulty instruments, because the readings made no sense: no single failure should have knocked out so many systems at once. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming into space. It was oxygen. One of the two tanks was already empty, and the other was leaking.

That oxygen was not only what the crew breathed. It fed the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity and, as a by-product, its drinking water. As the pressure fell, two of the three fuel cells died, and the third would follow. The command module, named Odyssey, was left with only its small reentry batteries and a small reserve of oxygen, and those had to be saved for the last hours of the flight. The Moon landing was off. The question now was whether the crew could get home.

The answer was attached to Odyssey's nose. The lunar module, Aquarius, had its own oxygen, batteries, and engine, all untouched. It had been built to keep two men alive for about two days on the Moon. It would now have to keep three alive for four. Under flight director Gene Kranz, and then Glynn Lunney's team, which took over that night, the controllers had the crew power up Aquarius and shut Odyssey down completely, in a race against the dying fuel cell. The astronauts copied the navigation platform's alignment from one ship's computer to the other by hand, with Houston checking Lovell's arithmetic, and finished with only minutes of power left in the command module.

Turning straight around was not possible. That would have required the main engine in the service module, and nobody knew whether it had been damaged; firing it might destroy the ship. Instead, the crew would continue on, swing around the Moon, and let its gravity send them back. The spacecraft had earlier left the path that would return it to Earth automatically, so about five hours after the explosion the crew fired Aquarius's landing engine to get back onto it.

On the evening of April 14 they passed behind the Moon, out of radio contact, about 150 miles above the far side. Haise and Swigert, who had never been there, took photographs of the surface they would not walk on. No human beings have travelled farther from Earth, before or since. Two hours later they fired the landing engine again, for more than four minutes, cutting about ten hours from the return and aiming for the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships were waiting.

Then came the long coast home. To make the batteries last, nearly everything in Aquarius was turned off, including the computer and the heaters. The temperature in the cabins dropped to about 38 degrees Fahrenheit. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men slept badly, in snatches. Water was the scarcest supply, because it was also needed to cool the lunar module's electronics, so each man was limited to about six ounces a day, a fraction of what he needed. All three became dehydrated, and Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever.

A quieter danger was building in the air. Every breath the men exhaled added carbon dioxide to the cabin, and the chemical canisters that scrubbed it out of Aquarius were made for two men, not three. Odyssey had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. In Houston, a team of engineers led by Ed Smylie worked out a way to make one fit the other using only items aboard the spacecraft: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight plan cover, a spacesuit hose, and duct tape. The instructions were read up to the crew step by step. They built the device, which they called the mailbox, and the carbon dioxide reading, which had been climbing toward dangerous levels, began to fall.

The spacecraft also kept drifting off course. With the computer switched off to save power, a correction had to be flown by hand. Lovell and Haise steered by keeping the Earth centred in the lunar module's window, while Swigert timed the fourteen-second burn on his wristwatch. It worked.

The last problem was the hardest for Houston. Odyssey had been cold and dead for days, and no one had ever restarted a command module in flight. It had to be brought back to life on batteries alone, and switching things on in the wrong order would drain them before splashdown. Engineers, with Ken Mattingly working in a simulator, spent days writing and testing a new procedure, which normally would have taken months. No one was sure the damp instrument panels would not short-circuit when the power came on. They did not.

On the morning of April 17, about four hours before reentry, the crew cast off the service module and finally saw what had happened to it. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. An entire panel had been blown off, from the engine to the base of the cabin. An hour before reentry they moved back into Odyssey and released the lunar module. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," Mission Control said.

One fear remained: that the explosion had cracked the heat shield. As Odyssey hit the atmosphere, the usual radio blackout began. It normally lasted about four minutes. This time it ran nearly a minute and a half longer, while controllers and a worldwide television audience waited in silence. Then Swigert's voice came through, and the capsule appeared beneath its three parachutes. It splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within a few miles of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, almost six days after launch.

Investigators later traced the explosion to an oxygen tank that had been damaged before the flight. During a ground test, its heaters had been left on for hours, and a safety switch that should have cut the power failed, so the insulation on wires inside the tank was burned away. When Swigert turned on the fans, a spark set the remaining insulation on fire. NASA called the mission a successful failure: it never reached the Moon, but it brought all three men home.
