Keep the Pen Moving: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years someone declares joined-up handwriting dead. Children type and swipe, the argument goes, and adults barely write anything longer than a shopping list. Why spend hours of primary school drilling looped letters that most people will rarely use? Finland dropped the requirement to teach cursive in 2016. The American Common Core standards left it out in 2010. To many people, holding on to joined-up writing looks like nostalgia passed off as pedagogy.

I think that view is wrong. Primary schools should go on teaching joined-up handwriting. That is not because it is pretty or traditional, and not because typing is a threat. It is because a fluent, joined hand is a learning tool. It helps children read, spell and compose. It helps them think on paper. It costs relatively little to teach, and it is very hard to learn well later in life. The real question is not whether children will type. They will. The question is whether they should also have a fast, automatic way of writing by hand. They should, and joining is the most reliable route to it.

**Fluency is the point**

Start with what handwriting is for in a primary classroom. For a seven-year-old, writing a story means juggling several jobs at once: having an idea, finding words for it, ordering a sentence, spelling each word, and physically forming each letter. Working memory is small, and a child's is smaller still. Every bit of attention spent on how to make a "k" is attention taken away from what the sentence should say.

Educational psychologists call this transcription, and research going back decades, much of it associated with Virginia Berninger and Steve Graham, has found that handwriting fluency is a strong predictor of the quality and length of children's written composition. When the mechanics of writing become automatic, children write more and write better. When the mechanics are slow and effortful, ideas evaporate before they reach the page. Studies that have given struggling young writers extra handwriting practice have generally found improvements not just in neatness but in how much and how well they compose.

Joined-up writing is a technique for producing that automaticity. In a fully printed hand, the pen lifts after every letter, and each letter starts afresh, often from a slightly different place. Joining turns common letter sequences into single continuous movements. Writers stop forming "t", "h" and "e" separately and form "the" as one gesture, much as a pianist plays a familiar chord rather than three separate notes. That is why, once mastered, a joined hand tends to be faster and more even than print. It is also why England's national curriculum expects children to begin joining in Year 2 and to develop a consistent, legible joined style through Key Stage 2. The aim is not decoration. It is speed with legibility, so that writing stops being the bottleneck.

**Joining helps spelling and reading**

There is a second benefit, less obvious but just as important. Joined writing ties letters together into units, and that seems to help children remember how words are spelled. When "ing", "ough" or "tion" are produced as one fluent movement, the spelling pattern is stored partly as a motor memory as well as a visual one. Many teachers of children with dyslexia have long recommended joined handwriting for this reason. It reduces the reversals that plague printed writing ("b" and "d" become much harder to confuse when each letter has a fixed entry stroke). It also gives the hand a way of "knowing" a spelling that the eye alone may not hold onto.

The broader research on handwriting and literacy points the same way. In a well-known study, Karin James and Laura Engelhardt found that young children who had practised writing letters by hand showed stronger activation in brain regions linked to reading when they later looked at those letters. Children who had only typed or traced them did not show the same effect. More recent EEG work from Audrey van der Meer's group in Norway has reported richer patterns of brain connectivity when people write by hand than when they type. We should be careful here: much of this research compares handwriting with typing rather than joined writing with print, and the evidence for cursive specifically is thinner than its advocates sometimes claim. But the general finding matters. Producing letters with the hand, through deliberate movement, does something for learning that pressing keys does not. If handwriting matters, the question becomes which form of handwriting best delivers fluency over the long run, and the case for joining rests on its speed and its built-in practice of letter sequences.

**"But they will just type"**

The strongest objection is practical. Adults today mostly type. Children will spend their working lives on keyboards and touchscreens, so primary time would be better spent teaching keyboard skills, coding, or simply more reading.

This assumes that handwriting and typing compete, and that typing has won. Neither is quite true. Children still do most of their school writing by hand, and in England the vast majority of public exams, including GCSEs and A-levels, are still sat on paper. A sixteen-year-old with a slow, cramped hand is at a real disadvantage in a two-hour English Literature paper. That disadvantage is created years earlier, in primary school, when fluent writing habits were or were not built. Onscreen exams may come in time. Pilots are under way. But schools should prepare children for the system that exists, not one that might.

More fundamentally, handwriting does jobs that typing does poorly. Notes taken by hand are slower, and that slowness can be useful: it forces the writer to select and rephrase rather than transcribe word for word. The famous 2014 study by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer, which found that students who took notes by hand understood lectures better than laptop users, has had mixed results in replication. It should not be overstated. But almost every working adult still reaches for a pen to sketch a plan, annotate a document, fill in a form or jot down a thought in a meeting. A fast, legible hand remains a basic tool, and it is far easier to acquire at seven than at twenty-seven.

Teaching handwriting also does not prevent teaching typing. The two skills sit comfortably side by side. The countries and states that dropped cursive have not, by doing so, produced a generation of brilliant typists. Several American states, including California in 2023, have since moved to put cursive back into their curriculums. Their reasons included the literacy research and the plain observation that some young people could no longer read handwritten documents or sign their own names with confidence.

**"It is too hard, and it puts children off"**

A second objection is that joined handwriting is fiddly and frustrating, especially for children with motor difficulties, and that insisting on it turns writing into a chore. There is truth here, and it deserves a serious answer.

Badly taught, joined writing can be a misery. Schemes that demand elaborate loops, or that force four-year-olds to join before they can form individual letters reliably, do more harm than good. Children with dyspraxia or other motor coordination difficulties may need a different path, and good teachers already make those adjustments, including early access to a keyboard where needed.

But these are arguments for teaching joining well, not for abandoning it. A modern, simple joined style, with minimal flourishes and joins that follow naturally from how letters are formed, is not especially difficult for most children. Taught gradually, after letter formation is secure, and practised little and often, it becomes routine within a couple of years. Many children, far from being put off, take pride in "grown-up" writing. And for children who find handwriting hard, the alternative to explicit teaching is not freedom. It is a slow, inconsistent print style that stays hard for life. Leaving children to work it out for themselves tends to widen the gap between those who pick it up at home and those who do not.

**"Legibility is all that matters"**

A subtler objection accepts that handwriting is important but argues that print is enough. Children should write clearly; whether the letters touch is irrelevant.

This misreads what joining is for. Legibility is necessary, but it is not sufficient. A child who can print beautifully but slowly will still run out of time, and will still be spending mental effort on transcription that could go into thinking. Joining is how a legible hand becomes a fast one. It is true that many adults end up writing in a hybrid style, joining some letters and not others. But that hybrid usually grows out of a taught joined hand that has been simplified with use. It rarely grows out of print alone. Teaching joining gives children the raw material for an efficient adult hand, whatever shape that hand eventually takes.

There is also a cultural argument, smaller but not trivial. A person who cannot read joined writing is cut off from letters from grandparents, historical documents, teachers' comments and countless handwritten notes. Reading cursive comes most easily to those who have learned to write it.

**Conclusion**

The case for joined-up handwriting is not a romantic one. It is a case about cognitive load, fluency and access. Children learn to compose better when the physical act of writing is automatic. Joining is the most effective way of making handwriting fast and automatic. Handwriting by hand appears to support reading and spelling in ways typing does not. Exams and everyday life still require a fluent pen. None of this means typing should be neglected, or that every child must write in identical copperplate. It means primary schools should keep doing what the best of them already do: teach a simple joined style, teach it carefully and gradually, adapt it for the children who need adaptation, and treat it as a foundation for thinking rather than a matter of neatness.

Dropping joined-up writing would save a modest amount of classroom time. The price would be paid later, by teenagers who cannot write fast enough to show what they know, and by adults for whom putting a thought on paper is harder than it needs to be. That is a bad trade. Schools should keep teaching children to join their letters.
