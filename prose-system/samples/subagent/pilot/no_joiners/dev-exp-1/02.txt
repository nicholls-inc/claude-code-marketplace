A heat pump does not make heat by burning something. It moves heat that already exists. That sounds odd when the air outside is colder than your living room, because heat naturally flows from warm to cold. The trick is that a heat pump uses a small amount of electricity to push heat uphill, the same way a fridge does, only in reverse.

Cold is a relative idea. Air at zero degrees Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy. It is only colder than your house. Physics lets you keep extracting energy from air until you get near minus 273 degrees, so a winter day at minus five is nowhere near the limit.

Here is the cycle. A sealed loop contains a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature, far below freezing. Outside, a fan blows air over a coil carrying cold refrigerant. Because the refrigerant is colder than the air, heat flows into it and it boils into a gas. Next, a compressor squeezes that gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, just as a bicycle pump warms up when you use it. The gas leaves the compressor hotter than your radiators need, perhaps 50 or 60 degrees. That hot gas flows through a second coil inside the house, where it gives up its heat to your heating water or indoor air. As it loses heat it condenses back into a liquid. Finally an expansion valve releases the pressure, the liquid becomes very cold again, and the loop restarts.

So the compressor does the pushing, and the electricity it uses is the only energy you pay for directly. The rest, usually two thirds or more of the heat delivered, is collected free from outside air.

Why this matters for your wallet. Efficiency is measured as a coefficient of performance. A figure of three means one unit of electricity yields three units of heat. A modern gas boiler tops out around 90 to 95 percent efficient, so it can never deliver more heat than the energy in the gas. A heat pump delivers roughly three times as much. Because electricity usually costs more per unit than gas, the saving is smaller than the raw efficiency suggests, and in some places running costs end up similar. Check your own tariffs. Time of use rates or a solar system can tip the balance.

Cold does hurt performance. The colder the outside air, the harder the compressor works, and the efficiency figure falls, perhaps to two on a very cold day. Higher flow temperatures also reduce efficiency. That is why heat pumps suit homes that run radiators at lower temperatures, which means good insulation matters. Modern units keep working in deep cold, and many cope down to minus 15 or beyond.

What to weigh before replacing a boiler. First, the state of your home. Poor insulation or draughts mean you need a bigger, costlier machine. Second, your radiators. Larger radiators or underfloor heating work best at low water temperatures, and some radiators may need swapping. Third, space. You need room outside for the unit and often a hot water cylinder inside. Fourth, upfront cost. Installation is typically several times that of a boiler, though grants may cover a large part. Fifth, the age of your boiler. If it is failing anyway, this is the natural moment to switch.

A sensible next step is to get a heat loss survey from a certified installer and ask for a quote that states the expected efficiency over the winter. If the numbers show your home can run at low flow temperatures, a heat pump is likely to cut emissions and bills over its life. If not, improve insulation first and revisit the question.
