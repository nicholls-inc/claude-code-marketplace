Why Primary Schools Should Keep Teaching Joined Up Handwriting

Every few years someone declares that handwriting is finished. Keyboards are faster, screens are everywhere, and the argument goes that children should spend their precious classroom hours on coding or critical thinking rather than looping an e into an l. It is a tidy argument and it has a certain appeal to anyone who has watched a seven year old grip a pencil as if strangling it. But it is wrong. Primary schools should continue to teach joined up handwriting, and they should teach it properly, with regular practice and clear expectations. The reasons are not sentimental. They rest on how children learn to read and spell, on how the hand and brain develop together, on what fluency frees a young writer to do, and on the plain fact that much of what children will be asked to produce for years to come still depends on a pen.

Start with what happens in the brain. Researchers who study early literacy have repeatedly found that forming letters by hand does something that pressing keys does not. Karin James and her colleagues, working with young children who had not yet learned to read, found that those who practised letters by drawing them showed stronger activity in brain regions linked to reading than those who typed or traced the same letters. Virginia Berninger and others have found that children who wrote by hand often produced more words, wrote faster and expressed more ideas than those using a keyboard. The explanation is intuitive once you say it aloud. A key looks and feels the same whichever letter you press. A handwritten letter has a shape that the child must plan, remember and execute with the fingers. That effort is not a waste. It is the learning.

Joined up writing deserves particular defence, because the usual compromise is to say that print is fine and cursive is a luxury. Print is certainly a good start. But joined writing offers advantages that print cannot match. When a child writes a word in one continuous movement, the word becomes a single unit rather than a string of separate stabs. Spelling patterns such as ing, tion and ough are written as flowing sequences, and the hand remembers them. Many teachers who work with children with dyslexia favour a joined style for this reason, since the rhythm of the movement supports memory and the continuous line makes it harder to scatter letters in the wrong order. Joining also helps with the infamous confusion between b and d. In a joined script those two letters begin and travel in very different ways, so the hand learns the difference even when the eye hesitates.

Then there is speed and fluency, which matter far more than they are given credit for. A child who must think about how to form each letter is spending mental effort that cannot be spent on what the sentence should say. Psychologists call the solution automaticity. When letter formation becomes automatic, working memory is released for vocabulary, structure and argument. Print can reach this point, but it is slow, because the pen lifts after every letter. A well taught joined hand is quicker and less tiring, which means a ten year old can finish a paragraph before the idea has evaporated. Anyone who has watched a struggling writer produce two painful lines in twenty minutes knows that the problem is often not a lack of ideas. It is the cost of getting them onto the page. Reducing that cost is one of the kindest things a school can do.

The strongest objection is that the world has moved on and typing is the skill children actually need. It is true that children need to type. But the choice is a false one. Handwriting practice takes a modest amount of the school day, perhaps ten or fifteen minutes of focused work, and keyboard skills can be taught alongside it, ideally with proper touch typing lessons in the upper years rather than the haphazard hunt and peck that most children develop alone. Nobody argues that because calculators exist we should stop teaching children to add. We teach number sense first, because it makes every later tool more useful. Handwriting is the same. It builds the foundation of literacy, and the keyboard then sits comfortably on top of it.

Nor has the practical world abandoned the pen. Children in most countries still sit written exams, and in many cases those exams carry the highest stakes of their young lives. A pupil who writes slowly or illegibly is penalised long before an examiner can appreciate the quality of the thinking. Beyond exams there are shopping lists, birthday cards, forms, notes on a whiteboard, a message left for a neighbour, and a signature, which for most adults is a joined up flourish that they learned at primary school. A signature is itself an argument for the case. It is a small act of identity that carries legal weight, and it would be strange to leave children to invent one by accident.

A second objection is that joined writing is hard and some children never master it, so forcing it on everyone is unfair. This deserves a serious answer. Some children do find joining difficult, especially those with motor coordination problems, and a good school will support them. But the response to difficulty is better teaching, not abandonment. If we applied the same logic to reading, we would stop teaching it to anyone who found phonics tough. Many schools now use a simple, upright style with only the most natural joins, which gives most of the benefit with less frustration, and children with significant needs can be given adapted approaches or alternative tools. What matters is that the default expectation is a fluent, legible, joined hand, and that the exceptions are handled with care rather than becoming the rule.

Some will point to countries that have dropped cursive from the curriculum. It is worth looking at what happened next. In the United States, a large number of states removed it during the years when keyboards seemed to have won, and many of them have since passed laws to bring it back, citing concerns about legibility, reading of historical documents and the loss of fine motor practice. That is not nostalgia winning an argument. It is a pattern of institutions noticing that something useful had gone missing. Policy makers who cut handwriting from the curriculum did so on the basis of a prediction about the future. Teachers and parents who watched what followed have supplied the evidence, and it has not been kind to the prediction.

There is also a question of fairness that rarely gets mentioned. A school that replaces handwriting with devices makes a quiet assumption that every child has access to a good keyboard, a stable connection and a calm place to use them. They do not. Pencil and paper are cheap, durable and always available. They work on the bus, at the kitchen table and in a house where the tablet has run out of battery. Handwriting is therefore one of the few literacy tools that costs almost nothing and can be mastered by every child with enough practice. To treat it as optional is to treat as optional the one tool that does not depend on household income.

Attention is another quiet argument in its favour. A child with a pen in hand is doing one thing. A child at a screen is a single tap from a game, a video or a message. Teachers of older pupils increasingly report that notes taken by hand are shorter, more selective and better remembered, because the writer cannot record everything and must decide what matters. That decision is thinking, and thinking is the point of school. The calm of a quiet writing lesson, with a class bent over their books and the only sound the scratch of pencils, is not a relic. It is a rare and valuable condition for learning, and one that children will have fewer chances to experience as the rest of their lives fill with notifications.

What would good teaching look like? It would begin early with the large movements that underpin writing, such as patterns in sand and air, before moving to pencils. It would teach correct posture and grip while children are young enough to adopt them easily, and it would give left handed pupils the attention they need so they are not forced to smudge their way across the page. Joins would be introduced in the first years of school rather than left until children have spent years in an unjoined habit that must then be unpicked. Practice would be short and frequent, because ten minutes a day does far more than an hour once a week. And it would be taught with some pride, as a craft rather than a chore, since children who are shown how satisfying a neat, quick line can be tend to want to produce it.

None of this requires a nostalgic attachment to copperplate or the rapped knuckles of a Victorian classroom. Nobody needs to resurrect elaborate looped capitals or the punishing drills of the past. The case is far simpler. Joined up handwriting supports the connection between letter shape, sound and spelling. It builds the fluency that releases attention for ideas. It serves children in exams and in daily life. It costs little and treats every child alike. And it offers a skill of the hand that is, for many people, a lifelong pleasure.

Technology will continue to change what adults write with, and schools should prepare children for that change. But preparing children for the future does not mean discarding the foundations that make future learning possible. The best argument for teaching joined up handwriting is the one a teacher sees each time a child who once struggled finally writes a whole sentence in a single fluent line and looks up, astonished, to see that it is theirs. That moment is worth protecting, and primary schools should go on protecting it.
