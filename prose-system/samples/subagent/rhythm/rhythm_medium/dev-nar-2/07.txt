On the evening of April 13, 1970, Apollo 13 was about two hundred thousand miles from Earth, carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. The mission had already survived a few early scares. Swigert had joined the crew only days before launch, replacing Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles, and one of the second-stage engines had shut down early during the climb into orbit. Yet the flight had settled into a comfortable routine, and the crew had just finished a television broadcast that most of the networks chose not to carry.

At about nine o'clock Houston time, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks aboard the service module, a routine step that kept the supercooled oxygen and hydrogen from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A few minutes later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert reported, in words that became famous in slightly altered form, that they had a problem. Lovell repeated it, and then added that the main bus B voltage was low and that something seemed to have gone wrong with oxygen tank number two.

The cause lay years in the past. The oxygen tank had been damaged during ground testing, and a thermostat inside it had been fused shut by a higher voltage than it was built to handle. When the stirring fans switched on, a spark in the damaged wiring ignited the Teflon insulation, and the fire ruptured the tank and blew off a panel on the side of the service module. Within minutes the second tank was leaking too, and the fuel cells that produced the ship's electricity and water began to die. Lovell, looking out of a window, saw a stream of gas venting into space and understood that the Moon was no longer possible.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his team of controllers faced a cascade of failing readouts that at first seemed to make no sense. They worked through instrument faults and sensor glitches before accepting that the service module was crippled. The command module, Odyssey, had its own small batteries, but these were meant to be saved for the final hours of reentry. The solution, which had been studied only as a remote contingency, was to treat the lunar module, Aquarius, as a lifeboat. Its systems were built to support two men for two days on the Moon, and now they would have to keep three men alive for four.

The crew moved into Aquarius and powered down nearly everything in Odyssey to conserve what remained. Then came the question of the route home. The spacecraft was on a free-return trajectory that would swing around the Moon and back toward Earth, but an engine burn had earlier moved it off that path so that it could reach the planned landing site. Left alone, it would miss the Earth by a wide margin. Controllers decided to use the lunar module's descent engine to put the ship back on course, and the crew performed that burn a few hours after the explosion. After rounding the far side of the Moon, they fired the engine again to speed their return, shaving hours off the trip.

The journey home was a long ordeal of cold, thirst and fatigue. With the spacecraft's heat gone, the cabin temperature fell to a few degrees above freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The crew drank little water, because it had to be saved to cool the lunar module's electronics, and Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection. They slept poorly, huddled in the cramped spaces of the two vehicles, and Lovell and Swigert wore their lunar overshoes for warmth.

A more immediate danger was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's scrubbers used round lithium hydroxide canisters, which were nearly exhausted after hosting three people far longer than intended. Odyssey carried plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the openings in the lunar module's system were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the same materials the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard, a sock, and duct tape, and worked out a way to join a square canister to a round hose. They read the instructions to the crew step by step, and the crew built the adapter themselves. Within hours the carbon dioxide readings began to fall.

Another problem remained in navigation. With the main guidance system shut down to save power, the crew had to align the spacecraft by other means before the final course correction. Lovell sighted on the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, and used it as a reference, while Houston worked out the timing of the burn. A final correction, done by hand using the lunar module's thrusters, steepened the angle of reentry enough to keep the ship from skipping off the atmosphere.

Meanwhile, back at the Manned Spacecraft Center, a team of engineers had to invent a procedure for reviving the command module that no one had ever written. Powering up Odyssey after days of cold soak was a risk, since it had never been done that way and the batteries had to last through reentry. Mattingly, the grounded astronaut, worked in the simulator and helped devise a sequence that brought the systems back with the minimum possible drain. By the time the crew was ready, the procedure had been checked and rechecked, and it was read up to them in pieces.

On April 17, about four hours before reentry, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module and saw for the first time the extent of the damage. An entire panel was missing, and the side of the vessel was scorched and ragged. Haise and his crewmates photographed it as it drifted away, and the images told controllers how serious the explosion had been. Then they released Aquarius, which had saved them, and it burned up in the atmosphere over the Pacific.

Reentry brought one more silence. Normally, the ionized air around a returning capsule blocks radio for about three minutes, but this time the blackout stretched longer than expected, and the people in Mission Control waited with no news. Kranz and his controllers watched the clock pass the expected time, and the minutes dragged on. Then Swigert's voice came through, and the parachutes appeared on television, three orange and white canopies against the sky.

Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa shortly after noon Houston time, and the recovery ship Iwo Jima picked up the men within about forty-five minutes. They were tired, thinner and dehydrated, but otherwise in good health. Millions of people around the world had followed the story, and the sense of relief was enormous.

The accident prompted a thorough investigation, and the review board traced the fault to the tank and its wiring. NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks, removed the flammable materials, and added a third tank and a separate battery in the service module. Apollo continued, and four more missions went to the Moon. Apollo 13 never reached it, but the way the crew and the ground team improvised a return became one of the most admired stories in the history of spaceflight. Lovell later said that the mission was a successful failure, a phrase that has stuck because it captures something true about what happened.
