How a Heat Pump Heats a House When It Is Colder Outside

The idea that a heat pump can warm your home from cold outdoor air sounds like it breaks the rules. It does not, because a heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists, and cold air still contains plenty.

Heat is not the same as warmth. Air at minus 5 degrees Celsius is cold to us, but it is still far above absolute zero, so it holds thermal energy. A heat pump extracts some of that energy and relocates it indoors.

The mechanism is the same one inside your fridge, run in the other direction. A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures. The cycle has four steps:

1. Outside, the refrigerant passes through a coil. Because it is colder than the outdoor air, even on a freezing day, it absorbs heat and evaporates into a gas.
2. A compressor squeezes that gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, so it leaves the compressor hotter than your living room, often 50 to 60 degrees Celsius or more.
3. Inside, the hot gas flows through another coil, where it releases its heat into your radiators, underfloor heating or warm air system. As it gives up heat, it condenses back into a liquid.
4. The liquid passes through an expansion valve, which drops its pressure and temperature, leaving it cold enough to start the loop again.

The compressor is the key. It lifts heat from a low temperature to a useful one, much as a pump lifts water uphill. That lifting takes electricity, but far less energy than the heat delivered.

This is why efficiency is measured as a coefficient of performance, or COP. A COP of 3 means one unit of electricity moves three units of heat into your home, with two units drawn from the outside air. A gas boiler, even a good condensing one, cannot exceed roughly 90 to 95 percent efficiency, meaning a COP of under 1. Modern heat pumps typically achieve a seasonal COP of around 3 to 4.

The colder it gets, the harder the pump has to work, and the COP falls. At minus 10 Celsius a good unit may still manage a COP of about 2, which still beats a boiler. Current cold-climate models keep running at minus 20 or lower, though with reduced output. Frost can form on the outdoor coil, and the unit handles this with short automatic defrost cycles.

What this means for your decision

Efficiency is not the whole story. Consider these points before replacing a boiler:

- Insulation matters. A heat pump works best delivering steady, moderate heat. A draughty, poorly insulated house needs a bigger unit and costs more to run.
- Emitters matter. Heat pumps run at lower water temperatures than boilers, so older, small radiators may need upsizing, or you may prefer underfloor heating.
- Electricity and gas prices matter. Running costs depend on the price ratio between the two. If electricity costs less than about three times as much as gas per unit, a heat pump usually wins. Time-of-use tariffs can help further.
- Upfront cost is higher. Installation commonly costs several thousand more than a new boiler, though grants or subsidies may cover part of it where available.
- Carbon falls. Because each unit of electricity delivers multiple units of heat, emissions drop substantially, and they fall further as the grid gets cleaner.

A practical step is to ask installers for a heat loss survey of your home. It will show whether your house suits a heat pump as it stands or needs insulation work first. If it does suit, the cold outdoors is not an obstacle. It is simply the source of the heat you are borrowing.
