# Agreeing on a Secret in Public

## The problem

Suppose you need to agree on a secret password with a stranger, and every message you send can be read by anyone on the line. You cannot meet beforehand, and you cannot lock the password in a box that only the other person can open. It seems impossible. Whatever you both know, the eavesdropper can know too.

For most of history it was true. Two people who wanted a private channel had to share a secret first, by meeting in person, by courier, or through some other channel already trusted. In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a method showing this was not always necessary. Their method, now called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, lets two people end up holding the same number while every message between them is visible to an eavesdropper. That number can then serve as a key for encrypting the rest of the conversation.

The method works because it uses a kind of arithmetic in which some operations are easy to perform and their reversal is extremely hard. Understanding it needs only a few ideas from school maths.

## Remainders and clocks

Think of a clock. If it is 10 o'clock and you add 5 hours, you get 3 o'clock, not 15. The clock wraps around after 12. Mathematicians write this as 10 + 5 = 3 (mod 12), read "modulo 12," meaning we keep only the remainder after dividing by 12. Fifteen divided by twelve leaves a remainder of three.

The same idea works with any number in place of 12. Cryptographers choose a large prime, call it p, and work with remainders after dividing by p. An exponent is repeated multiplication: 5 to the power 3 is 5 × 5 × 5. So 5 cubed, taken modulo 23, is 125 with as many multiples of 23 removed as possible. Since 125 = 5 × 23 + 10, the answer is 10.

Two facts make this useful.

First, computing a power modulo p is fast even when the exponent is enormous. You never have to produce the huge intermediate number. You square, reduce, multiply and reduce again. A computer can evaluate g raised to a, modulo p, in a few thousand steps even when a has hundreds of digits.

Second, going backwards is hard. Given g, p and the result, finding the exponent is called the discrete logarithm problem. Ordinary multiplication has no such gap: if you know 5 × 23 = 115, you can recover 5 by dividing. With modular powers, no known shortcut exists. For small numbers you can simply try every exponent. For numbers of the size used in practice, no known method finishes in any reasonable time. This asymmetry, easy in one direction and apparently infeasible in the other, is the foundation of the whole scheme.

## The exchange, with numbers

Use a small prime so every step can be checked by hand. Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers: p = 23 and g = 5. The eavesdropper, Eve, hears these too. Nothing about them is secret, and that is deliberate.

Alice privately picks the secret number a = 6. She computes A = 5^6 mod 23. Working through the powers: 5, then 25 which leaves remainder 2, then 10, then 50 which leaves 4, then 20, then 100 which leaves 8. So A = 8. She sends 8 to Bob.

Bob privately picks b = 15. He computes B = 5^15 mod 23. Since 5^6 leaves remainder 8, 5^12 leaves the remainder of 8 squared, which is 64, and 64 leaves 18. Then 5^15 = 5^12 × 5^3, which leaves the remainder of 18 × 10 = 180, and that is 19. So B = 19. He sends 19 to Alice.

Now each side combines the other's public number with their own secret.

Alice computes B^a = 19^6 mod 23, which equals 2.

Bob computes A^b = 8^15 mod 23, which also equals 2.

Both arrive at 2, and neither ever sent their secret. Eve has seen 23, 5, 8 and 19. She needs the shared value, 2.

## Why the two results match

The reason is an exponent rule. Alice's result is (g^b)^a, which equals g^(ab). Bob's result is (g^a)^b, which also equals g^(ab). Multiplying exponents gives the same answer in either order, so both sides land on g^(ab), modulo p, without either side ever learning the other's secret exponent.

Eve's position is the problem. She has g^a and g^b. To get g^(ab) she has two routes. She could recover a from g^a, or b from g^b, which is the discrete logarithm problem. Or she could compute g^(ab) directly from g^a and g^b without finding either exponent, which is the Diffie-Hellman problem. Nobody has found a fast general method for either. For the tiny example, Eve could test exponents from 1 to 22 until she found that a = 6. That approach collapses at real sizes, because the number of candidates grows with the size of the prime.

## Real sizes

The numbers in the example were chosen for readability. Real systems use primes of about 2,048 bits, a number of roughly 600 decimal digits, or they use elliptic curves, a different mathematical group with the same basic shape. Elliptic curves offer comparable security with much shorter keys. The principle is identical: a public starting point, two secret exponents, and a shared result that only the two secrets can produce.

Each session also uses fresh secrets. If Alice and Bob generate new values for a and b each time they connect, a later leak of one session's secrets reveals nothing about earlier conversations. Security engineers call this forward secrecy. Modern protocols, including TLS, the layer behind HTTPS, rely on variants of this exchange to provide it.

## The limitation: impersonation

Diffie-Hellman has a weakness. It proves nothing about who is on the other end. An attacker sitting between Alice and Bob can run one exchange with Alice while pretending to be Bob, and another with Bob while pretending to be Alice. Each side ends up sharing a key with the attacker, who can decrypt, read and re-encrypt everything that passes through.

The fix is authentication. Each side signs its public number with a long-term key whose owner has been verified, often through a certificate issued by a trusted authority. The exchange then establishes a secret shared with the party you intended to reach, not with whoever happened to intercept the messages.

## Summary

Two people who have never met can agree on a secret over a public line for three reasons. Modular arithmetic makes powers easy to compute and makes reversing them very hard. Each side keeps one exponent private and sends only the result of raising the public starting number to that exponent. And the exponent rule means that each side's combination of its own secret with the other's public value arrives at the same final number.

The public messages reveal the recipe but not the result. Anyone listening hears 23, 5, 8 and 19, and none of those numbers gives away the shared value, which required a secret that never crossed the line.
