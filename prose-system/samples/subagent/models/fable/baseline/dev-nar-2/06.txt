Apollo 13 lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, carrying three men toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was making his fourth spaceflight and had already orbited the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise would fly the lunar module with him down to the hills of Fra Mauro. Jack Swigert, who would stay in orbit in the command module, had joined the crew only days earlier, after the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, was exposed to German measles and grounded.

By then Moon flights had begun to seem routine. For two days the mission did little to change that impression. On the evening of April 13 the crew gave a television tour of their spacecraft, which the American networks did not bother to carry live. The astronauts were about 200,000 miles from Earth, and nearly 56 hours into the flight.

Minutes after the broadcast ended, Mission Control in Houston asked Swigert to carry out a housekeeping chore: switching on fans to stir the supercold oxygen and hydrogen stored in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the crew cabin that held the ship's main engine, power and supplies. Swigert flipped the switches. Inside oxygen tank number two, wiring whose insulation had been damaged during ground tests weeks before launch sparked, and the tank burst.

The crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights came on across the instrument panel. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Asked to repeat, Lovell said, "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first, controllers suspected faulty instruments, because the readings made no sense: no single failure should have produced so many alarms at once. But the readings were real. One oxygen tank was empty and the other was losing pressure. Two of the three fuel cells, which combined oxygen and hydrogen to make the ship's electricity and water, had died. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming into space. It was the ship's oxygen.

The command module, named Odyssey, was dying. Without oxygen the last fuel cell would fail, leaving only the small batteries and reserve oxygen meant for the final hours of re-entry. The landing was abandoned almost at once. The question became whether the crew could get home at all.

The answer was attached to Odyssey's nose. The lunar module, Aquarius, had its own oxygen, power, and engine, and it was undamaged. It had been built to keep two men alive for about two days on the Moon. Now it would have to keep three men alive for four. As Odyssey's power drained away, Haise and Lovell hurried into Aquarius to bring it to life, while Swigert shut down the command module completely to preserve its batteries for re-entry.

In Houston, the flight directors, Gene Kranz and Glynn Lunney among them, had to choose a route home. Turning straight around would have required the service module's main engine, and no one knew whether the explosion had damaged it. The safer course was to continue around the Moon and let its gravity swing the ship back toward Earth. That meant using the lunar module's descent engine, designed for landing on the Moon, to push the whole stack of joined spacecraft, something it had never been intended to do.

The engine worked. A first burn, about five hours after the explosion, put Apollo 13 on a path that would return it to Earth. On the evening of April 14 the ship passed behind the Moon, about 250 kilometres above the far side. The crew were farther from Earth than any humans before or since. Haise and Swigert took photographs of the surface they would never walk on. Two hours after they came back around, a second and longer burn sped the ship up, cutting about ten hours off the journey and aiming it at the Pacific Ocean.

Then came the hard part, which was waiting. To make Aquarius's batteries last, nearly everything was turned off, including the guidance computer and the cabin heaters. The temperature fell to just above freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men slept badly, in snatches, in a cabin too cold for rest. Water was needed to cool the lunar module's electronics, so each man drank about a fifth of a litre a day, a fraction of what he needed. Haise developed a kidney infection and ran a fever through the last days of the flight.

A more immediate danger was the air itself. The carbon dioxide the men breathed out was removed by canisters of lithium hydroxide, and Aquarius's supply was meant for two men, not three. There were plenty of spare canisters in Odyssey, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. As carbon dioxide levels climbed toward the danger point, engineers in Houston worked out a way to connect the two using only things on board: plastic bags, cardboard covers torn from the flight manuals, a spacesuit hose, and grey duct tape. They read the instructions up to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the device, which they called the mailbox, and the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

The ship still drifted slightly off course, pushed by venting gas, and needed one more correction. With the computer off, Lovell and Haise steered by hand, keeping the Earth centred in a window, while Swigert timed the fourteen-second burn on his wristwatch.

Meanwhile, teams on the ground faced a problem no one had ever planned for: how to wake a cold, dead command module in flight using almost no electricity. Controllers and astronauts, Mattingly among them, spent days in the simulators finding a sequence of switch settings that the batteries could afford. The finished checklist ran to hundreds of steps and was read up to Swigert, who copied it out by hand.

On the morning of April 17, with Earth filling the windows, the crew cast off the service module and saw the damage for the first time. An entire panel of its side, running the length of the hull, had been blown away. Swigert powered up Odyssey, and to everyone's relief the damp, frigid instruments worked. The three men then sealed the hatch and released the lunar module that had kept them alive. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," Mission Control radioed.

One uncertainty remained. Nobody knew whether the explosion had cracked the heat shield, or whether the parachutes would open after four days in the cold. As Odyssey hit the atmosphere, the heat of re-entry blocked radio signals, as it always did. The silence normally lasted about three minutes. This time it went on for almost a minute and a half longer than expected, while Houston called and heard nothing.

Then Swigert's voice came through, and television cameras on the recovery ship picked out the capsule beneath three orange-and-white parachutes. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the USS Iwo Jima, not quite six days after launch. The men were exhausted, dehydrated, and many pounds lighter, but they were alive.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon. NASA came to call it a successful failure, because the mission was lost and the crew was brought home.
