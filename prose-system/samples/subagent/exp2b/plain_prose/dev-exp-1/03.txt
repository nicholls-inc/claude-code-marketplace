A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists. Even on a freezing day the outdoor air holds a lot of thermal energy, because "cold" only means less energy than somewhere warmer, not none. Air at minus 5 degrees Celsius is still hundreds of degrees above absolute zero, and a heat pump is built to pull energy out of it and deliver it indoors, where you want it.

The trick is the same one your fridge uses, run in the opposite direction. A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at a very low temperature, far below freezing. Outside, the refrigerant passes through a coil with a fan blowing air across it. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, heat flows into it and it evaporates into a gas. Heat always flows from warmer to cooler, so the refrigerant only needs to be colder than the air, not warmer, to collect energy.

That gas then goes through a compressor, which is the part that uses electricity. Squeezing a gas makes it hot, the way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. The refrigerant leaves the compressor at perhaps 40 to 60 degrees or more, well above room temperature. It now flows through a second coil inside the house, either a heat exchanger feeding your radiators or underfloor heating, or a unit that warms air directly. Since the refrigerant is hotter than the water or air there, heat flows out of it and into your home. As it gives up that heat it condenses back to a liquid. A valve then lets the pressure drop, the refrigerant becomes very cold again, and the loop restarts.

So the compressor raises the temperature of heat that was already there, rather than creating it. The result is why heat pumps are so efficient. For each unit of electricity used, a good modern unit delivers roughly three to four units of heat over a season, a figure called the seasonal coefficient of performance. A gas boiler, even a condensing one, tops out below one unit of heat per unit of gas. The heat pump looks almost too good because most of its output was borrowed from the outdoors for free.

Cold does reduce performance. The colder the air, the harder the compressor works to lift the heat to a useful temperature, so efficiency falls as the weather worsens. Modern cold-climate models keep working well below minus 15 degrees, but a unit that manages a ratio of four in autumn may manage nearer two on the coldest nights. Even then it still matches or beats the boiler on energy used, though not always on running cost, because electricity usually costs more per unit than gas.

For your decision, several points matter more than the physics. First, your house. Heat pumps work best at lower flow temperatures than boilers, so well-insulated homes and those with large radiators or underfloor heating suit them best. Older, draughty properties with small radiators may need insulation work or some radiator upgrades, which adds cost. Second, running costs depend on your local ratio of electricity to gas prices. If electricity costs less than about three to four times as much as gas per unit, the heat pump usually saves money. Time-of-use tariffs can improve this further. Third, installation costs are higher than a boiler swap, though grants or subsidies often cover a meaningful share, so check what is available where you live. You also need outdoor space for the unit and usually a hot water cylinder.

Finally, a heat pump cuts carbon emissions substantially, and more so as the electricity grid gets cleaner. Ask installers for a heat loss calculation of your actual home and a quote that states the expected seasonal efficiency. Those two documents will tell you far more than any general rule about whether replacing your boiler makes sense for you.
