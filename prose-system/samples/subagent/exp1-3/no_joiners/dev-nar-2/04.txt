On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third lunar landing, and by then the public had grown used to the idea. Two crews had already walked on the surface. Television networks had stopped carrying the flights live. Few people watching the early hours of the mission expected anything but another success.

The crew was commanded by Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights who had already circled the Moon once with Apollo 8. Fred Haise, a rookie, was to pilot the lunar module. The command module pilot was meant to be Ken Mattingly, but a week before launch doctors learned he had been exposed to German measles and had no immunity. The flight surgeons grounded him. Jack Swigert, the backup, stepped into the seat only days before liftoff. Swigert had trained hard for the role, yet nobody could have known how much his hurried promotion would matter.

The spacecraft had two main parts. The command module, which the crew named Odyssey, was a cone shaped capsule where the men would live and where they would ride home. Behind it sat the service module, a cylinder packed with fuel, water, oxygen and the fuel cells that made electricity. The lunar module, named Aquarius, rode folded inside the rocket and was built to carry two astronauts down to the surface and back up again.

The first two days passed quietly. The crew checked their systems, took a television tour of the spacecraft for viewers back home, and settled into the long coast toward the Moon. Then, on the evening of April 13, about 56 hours into the flight, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen tanks, a routine step that mixed the supercold contents so the gauges would read accurately. Swigert flipped the switch.

Moments later came a bang and a shudder. Alarm lights lit up across the instrument panel and the spacecraft began to shake. Swigert reported to Houston that they had a problem. Lovell repeated it, adding that there had been a main bus undervoltage. In the ground control room, engineers stared at screens full of numbers that made no sense. Readings were falling. Power was dropping. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space. He told Houston that it was oxygen, and that it looked like they were venting it to the void.

The cause went back years. A heating element inside one of the oxygen tanks had been damaged during ground testing before the flight, and its protective insulation had burned away. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, bare wires sparked inside a tank full of pure oxygen. The tank burst, ripped away a panel from the service module and crippled the neighboring tank. Without oxygen, the fuel cells would soon die. Without the fuel cells, the command module would have no power and no air. The crew was roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, and the Moon landing was now out of the question. The only goal left was survival.

The crew did the one thing that gave them a chance. They climbed into Aquarius and used it as a lifeboat. The lunar module had its own batteries, its own oxygen and its own water, but it had been designed to support two men for about two days on the Moon. Now it had to keep three men alive for four. Meanwhile, the command module was shut down to save its batteries, since those would be needed for the return to Earth.

On the ground, Flight Director Gene Kranz gathered his teams and set them to work. The route home was a problem in itself. Apollo 13 had been aimed along a path that would let it return to Earth if the engines failed, but a later adjustment had put it on a course that would miss the planet. Controllers chose to swing the ship around the far side of the Moon, using lunar gravity as a slingshot, and fire the lunar module descent engine to speed the trip. That engine had never been meant for such a job, but it worked. The craft passed behind the Moon and reached a distance of nearly 250,000 miles from Earth, farther than any humans had ever traveled.

Then came new troubles. The lunar module had been built for a short stay, and its air filters were filling with carbon dioxide. Spare filters existed in the command module, but they were square and the lunar module sockets were round. Engineers in Houston dumped onto a table the same odds and ends the astronauts had aboard, which included cardboard, plastic bags, a hose and a great deal of duct tape. They built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew. Lovell, Swigert and Haise assembled it, and the carbon dioxide level fell.

Life aboard the spacecraft grew miserable. Without power for heat, the temperature dropped to near freezing and moisture condensed on the walls. The men wore the thin gear they had, and slept badly. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day each, and Haise developed a painful infection and a fever. Their food was cold and little of it was eaten. They were tired, thirsty and very cold, and still they had to navigate.

Navigation was another puzzle. The computer in the lunar module was not set up for the job, and the crew could not rely on the usual star sightings because debris from the explosion glittered around the ship like a cloud of fireflies. Lovell steered by lining up the Earth against the window and holding a burn for the exact number of seconds Houston supplied, using nothing but his own steady hand. The correction worked.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the astronaut who had been left behind, was working in a simulator with engineers to invent a way to restart the command module using the tiny reserve of power it had left. Normally the start up took hours and drew far more energy than they could spare. The team rewrote the entire procedure, and the new steps were read slowly to the crew, who copied them down by hand.

On April 17, the crew prepared for the end. They released the damaged service module, and as it drifted away they saw the extent of the harm for the first time. An entire side panel was gone and the ruined interior was exposed. Lovell described it to Houston in plain, stunned words. Then they said farewell to Aquarius, the little craft that had saved them, and released it as well. It later burned up in the atmosphere.

Reentry brought the final test. The heat shield had to be intact, and nobody was sure it was. As the capsule plunged into the atmosphere, a communications blackout, normally about three minutes, stretched longer and longer. In Mission Control, men sat with fists clenched. The silence went on for a minute past the expected time, then another. Then Swigert, calm as ever, came on the radio.

Three parachutes opened over the South Pacific. Odyssey splashed down near the recovery ship Iwo Jima, and the men were picked up in good condition, though Haise was ill and all three were thin and exhausted. Around the world, crowds who had half forgotten the mission cheered the safe return.

Apollo 13 was called a successful failure. It never reached the Moon, yet it demonstrated what a determined team could do under pressure. An investigation led to redesigned oxygen tanks and tighter safety rules, and later Apollo flights went on to complete the program. Today the story stands as one of the great examples of improvisation, teamwork and courage in the history of spaceflight.
