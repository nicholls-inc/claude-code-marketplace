Houston, We've Had a Problem

On April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, had already flown to lunar orbit on Apollo 8 and had two Gemini flights behind him. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was on his first spaceflight. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had been added only days before launch. Ken Mattingly, the original choice for that seat, was pulled after he was exposed to German measles, and Swigert, who had been the backup, took his place. The mission's goal was a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands, a region geologists hoped would hold rocks from the Moon's early history.

The first two days went smoothly. The spacecraft was a combined stack: the command module Odyssey, which carried the crew home, and the lunar module Aquarius, which was meant to carry two of them down to the surface. The service module, attached behind the command module, held the oxygen, water, and electrical power for the whole flight. Inside it were two large cryogenic oxygen tanks and three fuel cells, which combined hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity and drinking water.

On the evening of April 13, roughly fifty-five hours into the flight, the crew was getting ready for a television broadcast. Swigert had stirred the contents of oxygen tank 2 to mix the supply, as was routine. The tank exploded. The crew heard a loud bang and felt a jolt. Lights dimmed and warning indicators lit up. Swigert radioed Houston with the line that became famous: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell later confirmed the situation, reporting that the spacecraft was venting something into space.

Looking out the window, Lovell could see gas streaming from the service module. Oxygen tank 2 was gone, and the explosion had also damaged tank 1, which was leaking. Without oxygen, the fuel cells could not run. Within minutes, the command module was losing power and its main bus was unstable. The mission had changed from a lunar landing to a question of survival.

The ground team was working from the back rooms of Mission Control in Houston. The flight director, Gene Kranz, and his controllers began sorting through systems and possible failures. The first decision was to abandon the landing. The second was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, batteries, and life support, and it was designed to keep two men alive for about two days. Now it would have to support three men for roughly four days. The crew moved into the lunar module and shut down the command module to conserve its remaining battery power.

The next problem was the most immediate one. Carbon dioxide from breathing builds up in a closed cabin, and the lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters were not the same shape as the command module's. The command module's canisters would not fit the lunar module's scrubber system. Engineers in Houston worked through the problem and devised a way to make the square canisters from the command module fit the round opening. The crew assembled the adapter using cardboard, plastic bags, hoses, and duct tape. The procedure had to be read to the astronauts over the radio and carried out exactly. It worked, and it kept the carbon dioxide levels manageable.

The Moon, meanwhile, was not finished with them. The spacecraft had been set on a free-return trajectory, a path that would loop around the Moon and bring the crew back to Earth using only gravity. Mission planners used a short burn of the lunar module's descent engine to adjust the course, and the spacecraft swung around the far side of the Moon. On April 15, the crew passed behind the Moon, lost radio contact with Earth, and then reappeared as they came around the other side. The flyby took them to a point about 254 kilometers above the surface. On April 17, they reached a distance of about 400,000 kilometers from Earth, the farthest any human beings have ever traveled from home.

Conditions aboard were grim. The lunar module had been designed for short stays, so the crew cut back on everything they could. Power was rationed, water was limited, and they used the lunar module's systems as sparingly as possible. The temperature inside Aquarius dropped sharply. Condensation formed on the walls, and reports from the mission describe the cabin nearing freezing. The crew wore heavy clothing and tried to sleep in shifts while the spacecraft drifted through space.

The most dangerous phase came near the end, when the crew had to bring the command module back to life for reentry. Systems that had been powered down for days had to be restarted. The team on the ground ran procedures carefully, because a mistake could damage the spacecraft or the parachutes. Just before reentry, the crew jettisoned the service module. When they saw it fall away, they got their first clear look at the damage: a large section of the service module's outer panels had been blown open, exposing the interior.

On April 17, 1970, the command module Odyssey reentered Earth's atmosphere. The heat shield, which had been protecting the capsule for the whole trip, worked as designed. The parachutes opened, and the spacecraft splashed down in the South Pacific about 142 hours after launch. Recovery teams from the USS Iwo Jima pulled the three men from the water. All three were in reasonable health, though exhausted and thin.

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon. The mission's official success was a rescue, and the crew and ground team received widespread praise for the way they handled an unforeseen disaster. The accident led to a review of the service module's oxygen system, changes to how the tanks were handled and tested, and a more careful approach to spacecraft design. Later Apollo flights carried on, and the Fra Mauro landing was finally achieved by Apollo 14 in February 1971.

The story is often remembered for its line of dialogue, but the more lasting lesson is about teamwork. Three men had to rely on engineering and judgment that lived mostly on the ground. Hundreds of people in Houston worked through the night to turn a broken spacecraft into a workable lifeboat, a working air filter from scraps, and a route home. Apollo 13 showed that a mission can fail in its original goal and still succeed as a human rescue.
