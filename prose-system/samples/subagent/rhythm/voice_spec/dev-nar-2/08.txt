Apollo 13 left the Kennedy Space Center on 11 April 1970, carrying three men toward what was to be the third landing on the Moon. The commander was Jim Lovell, who had already flown to the Moon on Apollo 8. The lunar module pilot was Fred Haise. The command module pilot was Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days before the launch. Ken Mattingly had been assigned the post, but doctors found he had been exposed to German measles, and the flight managers replaced him. The crew was to land in the Fra Mauro highlands. The command and service module was named Odyssey and the lunar module was named Aquarius.

The first two days were uneventful. The crew made a small course correction, entered a path that would take them around the Moon, and took part in a television broadcast on 13 April. The broadcast was not carried by the main American networks. Shortly afterwards Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the tanks of liquid oxygen and hydrogen in the service module, a routine step that kept the contents from stratifying. He switched on the fans at about 9:07 pm in Houston. Some eighty-odd seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. They were then about 322,000 kilometres from Earth.

Swigert reported that there had been a problem, and Lovell repeated it for the record. The instruments showed that the power supply was failing. Oxygen tank number 2 had ruptured. Tank number 1 was losing its contents through the damage, and two of the three fuel cells that made electricity and water from oxygen and hydrogen shut down. The cause was found later. In 1968 the tank had been dropped about five centimetres during handling, which damaged an internal fill tube. In March 1970 the tank had been emptied before the launch using a heater that was connected to a power supply of 65 volts instead of the 28 volts it was designed for. The higher voltage melted the insulation on the wires inside the tank and fused the switches meant to protect it. When Swigert turned on the fans, a spark in the bare wiring ignited the insulation, and the pressure in the tank rose until it failed.

Controllers on the ground first thought the readings might be faulty instruments. As the oxygen pressure fell, it became clear that the command module would have about two hours of power left. The mission to land was abandoned within a short time. Gene Kranz, the flight director on duty, told his team that the task was now to bring the crew home. The plan that followed used the lunar module as a lifeboat. It had its own oxygen, water, batteries and engine, and it had been built to keep two men alive for about two days on the lunar surface. It would now have to support three men for about four days.

The crew moved into Aquarius and shut down Odyssey to save its remaining battery power, which would be needed for the return to Earth. The next problem was the route. The spacecraft was on a path that would carry it around the Moon, but not back to Earth, so it had to be put on a free return trajectory, in which the Moon's gravity swings a craft back toward home. Controllers had the crew fire the lunar module's descent engine for about 30 seconds, about five hours after the accident, to achieve this. The path would still have brought them down in the Indian Ocean after about 150 hours, far from the recovery ships, and with limited supplies. After they passed behind the Moon, they fired the engine again, for about four and a half minutes, which shortened the trip by about ten hours and moved the splashdown area to the Pacific. At their greatest distance, the crew was about 400,000 kilometres from Earth, further than anyone has travelled before or since.

Life on board was hard. The lunar module had been designed to run on small amounts of power, and the crew switched off nearly everything that was not needed. The cabin temperature fell to about 3 degrees Celsius. Water was rationed to about 200 millilitres per man each day, and the crew drank little. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection, probably as a result. All three men lost weight, about 14 kilograms between them. They slept little, since the cold and the noise made rest difficult.

The most urgent technical problem was carbon dioxide. The lunar module used round canisters of lithium hydroxide to remove it from the cabin air, and it had enough for two men for two days. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square and did not fit the sockets in the lunar module. By 15 April the carbon dioxide level in the cabin was nearing a dangerous limit. Engineers in Houston gathered the items the crew had on board, which included plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a suit hose and duct tape. They worked out a way to fit a square canister to a round socket and read the instructions to the crew, who built the adapter. The level fell, and the crew called the device the mailbox.

Another problem was the restart of Odyssey. No one had planned to power the command module up again after it had been shut down for several days, and it had to be done with limited battery power before re-entry. Mattingly, who had stayed on the ground, worked in the simulator with other engineers to find a sequence that would use as little power as possible. They produced a checklist of several hundred steps, and read it to the crew over several hours. There was also the matter of navigation. The crew made a final course correction, about 5 hours before re-entry, which relied on the position of the Earth. Lovell lined the spacecraft up by looking at the line between night and day on the planet through the window, since the guidance system had been switched off and the sun could not be used.

On 17 April the crew released the service module, and for the first time saw the damage. One whole panel had been blown off the side, and the equipment inside was exposed. They photographed it, and the pictures were later used by the investigators. Later they jettisoned Aquarius, and it burned up on the way down. The remaining question was whether the heat shield of Odyssey had been damaged. No one could tell. The re-entry blackout, during which radio contact is lost because of the heat around the spacecraft, was expected to last about three minutes. It lasted about a minute longer, and the people in Mission Control waited with little to say. Then Swigert's voice came through, and the parachutes opened in the television pictures.

Odyssey landed in the South Pacific on 17 April at about 1:07 pm Houston time, close to the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted nearly six days. All three men were tired and thin, but they walked from the helicopter on to the deck of the ship. Many people around the world had followed the flight on radio and television, and the accident drew more attention than the first two landings had.

A review board led by Edgar Cortright reported in June 1970. It found that the failure came from a chain of earlier errors, mostly in the design and testing of the tank and its heater, and it did not blame the crew. NASA redesigned the tank, removed the insulation that burned, added a third oxygen tank in a separate place and put an extra battery in the service module. Apollo 14 flew in January 1971, and the programme made four more landings. Lovell later said that the mission was a successful failure. Others have named it as the occasion on which the ground teams and the crew showed what the programme had been built to do.
