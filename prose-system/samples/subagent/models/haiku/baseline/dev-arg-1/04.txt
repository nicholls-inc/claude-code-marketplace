Stop Forcing Every Building to Come With a Garage

Every apartment building in a typical American city carries a hidden line item: a parking garage nobody asked for, paid for by people who never drive it. For decades, zoning codes have required developers to provide a minimum number of parking spaces per home, per office, per restaurant seat. Those minimums should go. Cities should remove them from their planning rules entirely and let builders, tenants and businesses decide how much parking they need.

The case starts with cost. Parking is not free to build. Surface lots consume land that could hold homes or shops. Structured parking, the garages that sit under apartment blocks, can run to tens of thousands of dollars per space, and that cost does not vanish. It is folded into rents, condo prices and the price of everything sold in the building below. A requirement for one space per unit can add a substantial share to the cost of a modest apartment. Households that own no car end up subsidizing storage for households that do. Renters who never drive still pay for the garage under their building.

Donald Shoup, the UCLA planner who spent much of his career on this problem, called it "the high cost of free parking." When parking is provided at no direct charge to the person using it, people drive more, and more land and money go to storing vehicles. A minimum does not merely allow that outcome. It mandates it.

Minimums also fail on their own terms. A single number cannot fit a corner bakery in a dense neighborhood and a suburban medical office. Requirements are usually copied from other places' codes rather than measured against actual demand, and they tend to overbuild. The result is garages half empty at noon alongside streets full of drivers circling for a spot, because the parking that exists is neither priced nor managed. Minimums lock in a guess and call it planning.

Buffalo, New York, eliminated its citywide parking minimums in 2017, among the first large American cities to do so. Supporters argued it would lower construction costs and let developers build what the market wanted. Buffalo did not become unlivable. Developers kept building, and parking appeared where lenders, tenants or customers wanted it. The rule simply stopped forcing it everywhere.

Critics raise fair concerns. Overflow can spill onto residential streets. People with disabilities, families with small children and tradespeople who carry tools depend on a car, and they deserve reliable options. But eliminating minimums does not stop anyone from building a garage. It stops the city from making every project build one. Where spillover is a real problem, cities have better tools: residential permit parking, curb pricing, and neighborhood-level rules. Those respond to actual conditions instead of a blanket assumption made years before the building exists.

The housing argument is the strongest. Many cities face shortages that push rents beyond what working families can pay. Every mandated space on a lot is a unit that may not pencil out, a small business that cannot afford the site, or a sidewalk and a tree that never get built. Removing minimums lets a developer put 40 homes on a lot that could only support 30 if it had to park 40 cars. That difference is the gap between a project that gets built and one that stays a vacant lot.

There is also a question of fairness. Minimum requirements take choices away from the people who bear the costs. A developer building for a car-free young professional, a retirement community or a transit-oriented corridor should not have to pretend otherwise. Those who want to drive can still rent a space and pay for it directly, which is how the thing should work anyway.

Cities should not replace minimums with new maximums or a different one-size-fits-all formula. The goal is to stop the state from deciding in advance how much parking every building needs. Remove the mandate, keep the zoning envelope, enforce design and safety standards, and watch what the market does. Many projects will still include parking. The difference is that the parking will be a decision, not a dictate.

Cars are not the enemy of a good city. But a rule that forces every home to come with a garage is not a plan for mobility or affordability. It is a subsidy for storage, paid by the people least likely to use it. Cities should end it.
