**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American suburb and you will find a quiet monument to central planning. It is the parking lot: acres of empty asphalt, sized for the busiest shopping day of the year and idle the other 364. Nobody chose this landscape. It was mandated, line by line, in zoning codes that tell builders exactly how many spaces they must provide. Cities should delete those mandates.

Start with what a minimum parking requirement actually is. It is a rule that forces every new restaurant, apartment block, and corner shop to supply a fixed number of spaces, whether or not the owner wants them, whether or not customers will use them. A typical code might demand one space per 250 square feet of retail, or two per apartment. The numbers often trace back to guesses and to manuals built from suburban sites with ample free parking. They were never calibrated to a particular place, and they rarely get revisited.

The first problem is cost. Parking is not free to build, and the bill does not vanish. A surface space can run several thousand dollars, and a structured or underground space can cost tens of thousands. Those costs land in rents, in menu prices, and in the price of a loaf of bread. Tenants who own no car still pay for the spaces. Housing is where this stings most. Every mandated space is money that could have gone toward a unit someone can actually live in, and in some projects the parking requirement is the difference between a building that pencils out and one that never gets built.

The second problem is land. Parking eats space that could hold homes, shops, and offices. A single surface space, with its share of the driving aisle, takes up around 300 square feet, which is larger than many studio apartments' bedrooms. Multiply that across a downtown and you get what some researchers have documented in cities like Los Angeles and Houston: a core where more area is devoted to storing cars than to sheltering people. Dense, walkable places lose their density, and as a result they lose their walkability too. The destinations spread apart. The sidewalk becomes a hike across a lot. Driving turns from a choice into a necessity, which then seems to justify the next round of parking requirements.

That loop is the heart of the case. Minimums assume everyone will drive, make driving cheaper to assume, and so ensure that everyone drives.

Defenders raise fair objections, and they deserve answers. The most common is spillover: if a new building provides too few spaces, its residents will clog the neighborhood's streets. This is a real concern. But it is a problem of pricing, not of mandates. Cities that charge nothing for curb parking invite exactly this. A permit program, or a meter that rises until a few spaces always stay open, handles spillover directly. It also raises revenue, which can fund street improvements in the same neighborhood. Forcing every developer to overbuild in order to compensate for the city's own free-curb policy gets the logic backwards.

A second objection says that without requirements, developers will build no parking at all. They won't. Lenders want parking. Tenants want parking. Retailers who depend on drivers know it better than any zoning official does. When Buffalo scrapped its minimums citywide in 2017, it did not become a car-free city overnight, and developers there still build parking where the market wants it. What changed is that the decision moved from a code written decades ago to the person with money on the line. That person usually has better information than a committee.

A third objection is that transit and biking are not good enough yet to make car-light living realistic. Perhaps so in many places. Nothing about repealing minimums forces anyone to give up a car. It simply stops punishing those who would, and it lets a site with a bus line, a bike lane, and a grocery store next door make a different choice than a site on a highway frontage road. One rule for both makes no sense.

Reform also need not be reckless. A city nervous about change can begin by dropping minimums downtown, near transit, or for small businesses and affordable housing, then widen the exemption as the sky stays up. Many places have already done so, from Minneapolis to Anchorage to parts of California, where state law now limits requirements near transit. The sky did stay up.

What remains is a question of who should decide. A planner in a city office cannot know how many spaces a dentist's clinic or a bakery will need. The dentist and the baker can. Let them choose, let the curb carry a sensible price, and let the land that parking would have swallowed go to something that pays taxes, houses families, and gives a street its life.

Cities adopted these rules in good faith, and good faith is not the same as good results. The evidence is in the asphalt, mostly empty. Remove the mandate.
