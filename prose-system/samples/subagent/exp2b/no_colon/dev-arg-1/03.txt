Scrap the Parking Mandate

Every city has a rule that most residents have never read and that quietly shapes the place they live. It says that every new building must include a set number of parking spaces, whether or not anyone wants them. A restaurant must provide so many spots per thousand square feet. An apartment block must provide so many per unit. The number is set in advance by the city, not by the people building or using the place. I think cities should repeal these minimum parking requirements entirely. The case is strong on cost, on housing, on land, and on basic fairness.

Start with cost. A surface parking space takes roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles and the access lanes. A space in a structure or underground garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and in expensive cities far more. That money does not vanish. It lands in the rent, the price of the sandwich, and the cost of the new clinic. Researchers who have studied mandated parking, most notably Donald Shoup, have long argued that these requirements bundle a costly good into everything else and hide the price from the person who pays it. A tenant without a car still pays for the space, because the landlord had to build it and has to recover the cost somewhere.

That leads to the fairness problem. Roughly one in ten American households has no car at all, and the share is much higher among the poor, the elderly, and the young. Parking mandates ask these people to subsidize the storage of other people's vehicles. A rule that sounds neutral turns out to be a quiet transfer from those with less to those with more.

Next comes housing. Nearly every large city says it wants more homes, yet many of those same cities require so much parking that small, cheap projects cannot pencil out. A modest lot that could hold eight apartments might hold only five once the ramp and the stalls are included. Some sites cannot be built on at all, because the required parking will not physically fit. When a city sets a parking floor, it sets a ceiling on how much housing can exist. Cities that have lifted the requirements, such as Buffalo, which scrapped citywide minimums in 2017, and Minneapolis and Seattle in various forms, have seen builders propose more homes and more flexible designs. The effect is not magic, and zoning and permitting still matter, but removing the mandate takes one real obstacle off the table.

Now the land. Parking is among the least productive uses of valuable urban ground. A lot full of empty stalls pays little in property tax compared with the same lot holding shops, offices, or homes. Look at an aerial photo of almost any American downtown and you will see seas of asphalt that would have been something more useful had the code not demanded otherwise. Those gaps also spread everything out. When each destination needs its own private lot, walking from one to the next becomes unpleasant and driving becomes the only sensible choice. The mandate does not merely respond to car dependence. It produces it.

The strongest objection is that without mandates, developers will skimp, and neighborhoods will drown in cars competing for street spaces. This worry deserves a serious answer, and the answer has three parts. First, repealing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it wherever customers want it, because a building with no parking is hard to lease in many places. The difference is that the amount will follow demand rather than a formula copied from a manual. Those manuals, incidentally, rest on thin evidence. The traditional standards were drawn from surveys of suburban sites at peak demand, often with free parking, and then treated as universal law.

Second, spillover onto the street is a pricing problem, and it has a pricing solution. If curb space is free, it will always be scarce. If a city charges a fair market rate, perhaps adjusted to keep a few spaces open on every block, and returns the revenue to the neighborhood, the shortage disappears. Residents can even be given permits. Many cities already do this well. The remedy for crowded curbs is to manage the curb, not to force every landlord to build a private garage.

Third, the choice is not between a mandate and chaos. Cities can keep the tools that make sense, such as accessible spaces for people with disabilities, loading zones, and bicycle storage. They can also set maximums in the few places where transit is excellent and driving would be a burden on everyone. What they should stop doing is dictating the minimum number of cars a private owner must plan for.

There is also a matter of humility. Planners cannot know how many spaces a given taxi-dispatch office, yoga studio, or senior apartment building needs. The owner, who bears the cost and wants tenants and customers, has every incentive to find out. Habits are changing as well, with ride-hailing, delivery, remote work, and electric bikes all altering how people get around. A fixed ratio written years ago cannot keep up. Letting the market and the owner decide adapts automatically.

Cities that cling to minimum parking requirements are making a bet that the car-centered pattern of the last century should be locked in for the next one. That bet is expensive, unfair, and bad for housing and for tax bases. Repeal the mandate, price the curb sensibly, and let people build what they actually need. The cars that deserve a place to park will get one. The rest of us will stop paying for spaces we never use.
