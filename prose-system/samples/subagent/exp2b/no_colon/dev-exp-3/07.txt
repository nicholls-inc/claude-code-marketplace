Imagine two strangers, Alice and Bob, who want to share a secret number. They can only talk across a crowded room where everyone can hear every word. Public-key cryptography lets them end up holding the same secret, even though a listener hears everything they say. This explanation builds up to that result using ideas from school maths.

**The problem with ordinary secrets**

The oldest way to keep a message private is a shared key, a secret that both people know. Think of a code where every letter is shifted three places along the alphabet. If Alice and Bob both know "shift by three", they can write to each other in private.

The weak point is how they agree on the shift in the first place. If they meet in person, that is easy. If they have never met, they have to announce it across the room, and then everyone knows it. For centuries this looked unavoidable, because any secret you share has to travel somewhere. In the 1970s, researchers found a way around it using a special kind of sum.

**Mixing paint**

A famous picture helps first. Suppose Alice and Bob each have a large tub of yellow paint, and everyone in the room has the same yellow. This colour is public.

Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red, and mixes a good amount of it into her yellow. She sends the resulting orange tub across the room. Bob does the same with his own private colour, say blue, and sends his greenish tub across.

Anyone watching sees the yellow, the orange tub and the greenish tub. Now Alice takes Bob's tub and adds her own private red. Bob takes Alice's tub and adds his own private blue. Both end up with the same final mixture, yellow plus red plus blue, because the order of mixing does not matter.

The listener cannot copy this. They would need to know the red or the blue, and separating a mixed paint back into its ingredients is very hard. The key idea is that mixing is easy, and unmixing is practically impossible.

Mathematics has its own version of this. It needs an operation that is easy to do one way and extremely hard to undo.

**Clock arithmetic**

The operation uses clock arithmetic. On a clock with 12 hours, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, not 15. You go round and round, and only the leftover matters. Mathematicians call the leftover the remainder, and they write "mod" for it. So 15 mod 12 is 3, since 15 divided by 12 leaves 3.

You can use any clock size. We will use a clock with 23 positions, numbered 0 to 22. Then 30 mod 23 is 7, and 46 mod 23 is 0.

The second ingredient is powers. Writing 5^3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. On our 23-position clock, 125 mod 23 is 10, because 23 times 5 is 115 and 125 minus 115 leaves 10.

Here is the useful part. If I tell you that 5 to some secret power, taken on this clock, lands on 8, can you tell me the power? With small numbers you could just try 5^1, 5^2, 5^3 and so on until you hit 8. But the numbers are scrambled by the clock. The results jump around with no obvious pattern, so you cannot tell whether you are getting closer. With a clock of 23 you could simply try every possibility. With a clock of several hundred digits, there are more possibilities than atoms in the universe, and trying them all is hopeless.

Going forward, however, is quick. Computers have tricks to calculate huge powers on a clock in a fraction of a second. So we have our paint-mixing operation. Raising to a power is the easy mixing, and finding the power from the result is the impossible unmixing.

**The exchange, step by step**

Here is the full exchange with small numbers, so you can follow every step. In real life the numbers are enormously bigger.

First, Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers. The clock size is 23, and the base is 5. This is the shared yellow paint. Everyone in the room hears these, and that is fine.

Second, each person chooses a private number and tells nobody. Alice picks 6. Bob picks 15.

Third, each person works out their public number by raising the base to their private number on the clock.

Alice calculates 5^6, which is 15,625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 with 8 left over. So Alice's public number is 8.

Bob calculates 5^15. You can do this in steps. 5^2 is 25, which is 2 on the clock. Squaring again, 5^4 is 2 times 2, which is 4. Squaring again, 5^8 is 16. Now 5^15 is 5^8 times 5^4 times 5^2 times 5, which is 16 times 4 times 2 times 5, or 640. Dividing 640 by 23 gives 27 with 19 left over. So Bob's public number is 19.

Fourth, they swap public numbers across the room. Alice announces 8. Bob announces 19. The listener now knows 23, 5, 8 and 19.

Fifth, each person raises the number they received to their own private power.

Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private 6. Since 19 is the same as minus 4 on this clock, 19^6 behaves like (minus 4)^6, which is 4,096. Dividing by 23 gives 178 with 2 left over. Alice gets 2.

Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private 15. Since 8 is 2 times 2 times 2, 8^15 is 2^45. A handy fact is that 2^11 is 2,048, which is exactly 89 times 23 plus 1, so 2^11 equals 1 on this clock. Then 2^45 is 2^44 times 2, which is the same as 1 times 2, giving 2. Bob also gets 2.

They have both arrived at 2. This is the shared secret, and it never travelled across the room.

**Why both people get the same answer**

This is not luck. Alice's final number is the base raised to her power, then raised to Bob's power. Bob's is the base raised to his power, then raised to Alice's power. Raising a power to a power just multiplies the exponents. For us, 5^(6 times 15) is the same as 5^(15 times 6), since multiplication does not care about order. This is the same fact as the paint mixing, where order did not matter.

**What the listener can and cannot do**

An eavesdropper knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the secret, they need Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. Our tiny clock makes this easy to crack by trying every option, which is why it is only a teaching example. But when the clock has hundreds of digits, the best known methods would take longer than the age of the Earth, even on enormous computers.

This is why the scheme is trusted. Nobody has proved that no shortcut exists, but generations of clever people have searched and found none for properly chosen numbers.

**What happens next**

The shared secret is then used as the key for a fast ordinary code, the "shift by three" idea but vastly stronger. So public-key methods solve the hard problem of agreeing on a key, and the simple codes do the heavy lifting of protecting the actual messages.

This happens constantly. When your phone connects to a bank or a shop through a padlock symbol in the browser, a similar exchange takes place in a fraction of a second, between two parties who have never met. Neither of them ever had to whisper a secret, and neither of them could have been overheard giving it away.

**The big picture**

The whole idea rests on one lopsided fact. Some calculations are easy to perform and almost impossible to reverse. By building the exchange around such a calculation, Alice and Bob each add something private to a public starting point, and the maths guarantees that their private additions combine to the same result. The room hears every word, yet the secret stays between the two of them.
