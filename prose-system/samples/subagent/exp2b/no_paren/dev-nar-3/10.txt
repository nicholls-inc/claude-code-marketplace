On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with the ordinary noise of a working neighborhood. Children played near the waterfront. Dockworkers ate lunch. Horses and trucks clattered along Commercial Street, and the elevated railway rumbled overhead. Beside it all stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to become industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions as the world emerged from the Great War. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply, in a hurry to profit from wartime demand. Its walls were thinner than they should have been, and no one had properly tested its strength. When the tank was first filled, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cups. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown, which hid the stains and nothing else. Residents also reported groans and rumbles from the structure, but nobody with authority took them seriously.

Just after half past noon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a sound like machine gun fire as the rivets shot out, followed by a roar as the steel burst apart. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen feet high at its front, swept out across the streets at around thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a crushing weight, more than twelve thousand tons of thick liquid.

The wave tore buildings from their foundations. It knocked a firehouse sideways and bent the supports of the elevated railway so badly that a train narrowly avoided disaster. Wagons, horses and people were swept up and flung against walls. Those who were caught found themselves held fast, as the syrup that had been warm and fluid cooled quickly in the winter air and thickened around them. Rescuers waded in to their knees and waists, struggling with every step. Sailors from a nearby ship, police, Red Cross workers and soldiers rushed to help, dragging the trapped from the sticky mass.

Twenty-one people died, and about one hundred and fifty were injured. Among the dead were children and a number of city workers. The harbor ran brown with molasses for weeks afterward, and the smell lingered in the neighborhood for decades, especially on hot summer days.

The aftermath was as significant as the disaster. Purity Distilling blamed anarchists, claiming that a bomb had destroyed the tank. But a lawyer named Hugh Ogden led a long investigation, hearing from thousands of witnesses and engineering experts. In a landmark class action, the court found the company liable, concluding that the tank had been poorly designed and badly built. The company paid out about six hundred thousand dollars in settlements.

The case helped reshape American practice. It contributed to stricter rules requiring engineers to sign off on construction plans, and it showed that corporations could be held accountable for negligence on a grand scale. A strange and almost comic disaster left a serious legacy.
