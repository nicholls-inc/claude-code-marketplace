A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the rotation of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time that do not quite agree. One is astronomical: a day is the time the planet takes to spin once, and that spin is slightly irregular, gradually slowing because of tidal friction from the Moon and shifting with the movement of oceans, ice and the molten core. The other is atomic: since 1967 the length of a second has been defined by the vibrations of caesium atoms, which tick with extraordinary steadiness. Because the planet is a less perfect timekeeper than the atoms, the two drift apart by a few thousandths of a second every day.

To keep the difference small, international timekeepers introduced the leap second in 1972. When the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation threatened to exceed 0.9 seconds, an extra second was inserted at the end of June or December, so that a minute briefly contained 61 seconds. Twenty-seven have been added in all, the most recent at the end of 2016. For most people this made no difference at all, since nobody notices a single second on a wristwatch.

For the computers that now run the world, however, it has been a persistent headache. Software generally assumes that every minute has exactly 60 seconds, and an unexpected 61st can confuse it. Leap seconds have been blamed for crashes and outages at major websites, and for errors in financial trading, telecommunications and satellite navigation systems, where timestamps must be precise to a tiny fraction of a second. Because the Earth's spin is unpredictable, leap seconds cannot be scheduled years ahead, which makes them hard to plan for. Companies have built their own workarounds, such as spreading the extra second across several hours, and these differ from one system to another, creating further inconsistency.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of the second, voted to abandon the practice by 2035. The Earth has lately been spinning a little faster than expected, which could eventually require the first ever removal of a second, a change that would be even riskier for software. The plan is not to ignore the problem for ever: the gap between atomic and astronomical time will simply be allowed to grow, and in a century or so it may reach a minute or more. Scientists expect that a larger correction can then be made far less frequently, with ample warning.
