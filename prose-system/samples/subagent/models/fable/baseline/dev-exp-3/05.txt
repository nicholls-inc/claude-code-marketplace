# How two strangers can agree on a secret while others listen

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met, and everything they say to each other can be overheard by an eavesdropper, Eve. They want to end up with a secret number that both of them know and Eve does not. Once they have it, they can use it as the key to scramble all their later messages.

This sounds impossible. Anything Alice tells Bob, Eve hears too, so how can Bob learn something Eve doesn't? The answer, found in the 1970s, is that Alice and Bob never send the secret at all. Each sends a piece of a calculation, and each finishes that calculation privately with a number they never reveal. Eve hears both pieces but cannot finish the calculation herself.

The method is called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, and it is used every time your web browser shows a padlock.

## The idea in paint

Imagine that mixing two paints is easy, but separating a mixture back into its original colours is impossible.

1. Alice and Bob agree, in the open, on a common colour, say yellow. Eve hears this.
2. Alice picks a secret colour of her own, mixes it with the yellow, and sends the mixture to Bob. Bob does the same with his own secret colour and sends his mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures.
3. Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her secret colour. Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his secret colour.

Both now hold the same final colour: yellow plus Alice's secret plus Bob's secret. The order of mixing doesn't matter.

Eve has yellow and the two mixtures. To make the final colour she would need one of the secret colours on its own, and she cannot un-mix paint to get it. If she stirs the two mixtures together, she gets a colour with a double helping of yellow, which is the wrong one.

So the method needs two things: an operation that is easy to do and hard to undo, and one where the order doesn't matter. Arithmetic provides both.

## The one-way operation: clock arithmetic

On a clock face, 5 hours after 10 o'clock is 3 o'clock, not 15 o'clock. You count round and start again after 12. Mathematicians do the same with any number in place of 12: divide by that number and keep only the remainder.

We will use a clock with 23 hours. On this clock, 25 is the same as 2, because 25 divided by 23 leaves remainder 2.

Now take the number 5 and raise it to successive powers on this clock:

| Power | Calculation | Result on the 23 clock |
|---|---|---|
| 5¹ | 5 | 5 |
| 5² | 25 | 2 |
| 5³ | 2 × 5 = 10 | 10 |
| 5⁴ | 10 × 5 = 50 | 4 |
| 5⁵ | 4 × 5 = 20 | 20 |
| 5⁶ | 20 × 5 = 100 | 8 |

The results (5, 2, 10, 4, 20, 8) jump about with no visible pattern. That is the important feature. In ordinary arithmetic, powers of 5 grow steadily, so if I tell you a power of 5 is 15,625 you can work out which power it is by seeing how big it is. On the clock, size tells you nothing. If I tell you the answer is 8, the only way to find the power is to try them one after another.

Going forwards is easy: pick a power and calculate. Going backwards, from the result to the power, is a search. This is our paint that cannot be un-mixed.

## The exchange in numbers

Alice and Bob agree in the open to use the 23 clock and the starting number 5. Eve hears this.

**Alice** picks a secret number, 6, and tells nobody. She works out 5⁶ on the clock, which is 8, and sends **8** to Bob.

**Bob** picks a secret number, 15, and tells nobody. He works out 5¹⁵ on the clock, which is 19, and sends **19** to Alice.

Now each takes the number they received and raises it to their own secret power:

- Alice works out 19⁶ on the clock and gets **2**.
- Bob works out 8¹⁵ on the clock and gets **2**.

They have the same number, and neither of them ever sent it.

## Why they get the same answer

Alice's 8 is really 5⁶. When Bob raises it to the 15th power, he is working out 5⁶ multiplied by itself 15 times, which is 5 raised to the power 6 × 15 = 90.

Bob's 19 is really 5¹⁵. When Alice raises it to the 6th power, she is working out 5 raised to the power 15 × 6 = 90.

Since 6 × 15 and 15 × 6 are the same, both have calculated 5⁹⁰ on the clock. This is the "order of mixing doesn't matter" property. Taking remainders along the way does not change the final remainder, so the clock does not spoil the match.

## What Eve is left with

Eve knows the clock size (23), the starting number (5), and the two numbers that crossed the line (8 and 19). To get the secret she needs 5⁹⁰, and to get that she needs either the 6 or the 15. Multiplying 8 by 19 does not help: that gives 5 to the power 6 + 15 = 21, not 5 to the power 90.

Her only route is to work backwards from 8 to find Alice's 6, which is the trial-and-error search described earlier.

With a clock of size 23, the search takes a few seconds. Real systems use a clock whose size is a number hundreds of digits long, and secret powers of similar length. Alice and Bob can still do their calculations in a fraction of a second, because there are shortcuts for raising to a large power (repeated squaring gets you to enormous powers in a few hundred steps). No comparable shortcut is known for going backwards. Trying every possibility would take the world's computers far longer than the age of the universe.

The security rests on that gap between easy forwards and infeasible backwards. Nobody has proved that a fast backwards method cannot exist, but decades of searching by mathematicians have not found one for ordinary computers.

## Where "public key" comes from

Each person has two numbers. The one they keep (6 for Alice, 15 for Bob) is their **private key**. The one they send (8 and 19) is their **public key**, and it can be announced to the whole world without harm. Public-key cryptography is the family of methods built this way: a pair of linked numbers, one safe to publish, the other never shared, with a one-way calculation connecting them.

## One gap that remains

The exchange keeps the secret from someone who only listens. It does not, by itself, tell Alice that the number 19 really came from Bob. If Eve can intercept and replace messages, she could pose as Bob to Alice and as Alice to Bob, and agree a separate secret with each. Real systems close this gap with a second public-key tool, the digital signature, which lets Bob prove a message is his. That is the job of the certificates your browser checks behind the padlock.

With that addition, two computers that have never been in contact can agree a secret key in a few thousandths of a second, over a line anyone may be listening to.
