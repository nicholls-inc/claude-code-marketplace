In the last days of August 1854, cholera arrived in Soho, a crowded district in London's West End. Nobody knew how it spread. Most doctors blamed "miasma," foul air rising from rotting matter, and the official health authorities followed that thinking. John Snow, a physician who had already spent years arguing otherwise, believed the disease travelled in water. He had set this out in 1849, and few had listened.

Within days the streets around Broad Street were emptying of the living and filling with the dead. Hundreds died in about ten days. Families who could leave did so. Those who stayed shut their doors and waited. Snow lived and practised nearby, and he began walking the streets, talking to families, and writing down where each death had happened and where the victim had got their drinking water.

His method was simple, and that was its strength. He did not start with a theory about the disease. He started with people. He asked households where they fetched their water, and many gave the same answer: a public pump on Broad Street, now Broadwick Street, a handle-operated pump standing in the middle of the road. Other water sources in the area did not show the same pattern.

Snow then did something unusual for his day. He drew a map. On a street plan he marked each death with a small black bar. The bars bunched together around the Broad Street pump. Snow also looked at who was not dying. A brewery on Broad Street, whose workers drank beer rather than water, had almost no deaths. A workhouse on Poland Street, which had its own well, was largely spared. A woman in Hampstead, several miles away, had liked the taste of Broad Street water and had it carried to her in jugs. She died after drinking it.

Snow took this evidence to the parish authorities, who were not persuaded at first. The pump was a respected public amenity, and the local officials were reluctant to blame it. Snow argued that the water itself must be poisoned. He suspected a cesspool, a pit for household waste, lying close to the well and leaking into it. The Reverend Henry Whitehead, a curate in the parish who had been keeping his own records, gathered more evidence. He found that many people who had moved away still walked back to draw water from the pump. On 8 September the handle of the Broad Street pump was removed.

The outbreak was already slowing by then. Many residents had fled, and Snow was honest that the epidemic might have run its course anyway. But the pump was the best explanation he had, and the deaths fell away quickly after the handle came off.

The stronger proof came the following year, from an experiment nobody had designed. Two water companies supplied houses in south London. The Lambeth company drew its water upstream of the city's sewage outlets. The Southwark and Vauxhall company drew from the river downstream, where it was contaminated by London's waste. Because the two companies' customers lived side by side, the comparison removed many other differences between households. Snow found that houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall had a far higher death rate. He published this evidence, along with his map, in the second edition of *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera* in 1855.

Snow's idea was not accepted at once. Miasma theory lingered for years. Snow never identified the microbe itself. An Italian anatomist, Filippo Pacini, had seen the bacterium in 1854, but his work was largely ignored. Robert Koch confirmed the cause in 1883. What Snow did was show where the disease spread, and his lasting lesson is about method. A careful count, a map, and the willingness to ask ordinary people where they got their water can locate a cause long before anyone can see it.
