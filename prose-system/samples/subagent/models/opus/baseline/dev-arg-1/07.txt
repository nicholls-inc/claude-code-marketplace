Let the Market Park the Car: Why Cities Should Scrap Parking Minimums

Somewhere in your city's zoning code is a table that decides more about how your neighborhood looks than any architect does. It says how many parking spaces each new building must provide: so many per apartment, per thousand square feet of shop floor, per restaurant seat, sometimes per church pew. These rules are called minimum parking requirements. Most residents have never heard of them, yet they shape almost everything we build. Cities should get rid of them.

The case starts with a simple question: why does a city government decide how much parking a building needs? We don't make bakeries provide a set number of chairs or require every apartment to come with a guest bedroom. We let owners, tenants and customers work out what they need. Parking is the strange exception, and the reasoning behind it has never been strong. Many of the ratios in today's codes were copied from neighboring towns or taken from studies of suburban sites at peak demand, then applied everywhere, from downtown cores to transit corridors. The planner Donald Shoup, whose book "The High Cost of Free Parking" set off much of the reform movement, has spent decades showing how weak the evidence behind these numbers is. They are guesses that became law.

Those guesses are expensive. A parking space is not a painted rectangle. Counting the aisles and ramps needed to reach it, a surface space takes up about as much land as a small studio apartment, and a space in a structured or underground garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build. When a city requires that space, the cost does not disappear. It is folded into rents, home prices and the price of everything sold in the shops downstairs. People who don't own a car, who tend to be poorer, younger or older, pay for parking they never use.

The bigger cost is everything that never gets built. On small or oddly shaped urban lots, the required parking often doesn't fit, or it eats so much of the budget that the project stops making sense. The corner apartment building above a café, the conversion of an old warehouse into flats, the modest infill project on a vacant lot: these are the first things parking minimums kill. Cities in the middle of a housing shortage are, in effect, banning housing in order to protect car storage.

Then there is what the rules do to the street. Parking minimums are a major reason so many commercial strips look like a few buildings scattered across a sea of asphalt. Shops get pushed back from the sidewalk behind their lots, and walking from one to the next becomes unpleasant or unsafe. Once everything is spread out, driving becomes the only practical way to get around, which seems to confirm that all that parking was needed. The policy creates the demand it claims only to be meeting. It also works against the climate goals most cities have adopted: you cannot credibly promise to cut transportation emissions while legally requiring every new building to make driving as easy as possible.

The usual objection is that without minimums, developers will build too little parking and cars will flood neighborhood streets. The concern is real, but it points to a different fix. Developers have every reason to provide parking where buyers and tenants will pay for it. A grocery store in a car-dependent area is not going to build a store nobody can drive to. What abolition changes is that parking gets built where it is actually wanted, in the amount that is actually wanted, instead of by a fixed ratio regardless of location. The spillover worry is better handled by managing the curb directly with residential permit zones, meters and pricing that keeps a few spaces free on each block. Those tools target the problem itself. Minimums force every building to overbuild just in case.

This is not a theory. Buffalo, New York, dropped citywide parking minimums in 2017. Minneapolis followed in 2021, and California has barred cities from imposing them near major transit. New Zealand removed them from its larger cities by national policy. In these places, new projects have not been built with zero parking across the board. Developers have kept building parking, just less of it, and in proportions that fit each site. Smaller buildings that would have been illegal or unaffordable before have become possible. The predicted parking chaos has largely failed to show up.

Ending minimums is also one of the cheapest reforms a city can make. It requires no new spending, no new agency and no bond measure. It just takes a bad rule off the books and trusts people to decide how much parking they need, as we already do for nearly everything else about a building.

Cities say they want affordable housing, lively streets, thriving small businesses and lower emissions. Parking minimums work against every one of those goals. It is time to delete that table from the zoning code.
