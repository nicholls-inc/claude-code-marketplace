How Two Strangers Can Agree on a Secret in Public

The puzzle

Imagine you want to whisper a secret to someone you have never met, but the only way to reach them is by shouting across a crowded room. Everyone can hear every word. It sounds impossible to agree on a secret under those conditions, yet this happens millions of times a day. Every time your phone connects to a bank or a shopping site, it and the website's computer settle on a shared secret over a connection that strangers could be watching.

The trick is called public-key cryptography. You do not need any computing knowledge to follow it. You need only the kind of arithmetic taught at school, plus one idea: some jobs are easy to do but extremely hard to undo.

Easy to do, hard to undo

Think about mixing paint. If you stir yellow and blue together, you get green in seconds. But if someone hands you a pot of green and asks you to separate it back into the exact yellow and blue you started with, you cannot. Mixing is easy. Unmixing is practically impossible.

Here is the famous way to use that idea. Alice and Bob want a shared secret colour. First, they publicly agree on a starting colour, say yellow. Everyone in the room hears this, and that is fine.

Next, each of them privately picks a secret colour. Alice picks red and Bob picks blue. They never say these aloud.

Each mixes their private colour into the public yellow. Alice gets orange, and Bob gets green. They then shout the results across the room. Everyone hears "orange" and "green", but nobody can unmix them to find the private colours.

Now comes the clever step. Alice takes Bob's green and adds her private red. Bob takes Alice's orange and adds his private blue. Both end up with the same final colour, because both have mixed yellow, red and blue together. The order of mixing does not matter.

The eavesdropper holds yellow, orange and green. To reach the final colour, they would need red or blue, and those were never spoken. Unmixing is too hard. So Alice and Bob now share a secret that no listener possesses.

Paint is only a picture. Computers cannot mix paint, so they use arithmetic that behaves the same way: easy one way, very hard in reverse.

Clock arithmetic

You already know a kind of arithmetic that wraps around: telling the time. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You went past 12 and started again. Mathematicians call this "modular arithmetic", and the number you wrap at is the "modulus". On a normal clock the modulus is 12.

We will use a clock with 23 positions instead, numbered 0 to 22. We write "mod 23" to mean "wrap around at 23". So 25 mod 23 is 2, because 25 is one full lap (23) plus 2 more.

We also need powers. Five to the power 3 means 5 x 5 x 5 = 125. The numbers grow quickly, but on our 23-position clock we just keep only the remainder after wrapping.

Here is the key fact. Suppose I tell you that 5 raised to some secret power, wrapped on the 23-clock, gives 8. Can you find the power? With a clock this small, yes, you could just try 1, 2, 3 and so on until you hit 8. But if the clock has hundreds of digits, there are more possibilities than atoms in the universe. Going forward (raising to a power) is quick. Going backward (finding which power was used) is hopeless. This is our "mixing versus unmixing".

The exchange, step by step

Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers: the clock size 23, and a starting number 5. These play the role of the yellow paint. Anyone may know them.

Alice secretly chooses the number 6. Bob secretly chooses the number 15. These are the private colours.

Alice computes 5 to the power 6, wrapped on the 23-clock. The full number is 15,625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 whole laps with 8 left over. So her result is 8. She announces "8" to the room.

Bob computes 5 to the power 15 on the same clock. The raw number is huge (over 30 billion), but wrapping gives 19. He announces "19".

Now each uses the other's announcement together with their own secret.

Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power, 6. The result, wrapped on the 23-clock, is 2.

Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power, 15. Wrapped on the 23-clock, this also gives 2.

They both arrive at 2. That is their shared secret.

Why do they match? Alice effectively computed 5 to the power (15 x 6), and Bob computed 5 to the power (6 x 15). Multiplication does not care about order, so 15 x 6 equals 6 x 15, and both reach the same place. This is the mathematical version of "mixing order does not matter".

What the eavesdropper sees

A listener heard 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret 2, they would need Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. In our toy example they could find these by trial and error, because the clock is tiny. That is why real systems use enormous clocks, with numbers hundreds of digits long. At that size, even the fastest computers on Earth working for longer than the age of the universe could not try enough possibilities. The listener has everything except the one thing that matters.

From a shared secret to a safe conversation

Once Alice and Bob share a secret number, they can use it as the key for an ordinary scrambling method that both sides can run quickly. Think of it as a lock whose key only they hold. Everything they say afterwards is scrambled, so the crowd hears only noise.

This is why the whole idea is called "public key". Alice's announced 8 is a public value that anyone may see. Her 6 is private and never leaves her. Two linked numbers, one public and one private, make the system work.

A second flavour: locks anyone can close

There is another famous way to use the same principle, and it works like a padlock. Suppose Bob makes thousands of identical open padlocks and hands them out freely. He keeps the only key. Anyone, including Alice, can snap a padlock shut on a box containing a message, and send the locked box to Bob. Only Bob can open it. The padlock is the public key and the key is the private one.

In real systems the padlock is a number, and "snapping it shut" is a calculation. Bob builds the pair by multiplying two huge prime numbers together. Multiplying them is easy. Taking the result and working out which two primes made it is brutally hard when the numbers are big enough. That is again easy one way, hard in reverse, with Bob's two primes as the secret that makes unlocking possible.

What is actually being trusted

It is worth being honest about the limits. Nobody has proved that the reverse direction is truly impossible. It is simply that after decades of effort by many clever people, no quick method has been found. The security rests on that shared experience of failure, together with the sheer size of the numbers.

There is also a practical danger. If an attacker can pretend to be Bob and trick Alice into talking to them, the maths will faithfully produce a secret between Alice and the impostor. So real systems add a way to check you are really talking to the right person, using certificates that vouch for identities.

The big idea

Strangers can share a secret in public because some operations are one-way streets. Each person mixes a private ingredient into something public, publishes the mixture, and combines the other person's mixture with their own ingredient. The order does not matter, so both reach the same result, while the listener, holding only mixtures, cannot unmix them. A little clock arithmetic is all it takes to turn that idea into something a computer can do.
