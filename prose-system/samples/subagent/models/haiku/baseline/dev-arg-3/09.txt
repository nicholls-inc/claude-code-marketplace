Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every autumn, somewhere in a primary classroom, a seven-year-old sits with a pencil and a sheet of loops and ladders, learning to connect one letter to the next. Critics ask why. Keyboards are everywhere, speech recognition works well enough, and the hours spent on joined-up writing could go to coding, reading, phonics, or any of the other demands on a crowded curriculum. The question deserves a serious answer. My answer is yes: primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it early, deliberately and well.

The strongest objection is that handwriting is obsolete. Most adult communication now happens on screens, and many adults type faster than they write. Why train a skill that almost nobody needs?

The premise overstates the change. Handwriting is still used constantly. People sign forms, label things, write cards, take notes in meetings, fill in paper exams in many countries, and scribble lists and margin notes they use to think. Some of these uses will shrink, but the more important point is different. The real question is not whether children will write by hand as adults. It is what handwriting does for them as learners, and the evidence on that is more interesting than the obsolescence argument allows.

Researchers have compared handwriting with typing and tracing for years. Studies by Longcamp and colleagues found that children who practised forming letters by hand recognised and remembered those letters better than children who typed them or traced printed examples. The physical act of producing a shape appears to anchor the shape in memory. Neuroimaging work with adults, including a 2017 EEG study by van der Meer and van der Weel, has suggested that handwriting engages wider networks of brain activity than typing does. These studies are small and the field is still developing, but the direction is consistent. Writing by hand is not just a way of recording letters. It helps children learn them.

That argument applies to any handwriting. The case for joined-up writing in particular rests on three further points.

The first is fluency. Print letters are discrete. Each one begins and ends separately, so a word becomes a series of stops and starts. Joined writing is continuous. The hand moves along a word in a rhythm, lifting the pen only at the end. Fewer pen lifts means less effort per word, and less effort means more working memory left over for the thing a child is actually trying to say. This matters because writing is a bottleneck for many children. A pupil who has to think hard about every letter often produces short, thin pieces, not because she lacks ideas but because the mechanics consume the attention those ideas need. Researchers who study writing development, such as Steve Graham and Virginia Berninger, have repeatedly found that handwriting fluency is linked to the quality and length of children's compositions. Joined writing is one of the most direct routes to that fluency.

The second is word-level memory. When a child writes a joined word, the word is produced as a single movement with a characteristic shape and rhythm. Teachers who use joined writing often describe children who begin to recognise familiar words as patterns, the way a skilled typist recognises common letter sequences. The evidence that cursive directly improves spelling is thinner than enthusiasts claim, and I do not want to overstate it. But it is reasonable to think that producing a word as one continuous gesture supports the kind of whole-word memory that good spellers rely on, and that the joins give children an extra cue for where one letter ends and the next begins.

The third is reading. Children who learn only print cannot read most handwritten material from the past or from their own families. Letters from grandparents, old diaries, parish registers, handwritten recipes and archives are written in joined hands. A literate culture includes the ability to read its own earlier writing, and a generation that cannot do this is cut off from a part of its inheritance. Joined-up writing also has a practical benefit for some children. Many teachers report that joined letters, which begin on the baseline with consistent entry strokes, reduce confusions such as b and d. The evidence here is largely anecdotal, but it fits the idea that a consistent motor pattern gives the eye and hand a reliable way to tell letters apart.

Handwriting also builds things beyond literacy. It develops fine motor control, pressure regulation, posture and sustained attention. For many children, a short, rhythmic writing task is calming and focusing, a rare chance in the school day to work slowly and carefully. There is a social dimension too. Legible, fluent handwriting affects how work is read and graded, and how a writer is perceived. That is unfair, and teachers should know it is unfair, but it is a fact about the world children will live in. A school that teaches children to write well by hand gives them a form of presentation that no keyboard can fully replace.

The strongest counterargument is time. Joined-up writing takes lessons, practice and patience, and every hour spent on it is an hour not spent elsewhere. The reply is that the cost is modest and the payoff compounds. A few short sessions a week in the early primary years, taught properly, is enough to make joined writing automatic by the time children reach the demanding writing of later years. Once a motor pattern is automatic, it costs almost nothing to maintain. The alternative is a generation that never quite masters any handwriting and then struggles to write fluently when the demands of secondary school arrive. Teaching handwriting once, early, is cheaper than repairing its absence later.

Teaching keyboard skills belongs alongside handwriting, and the two do not compete. Children need to type, and they should be taught to do it efficiently and accurately. But typing is not a replacement for handwriting in the early years, because the benefits described above depend on the physical act of forming letters. A child who learns to type before she has a secure handwriting habit has gained a useful tool and lost a learning opportunity.

A second objection concerns children with motor difficulties. Some pupils have dysgraphia or developmental coordination disorder, and for them handwriting can be exhausting and demoralising. Forcing them into a rigid style is not a good answer. But the existence of these children is a reason for differentiated teaching, not for abandoning joined writing. Some children find joined writing easier than print because it removes the constant lifting and restarting. Others need extra practice, a looser grip, a sloped board, shorter tasks or assistive technology for longer pieces. A good school treats handwriting as a skill with a range of ability, not a pass-or-fail test of worth.

A third objection says that print is more legible. Some adults write joined letters so ornately that they are hard to read, and a joined hand that prizes flourish over clarity is a poor goal. But the argument is not for copperplate or for fancy loops. It is for a clear, simplified joined style that children can produce quickly and read easily. Schools can teach a plain joined hand with minimal decoration and clear letter forms, and many already do. The problem with ornate handwriting is a problem with how it is taught, not with the principle of joining letters.

What would be lost if primary schools stopped teaching joined-up handwriting? Not a museum piece, and not a status symbol. They would lose a tool that helps children learn letters and words, that gives them fluency for their own thinking, that connects them to the written record of their families and their culture, and that gives many of them a steady, focused task in an otherwise noisy day. They would hand those benefits to a screen, which offers less of them.

Schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should do it well: early, briefly, clearly and with room for children who find it hard. Modernising a curriculum does not mean discarding every tool that predates the keyboard. Some of those tools are still doing work that nothing else does. Keep the joins.
