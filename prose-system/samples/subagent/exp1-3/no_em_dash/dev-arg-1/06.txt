Let Cities Stop Dictating Parking

Walk through almost any American downtown and you will see a quiet monument to bad planning: a vast surface lot, mostly empty, sitting where a building could be. Much of it exists because a rule demanded it. Minimum parking requirements tell developers how many spaces they must build for every apartment, restaurant, or office, whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these rules. Not tweak them, not reduce them at the margins, but remove them.

Start with the problem the rules claim to solve. Planners argue that without mandates, new buildings will spill cars onto neighbouring streets. But the numbers used to set the mandates are famously shaky. Most codes trace back to manuals that sampled a handful of suburban sites with free parking and then treated the busiest hour as the standard for every building everywhere. The result is a system that confidently demands, say, one space per 300 square feet of retail, when nobody can explain why the figure is not 200 or 500. A rule built on guesswork should not carry the force of law.

The costs, meanwhile, are concrete. A single space in a surface lot can cost thousands of dollars to provide, and a structured or underground space can run into tens of thousands. That cost does not vanish. It is folded into rents, menu prices, and home values, and it is paid by everyone, including the many households that own no car. Tenants who bike, take the bus, or simply cannot afford a vehicle end up subsidizing the storage of other people's cars. It is a hidden tax, and it falls hardest on the people with the least money.

Housing is where this bites most. Every required space eats land and budget that could have gone to homes. On small urban lots, the parking requirement can make a project impossible, killing the modest apartment building before it is drawn. When cities complain about a housing shortage while mandating parking for each new unit, they are working against themselves. Buenos Aires, Auckland, and Minneapolis are among the places that have scrapped or sharply cut mandates in recent years, and the early evidence points to more homes being built, not fewer streets choked with cars.

Consider also what mandates do to the shape of a place. A city that requires parking for every use must spread everything out, because every building needs its own apron of asphalt. Shops sit behind lots instead of beside sidewalks. Walking distances grow, so more trips require a car, so more parking seems necessary. The rule produces the very demand it then cites as justification. Break the loop and neighbourhoods can become denser, more walkable, and more interesting, with more tax revenue per acre to show for it.

The strongest objection is that removing mandates will leave residents circling the block for a space. This worry deserves a real answer, and the answer is that repeal does not mean parking disappears. It means the decision moves from a bureaucrat to the people with the most information. Developers still build parking when customers want it, because a building without parking is hard to lease in a car-dependent area. Lenders and tenants insist on it. What changes is that a corner store near a transit stop, or a downtown conversion of an old office, is no longer forced to build spaces that sit empty. Flexibility replaces a uniform formula.

For the genuine problem of crowded curbs, cities have a better tool than mandates: manage the street itself. Charge fair prices for on-street parking, adjust them to keep a space or two open on each block, and return the revenue to the neighbourhood through benefit districts that fund sidewalks and trees. When the curb has a price, drivers find spots, businesses see turnover, and residents see money coming back. This approaches the problem directly, rather than trying to hide it by forcing private builders to over-supply.

Others worry about fairness for existing residents, especially in places with little transit. That concern is fair, and it argues for pacing the reform and pairing it with permit programs, not for keeping a flawed rule indefinitely. Even in car-heavy areas, removing the minimum does not ban parking; it simply stops forcing it. A suburban supermarket will still build a lot. A developer who wants fewer spaces, because the site is near a bus line or the tenants are students, can finally respond to reality.

There is also a plain argument about humility. Nobody, least of all a city council, knows exactly how much parking each building in a changing economy will need. Car ownership is shifting, ride-hailing and delivery have altered habits, and remote work has emptied some lots at midday. Fixed ratios freeze yesterday's assumptions into tomorrow's buildings. Letting builders and markets adjust is cheaper than rewriting the code every decade.

Cities that want more housing, lower costs, and livelier streets already have a clear first step. They can repeal minimum parking requirements, manage the curb honestly, and trust that people will build what people will use. The empty lots will tell us soon enough whether the old rules were ever needed.
