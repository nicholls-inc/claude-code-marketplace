Houston, We've Had a Problem

On the morning of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying Apollo 13 toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, who had already flown Gemini 7, Gemini 12 and Apollo 8, commanded the mission. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was on his first spaceflight. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had been the backup for the seat and was flying only because of a medical problem with the original choice. Six days before launch, Ken Mattingly had been exposed to German measles. He had no immunity, and flight surgeons removed him from the crew. Swigert took his place, which left him with a few days to learn a spacecraft he had barely trained on. Nobody expected anything dramatic. The plan was a routine landing in the Fra Mauro highlands, the third lunar landing of the program.

Two days into the flight, the crew had finished a television broadcast showing the spacecraft's interior and the view of Earth receding behind them. Afterward, the crew ran a routine stir of the cryogenic oxygen tanks in the service module, the large section of the spacecraft that carried fuel, oxygen and power for the trip. Shortly after 9 p.m. Eastern time on April 13, something inside oxygen tank No. 2 failed. The sound came through the spacecraft as a bang and a jolt. Warning lights began to flash, and the electrical system started to fail.

Swigert reported the trouble to Houston in one of the most quoted lines of the space age: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell later looked out a window and saw gas venting into space from the service module, a white cloud that trailed behind the ship. Oxygen tank No. 2 was gone. Oxygen tank No. 1 was leaking. The fuel cells that produced electricity and water depended on that oxygen, and the command module's crew air came from the same supply. The ship was losing the things it needed to keep three men alive.

Investigators later traced the explosion to a chain of decisions made long before launch. Heaters inside the tank had been wired to run on 65 volts, but the thermostatic switches were rated for 28. During ground testing, the switches had been left in a configuration that let the heaters overheat, which damaged the Teflon insulation on the wires. When the stir fan switched on, a short circuit ignited the insulation in a pure-oxygen atmosphere. The tank had also been damaged in an earlier ground-handling accident. None of this was visible to the crew that night. What they knew was that they had to abandon the command module.

They moved into the lunar module, Aquarius, which had been designed to support two men on the Moon for about two days. Now it had to support three men for roughly four days, and it was not built to do the job of keeping them warm, dry or breathing. Because the spacecraft could not safely land, the flight team in Houston, led by flight director Gene Kranz, faced a different problem. They had to get the crew home. Apollo 13 was on a free-return trajectory, which meant the Moon's gravity could swing it around the far side and back toward Earth. But that path had to be corrected, and the Aquarius descent engine was the only propulsion available. Mission controllers and the crew calculated a short burn that adjusted the course. It worked.

Cold became the next enemy. With the command module powered down, the lunar module's systems were run at minimum. The temperature inside Aquarius fell to near freezing. Condensation formed on the walls and dripped onto the crew. Water was rationed. The men took turns sleeping in the cramped cabin, and what sleep they got was poor. Haise and Lovell shared a space meant for two, and Swigert worked on power and navigation calculations with the lights mostly off.

The most urgent problem was carbon dioxide. Each astronaut exhales carbon dioxide, and it builds up quickly in a closed cabin. Aquarius had lithium hydroxide canisters that absorbed the gas, but they were round, and the command module's canisters were square. The canisters that had been loaded into the command module were the only spares available, and they did not fit the lunar module's receptacle. The levels of carbon dioxide began to climb. Mission Control had to find a way to make the square canisters work in the round opening, using only what was on board.

Engineers in Houston worked through the night. They built a prototype adapter from materials the crew had available: plastic bags, a cardboard sheet from a flight plan cover, hoses, and duct tape. They ran tests in the simulators, then read the procedure to the crew step by step. Lovell and his crewmates assembled the adapter in the cabin, and it worked well enough to keep carbon dioxide levels down for the remainder of the trip.

On April 15, the spacecraft swung around the far side of the Moon. The crew were close enough that the lunar surface filled their windows, and then they passed behind the Moon, out of radio contact with Earth. Houston waited through a long silence until the signal returned. The flyby was a success. They were now heading home, but the trip was still far from safe. The ship had to be steered for a precise re-entry, and the power that would run the command module for that final stretch had to be preserved.

Before re-entry, the crew needed to power up the command module, which had been dormant for days. Its batteries had to be carefully managed. Mission Control calculated how much power would be needed and when the systems had to come back online. The command module was cold, damp and dark when the crew climbed back in. Before they jettisoned the service module, the crew photographed the damaged section. The photograph shows a large panel missing from its side, with the wreckage of the oxygen tanks exposed.

Re-entry was the final test. Earth's atmosphere forms a narrow corridor, and a spacecraft that enters too steeply burns up, while one that enters too shallowly skips off the air and drifts into space. The command module had to hit that corridor within a few degrees. Once the parachutes opened, the spacecraft descended toward the South Pacific. On April 17, 1970, Apollo 13 splashed down near the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The crew were thin, dehydrated and exhausted, but they were alive. The three men had covered about 142 hours of flight and had traveled farther from Earth than any humans before them.

The mission had failed to land on the Moon, but it succeeded in its most important goal: bringing the crew home. The accident investigation reshaped the way NASA handled spacecraft design, testing and crew safety. Engineers reviewed the wiring changes, the tank handling and the procedures that had allowed a damaged component to reach the launch pad. Mission controllers and astronauts later described the flight as a lesson in improvisation, teamwork and the value of plain engineering judgment. The phrase "Houston, we've had a problem" stayed in the public memory long after the mission ended, but the story that mattered most was the one the crew and the flight team wrote together in the four days between the explosion and splashdown.
