The Long Way Home

Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, carrying three men toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. The commander was Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights who had already circled the Moon once. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot, making his first trip into space. The command module pilot was Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days before. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons. Swigert stepped in after a hurried round of training.

The first two days were almost dull. The Saturn V performed well, with one engine in the second stage shutting down early, and the crew settled into the long coast toward the Moon. They had named their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius. They sent home a television broadcast that evening, touring the spacecraft and showing off weightlessness. Few networks bothered to carry it. Spaceflight had begun to feel routine to the public.

Then, on the night of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the flight and roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked Swigert to flip a switch that would stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, the cylinder of supplies and engines attached behind the command module. The tanks held super-cold liquid that tended to settle, and stirring it kept the gauges accurate. Swigert threw the switch.

A moment later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Lovell and Haise felt it through the hull. Warning lights lit across the panel. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Houston asked him to repeat it, and Lovell answered, "Houston, we've had a problem." The wording has been misquoted ever since, but the meaning was clear enough.

The cause had been planted years earlier. An oxygen tank had been damaged during ground handling at the factory, and when it was later tested the heaters ran far longer than intended, because switches had been built for a lower voltage than the launch pad supplied. The heat stripped insulation from the wiring inside the tank. When Swigert stirred it, a spark ignited the oxygen. The tank burst, tore away an entire panel of the service module, and crippled the second tank beside it.

For the first minutes, nobody in Houston or on board understood how bad things were. Instruments gave contradictory readings. Then Lovell looked out of a window and saw a gas venting steadily into space. It was oxygen, the crew's air and the fuel for the cells that made their electricity, pouring away. Within hours the two fuel cells that remained would starve, and the command module's batteries were meant to be saved for the final descent to Earth. Landing on the Moon was no longer possible. The only question was whether the three men could be brought home alive.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams, working in shifts around the clock, settled on a bold idea. The lunar module, a fragile craft built to carry two men down to the surface and back for two days, could serve as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, water and power. The crew shut down Odyssey to preserve what little it had left and crowded into the cramped lander, which now had to support three people for four days.

The next problem was the route. The spacecraft was on a path that would loop around the Moon and return toward Earth, but not by a safe margin. Controllers considered turning around at once, which would have meant using the service module engine that nobody trusted anymore. They chose instead to swing around the Moon and use the lander's descent engine to speed the return. After they passed the far side, where Lovell, Haise and Swigert looked down on craters that Lovell later said they had little time to admire, Aquarius fired its engine for about four minutes. That burn shortened the trip and aimed it at the Pacific Ocean.

Life aboard was miserable. To save power, the crew switched off nearly everything, including the heaters. The cabin temperature fell to around forty degrees Fahrenheit, and condensation ran down the walls. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day for each man, and Haise developed a fever and a painful infection that went untreated. Sleep was hard to come by in the cold. They wore their heavy flight suits and then, eventually, their lunar boots.

A more urgent danger crept up quietly. Breathing releases carbon dioxide, and the lander's filters, designed for two men over a short stay, were saturating. The command module held spare filters, but they were square and the lander's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers dumped onto a table the same odds and ends the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard, hoses and duct tape, and worked out a way to join a square canister to a round fitting. They read the instructions up to the crew step by step, and the men built the device. Carbon dioxide levels fell. It remains one of the best known improvisations in the history of spaceflight.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, still on the ground and now working in a simulator, helped write a procedure for powering Odyssey back up before reentry using as little electricity as possible. Nobody had ever restarted a command module in flight under such limits. The sequence had to be completed in a specific order, and the instructions were radioed to the crew.

As Earth grew larger, the crew prepared for the final stages. Shortly before reentry, they discarded the damaged service module. Looking at it, Lovell reported that one whole side of it was missing, and the crew photographed the wreckage as it drifted away. They had not previously realised the extent of the damage. Then they moved back into Odyssey and discarded Aquarius, which had saved them and now burned up in the atmosphere.

Reentry carried an unusual tension. Every Apollo return caused a radio blackout of a few minutes as superheated air surrounded the capsule. This time the silence stretched past the expected time. Controllers watched the clock and said little. Then, after about a minute and a half more than planned, Swigert's voice came back, and the control room erupted. Parachutes opened, and the capsule settled into the South Pacific near Samoa on April 17, a few miles from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima.

The men emerged tired, thin and unshaven, and several had lost weight. Haise was seriously ill from his infection and recovered over the following days. President Nixon later awarded the crew and the ground team the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and Lovell spoke of the mission as a successful failure, because it had not reached the Moon but had shown what well-trained people could do when everything went wrong.

The investigation that followed redesigned the oxygen tanks, removed the flawed switches and changed how the heaters and wiring were tested. Apollo flights resumed the next year, and Apollo 14 landed at the very site that Apollo 13 had been aiming for. Yet the memory that endured was not of a landing at all. It was the sight of three men in a freezing, crowded little ship, rationing water and counting hours, and the thousands of people on the ground who refused to give them up.
