A lithium ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions from the positive electrode, usually a metal oxide, into the graphite negative electrode. Charging forces this transfer against the cell's natural tendency. The charger supplies electrons through the external circuit while ions travel through the electrolyte and insert themselves between graphite layers. The cell voltage rises as the graphite fills, because the electrode potentials shift with state of charge.

Standard practice uses two stages known as constant current then constant voltage. In the first stage the charger holds current fixed, typically around 0.5 to 1 times the cell capacity per hour. The terminal voltage is left free to climb. This stage delivers roughly 70 to 80 percent of the capacity. When terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, about 4.2 volts for common chemistries, the charger changes its role. It now holds voltage fixed and lets current fall on its own.

The reason current must fall comes from the gap between terminal voltage and the true open circuit voltage. Terminal voltage equals the open circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance, along with additional polarization terms. Internal resistance has several parts. Ohmic resistance comes from the electrolyte, current collectors and contacts. Charge transfer resistance comes from the reaction at the electrode surfaces. Diffusion resistance arises because ions move slowly inside the solid particles, so concentration gradients build up.

Near the end of charge, the open circuit voltage approaches the 4.2 volt ceiling. With terminal voltage clamped there, the margin available to drive current shrinks. Current is roughly that margin divided by resistance, so it decays, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor. In truth the decay is slower than a single exponential, because diffusion in the particles behaves like many time constants stacked together.

Holding voltage fixed is a safety matter and not a convenience. Pushing the terminal voltage above the limit would drive the negative electrode potential toward that of metallic lithium. Lithium would then plate on the surface rather than insert, which forms dendrites, consumes cyclable lithium and raises the risk of internal short circuits. Higher voltage also accelerates electrolyte oxidation at the positive electrode and stresses its crystal structure.

The charger ends the process when current falls to a cutoff, commonly 3 to 5 percent of the rated current. The last 20 percent of capacity therefore takes about as long as the first 80 percent, which is why fast charging claims quote only a partial state of charge. Cold temperatures worsen the effect since resistance and diffusion limits both rise, so chargers reduce current or warm the cell first.
