Imagine two people, whom cryptographers traditionally call Alice and Bob, who want to exchange private messages but have never met and cannot arrange a secret code in advance, while everything they say to each other travels down a line on which a third person, Eve, is listening to every word. For most of history this problem was considered unsolvable, since a code seemed to require that both sides already hold the same secret, which in practice meant a courier carrying a codebook before any conversation could begin, and it came as a real surprise when Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, building on ideas from Ralph Merkle, showed in 1976 that two strangers can in fact end up sharing a secret that nobody listening in can work out. The trick depends on a kind of arithmetic in which a calculation is quick to perform in one direction and, as far as anyone has been able to discover, hopelessly slow to run backwards.

A way to feel the idea without any numbers is to think about paint. Suppose Alice and Bob publicly agree on a shared colour, say yellow, and each of them then privately chooses a secret colour that they never reveal, Alice picking red and Bob picking blue. Alice mixes her red into a pot of the yellow and Bob mixes his blue into another pot of the yellow, and they swap the pots in full view of Eve, who can see the two mixed colours and the original yellow. Alice then adds her own secret red to the pot that came from Bob, and Bob adds his secret blue to the pot that came from Alice, so that both of them end up with exactly the same final blend of yellow, red and blue, whereas Eve, who holds only the yellow and two partial mixtures, has no pot she can add anything to that would reproduce it without knowing at least one of the secret colours. Paint is an imperfect picture, because a sharp-eyed person could estimate which colours went into a mixture, so the real method swaps paint for arithmetic in which un-mixing really is impractical.

The arithmetic needed is nothing more than powers and remainders. You already know that 5 to the power 6 means six fives multiplied together, which comes to 15,625, and you know from long division that 15,625 divided by 23 goes 679 times with 8 left over, so we can say that the remainder of 5 to the power 6 on division by 23 is 8. It helps to picture a clock face with 23 hours on it, where counting past the top simply takes you round to the start again, and the remainder is the hour the hand lands on after all the counting is done. A useful feature of this clock arithmetic is that you may take remainders at any stage of a calculation and the final answer comes out the same, which is why even enormous powers can be handled without ever writing the enormous number down.

To see the whole exchange with small numbers, suppose Alice and Bob publicly agree on the numbers 23 and 5, which Eve is welcome to know. Alice privately picks 6 and sends Bob the remainder of 5 to the power 6 on division by 23, which we have just seen is 8. Bob privately picks 15 and sends Alice the remainder of 5 to the power 15 on division by 23, which works out to 19, and which he can find by repeatedly squaring and reducing, since 5 squared leaves 2 on the 23-hour clock, then 2 squared gives 4 for the fourth power, 4 squared gives 16 for the eighth power, and multiplying the pieces 16, 4, 2 and 5 together gives 640, whose remainder on division by 23 is 19. The numbers 8 and 19 cross the line in the open, but the 6 and the 15 never leave the heads of their owners. Alice now takes the 19 she received and raises it to her own secret power of 6, reducing as she goes, and gets 2, while Bob takes the 8 he received and raises it to his secret power of 15, and he too gets 2, so both now hold the number 2 as their shared secret without ever having transmitted it.

The reason they agree is that Alice effectively computed 5 raised to the power of 15 and then to the power of 6, while Bob computed 5 raised to the power of 6 and then to the power of 15, and both come to 5 raised to the power of 6 times 15, which is 90, because multiplying the exponents in either order gives the same product; the remainders carry through unharmed because of the clock-arithmetic property just described. In effect the final blend of the paint story is the number 5 to the power 90 on the 23-hour clock, and it can be assembled from either person's private number together with the other person's public one, but not from the public numbers alone.

What Eve knows is the clock size 23, the base 5, and the two messages 8 and 19, and to recover the secret she would need to discover one of the private exponents, which means finding a power to which 5 must be raised to land on 8. With a clock as small as 23 hours she could simply try the exponents one after another until something fit, and she would succeed within a minute or two, so nothing about this toy example is safe. Real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits in their size, typically around 600 digits or more, and the private exponents are similarly vast, so that trying them one by one would take longer than the age of the universe even on every computer ever built. Meanwhile the honest parties are not slowed down, because repeated squaring, the method used for 5 to the power 15 above, needs only a few hundred steps even for exponents of that size, and so the asymmetry that the whole scheme relies on is that raising to a power and reducing is fast while finding the power from the result, a problem known as the discrete logarithm, has no known fast method for the clocks and bases that are chosen. It should be said honestly that nobody has proved such a method cannot exist, and our confidence rests on decades of determined attempts by mathematicians that have failed, although quantum computers of sufficient size would change this picture for the exact schemes described here, which is why newer designs are being introduced.

The name public-key cryptography comes from the way each person ends up with a pair of numbers, a private one kept hidden and a public one that can be shouted across the room, linked by a calculation that cannot be reversed in practice. In our example Alice's pair is 6 and 8, and Bob's is 15 and 19. Other schemes use the same two-part idea differently, the best known being RSA, which rests on the fact that multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy whereas splitting the product back into its two prime factors is not, and which lets anyone encrypt a message using a published number that only the holder of the matching private factors can decrypt. In everyday use, such as when your browser connects to a bank, a method of this kind is used only briefly at the start to agree on a shared secret number, and that number then becomes the key for an ordinary and much faster cipher that scrambles the actual traffic, since both sides now hold the same key and can use it to lock and unlock messages quickly.

A serious weakness remains, which is that the exchange protects against someone who merely listens but not against someone who can actively interfere with the line, because an impostor could stand in the middle, run one exchange with Alice pretending to be Bob and another with Bob pretending to be Alice, and then decrypt and re-encrypt everything that passes between them. To defeat this the parties need some way of confirming who is on the other end, and that is done with digital signatures, a further use of public and private key pairs in which a private key is used to sign a message and anyone holding the matching public key can check the signature, combined with certificates in which trusted organisations vouch that a particular public key belongs to a particular website or person. So the secrecy comes from arithmetic that cannot be undone in practice, while the assurance about who you are really talking to comes from a chain of trust that people have to establish and maintain, and both parts are needed for two strangers to talk privately across a line that anyone can hear.
