In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell upon Soho, a crowded district of London. Cholera, a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhea and can kill a healthy person within a day or two, had visited the city before. But this outbreak was fierce even by the standards of the time. In the first days of September, hundreds of people living within a few streets of one another began to sicken and die. Within about ten days, more than five hundred had perished.

Nobody then understood what caused cholera. The dominant belief among doctors and officials was the miasma theory, the idea that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting waste, filthy ground and the stench of poor neighborhoods. It was a reasonable guess for the era, since the worst outbreaks did tend to strike the dirtiest places. Germs had not yet been shown to cause disease, and the microscope had not settled the matter.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked nearby, was not convinced. Snow was a respected figure in his own right. He had pioneered the use of anesthesia, and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He had also been thinking about cholera for years. Its symptoms struck the gut, not the lungs, which seemed odd if the poison were something people breathed. Snow suspected instead that the disease was swallowed, carried in something people drank or ate, and passed from the sick to the healthy through contaminated water.

The Soho outbreak gave him a chance to test the idea. Snow went door to door and spoke to families, asking where the dead had lived and where they had gotten their drinking water. At that time, most Londoners did not have taps at home. They fetched water from shared street pumps, and the water came from wells or from the River Thames, which was heavily polluted with sewage.

As he gathered his findings, Snow drew a map. On a street plan of the neighborhood he marked each death with a small black bar, stacked at the address where the victim had lived. The pattern leapt off the page. The bars clustered thickly around one spot, the water pump on Broad Street. The farther one moved from that pump, the fewer deaths there were.

Snow did not stop at the pattern, because he knew a cluster alone proves little. He looked closely at the exceptions, and the exceptions were what made his case. A workhouse nearby housed hundreds of poor people, yet very few of them fell ill. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well inside the building, so its residents had no need to use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped almost entirely. Its workers were given malt liquor to drink, and the brewery had its own well as well.

Then came the opposite kind of evidence. Snow heard of a woman who lived far from Broad Street, in Hampstead, and who had died of cholera. Her family told him that she had always liked the taste of the water from the Broad Street pump. She had a servant bring bottles of it to her each day. A niece who drank the same water also died. In an area with no other cases, the one link was the pump.

Snow also checked the few nearby deaths that seemed not to fit his theory. Some of them turned out to involve children who attended a school near the pump and had drunk from it on the way. The more he investigated, the more the pump explained.

Snow took his evidence to the local Board of Guardians, the officials who governed the parish. On the evening of September 7, he urged them to act. The men were not certain he was right, but they agreed to take a modest step. The next day, the handle of the Broad Street pump was removed, so that no one could draw water from it.

The story is often told as if the removal ended the epidemic at once, but the truth is a little more complicated. The outbreak had already been fading by the time the handle came off, probably because many residents had fled the area and others had stopped drinking from the pump out of fear. Even so, Snow's action made sense, and his reasoning held up.

The clinching discovery came afterward. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, had at first doubted Snow's theory. Whitehead lived in the parish and knew its people well, and he set out to find the true cause. He learned that the first case in the outbreak was an infant girl who had fallen ill just before the surge began. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled diapers and thrown the dirty water into a cesspool near the pump. Inspectors later found that the old cesspool had been leaking into the well that fed the pump. Sewage laden with the cholera illness had seeped into the water that hundreds of people drank.

Official opinion was slow to change. A committee that studied the outbreak declined to accept Snow's conclusion, and miasma remained the favored explanation for years. Snow died in 1858, before his view had won the day. Only later, as scientists identified the bacterium that causes cholera and learned how germs spread, did his work earn its place in history.

Today, John Snow is remembered as one of the founders of epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations. His method was simple and powerful. He counted the sick, mapped where they lived, asked questions, and looked hardest at the cases that did not fit. In doing so, he showed that careful observation can reveal the cause of a disease even before anyone can see what causes it.
