Joined-Up Writing Still Belongs in the Primary Classroom

Every few years someone proposes dropping joined-up handwriting from the primary curriculum. The argument usually runs the same way. Children will type for the rest of their lives, so the hours spent on cursive loops would be better spent on keyboard skills, coding or digital literacy. Handwriting, on this view, is a relic: a skill that once mattered for clerks and secretaries and now matters to almost nobody.

I think this argument is wrong, and that primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The reason is not sentiment about pen and paper. It is that handwriting is not really a separate subject. It is a foundation for reading, spelling, composition and the working memory that all of them depend on. Taking it away would not free up time for something more valuable. It would remove a tool that most children still need in order to think clearly on paper.

Start with the most practical point. Children spend a great deal of their school lives writing by hand, and they will keep doing so for years after they learn to type. Exams in many systems are still sat on paper. Notes in lectures, labels on laboratory equipment, forms, cards, lists and quick messages are all handwritten in ordinary adult life. A child who has never been taught to join letters will not simply type instead. They will write slowly, with separate capitals and laboured pen lifts, and every word will cost them effort that a fluent writer does not spend. A school that stops teaching joined writing is not preparing children for a keyboard-only future. It is leaving them to work out a physical skill alone, usually without feedback, and many will do it badly.

Speed and fluency matter more than people assume. Handwriting is a motor skill, and like other motor skills it becomes more accurate and more automatic with practice. When letter formation is automatic, the child's attention can move up to the content of the sentence. When it is not, attention is pulled down to the shape of each letter. Researchers who study writing have described this as a competition for limited working memory: if the hand is slow or uncertain, there is less room left for planning ideas, choosing words and checking meaning. Several studies have found that children who write by hand in longer, more fluent bursts produce more words and more developed ideas than those who struggle with the physical act. The effect is not huge in every study, and replication is patchy, but the direction of the finding is consistent with what teachers see every day. Children who cannot write quickly tend to write less.

Joined-up writing also supports spelling, which is the part of literacy that many people least expect. Joining letters encourages children to think about the whole word as a unit, moving from one letter to the next in a continuous stroke. Teachers of the British tradition have long observed that children who write fluently are less likely to reverse letters, to mix up b and d, or to write letters in the wrong order. Some of this is about motor patterns that repeat across a word, and some is about the visual memory that comes from seeing words built in a consistent way. There is good reason to think that the physical habit of writing a word, and not just selecting its letters from a screen, helps fix its spelling in memory. A child who types a word can use autocorrect to produce it correctly without ever having to know it. Writing by hand leaves less room for that kind of hiding.

Then there is reading. Children learn to recognise letters partly through the way they produce them. The act of forming a letter reinforces its shape and its sound, and the connection between a grapheme and its motor pattern gives children an extra route into the alphabet. Early readers who learn letters only by pressing keys or tapping screens may recognise them, but the research on early literacy suggests that the handwriting route gives many children a firmer grip on letter identity. This is particularly important for children with dyslexia or other difficulties with visual memory, for whom multisensory teaching is often recommended.

The objection that matters most is the one about time. The primary curriculum is crowded, and every hour spent on handwriting is an hour not spent on something else. This is a serious point, and I do not think it can be waved away. But it is an argument for teaching handwriting well and briefly, not for abandoning it. A daily ten-minute practice on letter formation and joining, taught with clear models and sensible progressions, is a very small price for a skill that feeds into everything else a child writes. The alternative is not more time for keyboards. Keyboard skills are themselves taught in primary schools, and they are better learned later, once children have the coordination and literacy to type with purpose. Typing speed will come quickly to a child who already knows how words are built. It will not compensate for a child who has never learned to write fluently at all.

There is also a question of equity. Children from homes with plenty of books, pens and adult attention often arrive at school already writing well. Children without those advantages depend on school to teach them. If handwriting is removed from the curriculum, the children who lose most are likely to be those who cannot pick it up at home. Their writing will remain laboured, their spelling uncertain, and their confidence low, and the school will have taken away the one place where they were reliably taught it. A curriculum that treats handwriting as optional quietly assumes that children will learn it elsewhere, and that assumption is least safe for the children who most need the teaching.

Critics sometimes argue that the mechanics of joined writing are arbitrary. Why should a child spend effort on a loop that no professional uses? I think this misunderstands what joining is for. The point of joined-up writing is not aesthetic continuity for its own sake. It is the rhythm of an uninterrupted movement, which keeps the pen moving and the thought moving with it. Print letters lift and restart, and each restart is a small interruption. Joined writing reduces those interruptions. That is why many adults who learned joined writing as children still use it in notes and quick work, even when they are not required to. The skill persists because it is useful.

Nothing here means that handwriting should be taught rigidly, or that every child must produce the same script. Teachers should allow for left-handed children, for children with motor difficulties, and for the fact that a legible, reasonably fast hand matters more than a perfect copybook style. But these are arguments for good teaching, not for removal. The right answer to a poorly taught subject is to teach it better.

Primary schools exist to lay foundations that children cannot easily lay for themselves. Joined-up handwriting is one of those foundations. It supports fluency, spelling, reading and the working memory that thinking on paper requires, and it gives children a practical skill for the many occasions when a keyboard is not to hand. The keyboard will remain an essential tool, and children should learn to use it. But it is not a replacement for the pen in a young writer's hand, and schools that treat it as one are making a mistake that children will pay for long after they leave the classroom.
