A lithium ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions between two host materials. During charging, ions leave the cathode, travel through a liquid electrolyte, and insert into the layered graphite of the anode, while electrons take the external circuit. The charger's job is to drive this transfer without damaging either electrode.

Most cells use a two stage protocol called constant current then constant voltage. In the first stage the charger forces a fixed current, commonly one times the cell capacity per hour. The cell's terminal voltage rises slowly from about 3.0 volts as the electrodes fill. This stage delivers roughly 70 to 80 percent of the charge. When terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, typically 4.2 volts for a standard cobalt based cell, the charger switches to holding that voltage fixed. The current then decays on its own, and charging ends when it falls to a small fraction of the original, often five percent.

The slowdown is not a convenience chosen by designers. It follows from the physics. The terminal voltage equals the open circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance. Open circuit voltage climbs as the anode fills with lithium, so the cell reaches the 4.2 volt ceiling before it is truly full. Because the charger must not exceed that ceiling, the voltage available across the internal resistance shrinks, and by Ohm's law the current must shrink with it. Near full, the open circuit voltage sits within a few millivolts of the limit, so very little current is allowed.

Transport limits make this worse. Lithium must diffuse into graphite particles, and diffusion is slow compared with the rate at which ions arrive at the particle surface. As the outer shell of each particle saturates, the concentration gradient that drives inward diffusion flattens, so the interior fills at a decreasing rate. A sponge is a fair analogy. It takes up water quickly at first, then more slowly once its surface layers are already wet.

There is also a safety reason. If the anode surface is full while current is still forced, the anode potential can fall below that of lithium metal. Ions then plate out as metallic lithium instead of inserting into the graphite. This reduces capacity, grows needle shaped dendrites, and can cause internal short circuits. High voltage also accelerates oxidation of the electrolyte at the cathode, which shortens life. Tapering the current keeps the anode potential positive and the cathode stress low.

The result is a charge curve with two distinct regimes. The first is fast and limited by what the cell can safely accept as current. The second is slow and limited by voltage, resistance, and diffusion. Fast chargers shorten the first regime by managing heat and using cells with low resistance, but the final taper remains. This is why a battery reaches 80 percent in about half the time it takes to go from 80 to 100.
