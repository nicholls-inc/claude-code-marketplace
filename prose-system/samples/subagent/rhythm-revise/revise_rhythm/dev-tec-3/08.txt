A lithium-ion cell is charged with a two-stage protocol called constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV). In the first stage, the charger forces a fixed current into the cell, commonly expressed as a C-rate, where 1C is the current that would fill the nominal capacity in one hour. While this current flows, lithium ions leave the cathode (for example a nickel-manganese-cobalt oxide), travel through the electrolyte, and insert, or intercalate, between the graphene layers of the graphite anode, while the electrons take the external circuit. The cell's terminal voltage rises steadily during this stage, from roughly 3.0 V when empty toward 4.2 V when full for a typical cell, and most of the charge, often 70 to 80 percent, is delivered here.

The voltage rise happens for two separate reasons. The first is thermodynamic: the cell's open-circuit voltage is the difference between the cathode and anode equilibrium potentials, and each depends on how full of lithium the electrode is, so as the anode fills and the cathode empties, that difference grows. The second is the voltage drop caused by internal resistance: the terminal voltage under charge equals the open-circuit voltage plus current times resistance, plus additional overpotentials from sluggish charge-transfer reactions at the particle surfaces and from concentration gradients in the electrolyte and within the solid particles. Lithium diffuses slowly in solids, so during fast charging the particle surfaces fill well ahead of their interiors.

Once the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, the charger switches to the second stage and holds the voltage constant. That limit is a hard safety boundary: above it, the electrolyte oxidises, the cathode degrades, and lithium metal can plate on the anode, risking short circuits and fire. With voltage pinned, the current is no longer set by the charger but by the cell. As the open-circuit voltage continues to creep up toward the limit, the gap between the applied voltage and the open-circuit voltage shrinks, so the current needed to sustain that gap across the internal resistance falls, roughly in proportion to the gap, producing an approximately exponential decay, like a capacitor charging through a resistor.

This is why charging slows near full: the charger cannot keep pushing the same current without exceeding the voltage limit, because the resistive and kinetic voltage drops would then push the surface potential past the safe value even though the bulk of the electrode is not yet full. Holding the voltage lets the concentration gradients inside the particles relax, so lithium continues to diffuse inward and fill the remaining sites, but at a rate that diminishes as the driving force vanishes. The stage ends when the current has tapered to a cutoff, typically about 0.05C to 0.1C, at which point the cell is declared full. Chasing the last few percent takes disproportionately long, which is why fast chargers usually stop tapering early or accept a slightly lower state of charge.
