On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unseasonably warm. After days of bitter cold, the temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, and people were out in the thin winter sun. Around half past noon, workers, children and horses were going about their business near the harbor, beside a huge steel tank that loomed over Commercial Street.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It would be turned into industrial alcohol, which was used in munitions and, soon, because Prohibition was weeks away from taking effect, in a great deal of liquor.

The tank had always been a poor neighbor. It leaked so badly that residents collected the brown syrup in cans. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown, so the stains would not show. Nobody seems to have run a proper stress test. A fresh shipment had been pumped in the day before, and the warming air may have made the contents expand.

At 12:30, witnesses heard a deep rumble, then a sound like a machine gun as the rivets began to shoot out. The tank burst.

A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, rushed down the street at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It was not a trickle. It was a wall. It knocked buildings off their foundations, and it tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports, leaving a train teetering on the edge. Wagons, horses and people were swept up together.

Then came the cold. As the molasses chilled in the winter air, it thickened, and the people caught in it were stuck as if in setting glue. Rescuers waded in, but the syrup clung to boots and clothes and slowed every step. Soldiers, sailors and police officers rushed to help, and the Red Cross set up a station. Searchers cut through the goo for days. Twenty-one people died, among them children, and about 150 were injured. Horses that could not be freed had to be put down.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats and covered the ground with sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown until summer. For decades afterward, locals insisted that on hot days the North End still smelled faintly sweet.

The company blamed anarchists, claiming that someone had planted a bomb. The residents did not accept this and sued. After a long trial, a court-appointed auditor ruled in 1925 that the tank had been structurally unsound, and the company paid roughly $600,000 in settlements. The ruling helped push cities toward modern building codes and the requirement that engineers sign off on large structures.

A disaster that sounds almost comic left a serious legacy.
