A lithium-ion cell is charged with a two-stage protocol called constant current followed by constant voltage, usually abbreviated CC-CV, and the slowing near full is a direct consequence of how the second stage works. In the first stage the charger acts as a current source, forcing a fixed current, commonly expressed as a multiple of the cell's capacity (1C would fill the nominal capacity in one hour), into the cell while the terminal voltage is free to rise. Lithium ions deintercalate from the cathode, travel through the electrolyte and insert into the graphite anode, and the cell's open-circuit voltage climbs as the anode fills, from roughly 3.0 V when empty to about 4.2 V when full for a typical cobalt-based chemistry. The terminal voltage you measure is not that open-circuit voltage, though, because it also includes the drop across the cell's internal resistance, which lumps together ohmic losses in the electrodes and electrolyte, charge-transfer resistance at the particle surfaces, and the slower diffusion of lithium within the solid particles; the terminal voltage during charging is therefore the open-circuit voltage plus the current times that resistance, plus any concentration-driven overpotential. Because the upper voltage limit exists to protect the cell, since pushing the anode too negative against the lithium reference risks plating metallic lithium and pushing the cathode too positive accelerates electrolyte oxidation and structural damage, the charger must stop raising the voltage once it hits about 4.2 V. At that moment the cell is not actually full, since the terminal voltage is inflated by the current-resistance term, and the open-circuit voltage is still lower than 4.2 V. The charger then switches to the second stage and holds the terminal voltage constant. With the terminal voltage pinned, any further rise in the open-circuit voltage as the cell fills must be matched by a fall in the resistive drop, so the current has to decay, roughly exponentially, and the cell approaches its true full state asymptotically rather than reaching it at a definite moment. Lithium concentration gradients inside the particles also relax during this stage, so that the surface and the core equalize, which is why the last portion of capacity cannot be pushed in quickly even in principle. Charging is conventionally terminated when the current has decayed to a small cutoff, often around 0.05C to 0.1C, because chasing the final fraction of a percent costs a long time for very little stored energy. In an engineering analogy, the cell behaves like a capacitor with a series resistance and a voltage-dependent state, charged from a source with a hard voltage ceiling, and the tail of the charge is the familiar RC-style settling toward that ceiling. The practical consequence is that the first 80 percent of capacity can often be delivered in perhaps half the total time, while the remainder takes the other half.
