Scrap the Parking Mandates

Every city has a building that explains what is wrong with its planning code. It might be a corner shop that sat empty for a decade because reopening it as a restaurant would have triggered a requirement for twelve parking spaces on a lot that fits three. It might be an apartment block that was never built, because the underground garage the rules demanded cost more than the developer could recover in rent. These buildings, the ones that exist and the ones that don't, are the product of minimum parking requirements, and cities should abolish them.

The rules sound reasonable. A new building brings new people, new people bring cars, and cars need somewhere to go. So the code specifies: one and a half spaces per apartment, one space per 300 square feet of retail, one per four seats in a church. The trouble starts when you ask where those numbers come from. Mostly they were copied from other cities, which copied them from surveys of suburban sites with free parking and no transit, measured at peak demand. The economist Donald Shoup, who spent a career studying the subject, called this pseudoscience, and he was being fair. A ratio derived from a Florida office park in 1985 has no business governing a bakery near a train station.

The result is a lot of parking that nobody uses. Studies of American cities routinely find several parking spaces for every car, and surveys of apartment garages find a third or more of stalls sitting empty overnight, the hour when residential demand peaks. That is expensive emptiness. A space in a surface lot costs thousands of dollars to build; a space in a structured garage costs tens of thousands, and underground it can run well beyond that. None of this is paid by drivers at the point of use. It is folded into rents, into the price of groceries, into the cost of a haircut. The tenant who doesn't own a car pays for a stall anyway. Since car ownership rises with income, the mandate works as a transfer from poorer households to richer ones, written into law.

Housing is where the damage is greatest. When each apartment must come with a space or two, the parking often determines what can be built at all. Small lots become undevelopable. The modest three-storey walk-ups and flats above shops that made older neighbourhoods affordable and pleasant are, in many cities, now illegal to replicate, not because anyone objects to the buildings but because there is no room for the cars. A city that requires homes for cars and merely permits homes for people should not be surprised to find itself short of the latter.

Then there is what mandated parking does to the place itself. Spread buildings apart with asphalt and you lengthen every trip, which makes walking impractical, which makes driving necessary, which justifies more parking. The requirement manufactures the demand it claims to accommodate. It also paves over land that could produce tax revenue, soaks up heat in summer, and sheds stormwater into drains that were not sized for it.

The best objection is spillover. If a new building provides too little parking, residents will park on neighbouring streets, and existing residents will bear the cost. This is a real concern, but it is a problem with how cities manage their kerbs, not a reason to regulate private land. Street parking is a valuable public asset that most cities give away. Price it, or issue permits, and spillover stops being free. Charging for the kerb is politically harder than hiding the cost in a zoning table, but it puts the cost on the person making the choice.

A second objection is that developers will build no parking at all. They won't. Abolishing a minimum is not imposing a maximum. Developers still build parking where buyers and tenants will pay for it, because a building that cannot be leased is a bad investment, and lenders know it. What changes is who decides the number: someone with money at stake and knowledge of the site, rather than a table drafted decades ago.

We also have evidence, not just theory. Buffalo dropped its minimums citywide in 2017, and subsequent research found that mixed-use projects built substantially fewer spaces than the old code would have required, while projects that needed parking still provided it. Minneapolis eliminated its requirements and, alongside other reforms, saw a surge in apartment construction and rents that rose more slowly than in comparable cities. Parking reform was not the only cause, but it removed an obstacle. Dozens of other cities, from Austin to Edmonton, have followed, and none has reversed course in a panic.

Removing parking minimums costs a city nothing. It needs no new agency and no subsidy. It simply stops forcing people to build something they may not need, at a price everyone else pays. Few reforms offer so much for so little. Cities should take it.
