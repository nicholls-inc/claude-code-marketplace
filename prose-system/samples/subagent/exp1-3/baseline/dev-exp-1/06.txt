**How a heat pump heats a house when it is colder outside**

The puzzle is real: heat flows from warm to cold, so how can a heat pump warm your home from a freezing garden? The answer is that a heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists, and it uses a little electricity to push that heat uphill.

**Cold is not the same as no heat**

"Cold" is relative. Air at -5C still holds a large amount of thermal energy. It is only "cold" compared with the 20C you want indoors. Absolute zero is around -273C, so outdoor air at -5C has nearly 270 degrees of heat left to give. A heat pump extracts some of it.

**The refrigerant cycle**

A heat pump is essentially a refrigerator running in reverse. A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures (well below -20C). The cycle has four steps:

1. **Evaporator (outdoor unit).** Cold liquid refrigerant passes through a coil as a fan blows outdoor air across it. Because the refrigerant is colder than the air, even on a frosty day, it absorbs heat and boils into a gas.
2. **Compressor.** This is the key step. The compressor squeezes the gas, which sharply raises its pressure and temperature, typically to 50-60C or more. This is where the electricity goes. Compressing a gas heats it, the same way a bicycle pump warms up.
3. **Condenser (indoors).** The hot gas flows through a coil connected to your radiators, underfloor heating or hot water cylinder. Water or air at a lower temperature takes the heat, and the refrigerant condenses back to a liquid.
4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid's pressure drops suddenly, which cools it well below the outdoor temperature, and the loop starts again.

Heat moves "uphill" because the compressor changes the refrigerant's temperature at each stage. It is colder than outside at the evaporator and hotter than your radiators at the condenser. Heat always flows from hotter to colder, but the refrigerant is deliberately made hotter or colder than its surroundings at the right moments.

**Why it beats a boiler on efficiency**

A gas boiler turns about 85-95% of the fuel's energy into heat, so it can never deliver more heat than the gas it burns. A heat pump delivers more heat than the electricity it uses, because most of the output was collected from outside. This is measured as the coefficient of performance (COP), or seasonal COP (SCOP) over a year. A typical UK-style installation achieves a seasonal figure of around 3 to 4, meaning each 1 kWh of electricity yields 3 to 4 kWh of heat. That is roughly 300-400% efficiency.

**The catch: colder weather and hotter water cost efficiency**

The bigger the temperature gap the compressor must bridge, the harder it works and the lower the COP. On a very cold day a heat pump might drop to a COP of about 2 or 2.5. Modern units still run down to -15C or lower, and many are rated for -20C, but efficiency falls as it gets colder. Likewise, a heat pump is most efficient when it supplies water at 35-45C rather than the 70C or so that old boiler systems often use. That is why installers focus on your house's insulation, radiator sizing and flow temperatures.

**What this means for your decision**

- **Heat loss matters most.** A well-insulated home needs less heat, so the pump can run gently and efficiently. A heat-loss survey should come before any quote.
- **Radiators may need upgrading.** Larger radiators or underfloor heating let the system run at lower temperatures.
- **Running cost depends on prices.** Heat pumps win when electricity is not much more than about three to four times the price of gas per kWh. Check your own tariffs, and consider time-of-use tariffs.
- **Upfront cost is higher.** Installation often costs several thousand pounds more than a boiler, though grants may be available depending on where you live.
- **Space and noise.** You need room outside for the unit and, usually, a hot water cylinder indoors.

Done well, a heat pump heats reliably through winter and uses a third or less of the electricity-equivalent energy a resistance heater would. Ask installers for a design that states the expected seasonal efficiency and the design flow temperature, and compare those figures between quotes.
