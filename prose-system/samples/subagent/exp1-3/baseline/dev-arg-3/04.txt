**Keep Teaching Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years a familiar proposal resurfaces: primary schools should stop teaching cursive. Children type everything eventually, the argument runs, so the hours spent looping an "e" into an "l" would be better spent on coding, or on almost anything else. Finland made headlines in 2016 by dropping compulsory cursive in favour of keyboard skills, and several American states went the same way before many reversed course. The argument sounds modern and practical. I think it is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, as a core skill taught with care and not an ornamental extra. The reasons are cognitive, practical and civic, and the costs of the alternative are larger than they first appear.

**What we are really asking**

The question is often framed as handwriting versus keyboards, as though a child must choose a technology for life. That is a false choice. Children will learn to type, and should. The real question is narrower: given limited hours in a primary week, is it worth teaching children to join their letters? The case for doing so rests on what joined writing does that other approaches do less well.

**The cognitive case**

The strongest evidence concerns what handwriting does to the learning brain. Studies using brain imaging, including work by Karin James at Indiana University, have found that children who practise forming letters by hand show richer activation in reading-related regions than children who type or trace the same letters. Writing a letter by hand forces the child to produce its shape from scratch, with variation, and that effortful production seems to help the letter become a stable, recognisable object. Typing delivers identical, ready-made characters at the press of a key, with no formative effort at all.

Research on note-taking points the same way. Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer's much-cited study found that students who wrote notes longhand tended to understand and retain concepts better than those who typed, partly because the slowness of handwriting forces selection and summarising. More recent work in Norway, using electroencephalography, found more widespread connectivity in the brain when people wrote by hand than when they typed. These studies are not flawless, and some findings have been hard to replicate in their strongest form. But the direction of the evidence is consistent: the physical, sequential act of handwriting does something useful for learning.

Joining matters within that picture, not just handwriting in general. A joined script turns a word into a single flowing movement. The child learns to hold a whole word in motor memory instead of assembling it as a string of separate stabs. Spelling patterns such as "-ing", "-tion" and "-ough" become movements the hand knows, which is why many teachers of children with spelling difficulties favour cursive: the hand helps carry the memory. Printed letters, by contrast, require the pen to be lifted after every stroke, and the child must decide each time where the next letter starts. Joined writing, once learned, removes a great many small decisions.

**The fluency case**

There is also a plain practical argument. Handwriting is not going away in school. Children write in lessons, in exams, in notebooks and on whiteboards for the whole of their education, and in most countries high-stakes examinations are still handwritten. A child who writes slowly or illegibly is penalised in all of them, and often invisibly: examiners mark what they can read, and research has repeatedly found that neat scripts receive more generous marks than messy ones for identical content.

Fluency is what allows a child to think about what they are saying instead of how to form the letters. When handwriting is automatic, working memory is freed for composition, and studies of young writers consistently link handwriting speed and legibility to the quality and length of their written work. Joined writing is generally faster than print once mastered, since the pen stays on the page and the hand moves in rhythm. A well-taught joined hand is not a decorative luxury but a speed and stamina tool, much as touch typing is. Nobody argues that touch typing should be dropped because people can hunt and peck. We teach it because fluency pays.

**The inclusion case**

Defenders of the status quo are sometimes accused of nostalgia, but one of the best arguments for joined handwriting is about equity. Children with dyslexia and dyspraxia often find that a consistent, joined script helps, because it reduces letter reversals such as "b" and "d" (the joined forms begin differently) and builds a stable motor pattern. Occupational therapists have long used cursive-based methods for this reason. If schools stop teaching it, the children who most need structured help are the ones who lose it, while children whose parents can afford tutors or whose homes are rich in writing practice will pick up a joined hand anyway. Dropping the skill from the curriculum does not abolish it; it merely privatises it.

The same is true of keyboards. Not every child has a laptop or tablet at home, and not every school can guarantee a device for every pupil at every moment. Pencil and paper are the most reliably equal technology a school possesses. A curriculum that depends on them being used well is more resilient than one that assumes a charged device and a stable connection.

**The reading case**

There is a quieter literacy argument too. A great deal of the written record, including old letters, archives, family papers, historical documents and, in the present, the handwritten comments teachers and relatives leave on children's work, is in joined script. A child who has never been taught to join letters may struggle to read these at all. Reading cursive is a modest skill, but it is a gateway to the handwritten past and to the handwritten present: birthday cards, shopping lists, a grandparent's note. And the child who can read it will usually find it easiest to learn by writing it.

**The signature, and the self**

A smaller point, but a real one: most adults sign documents in a joined hand, and legal and banking systems still rely on signatures. Children who have never learned any joined writing will invent something, but a signature developed without instruction is often an inconsistent scrawl. More broadly, handwriting is a mark of individuality. Adolescents often develop distinctive hands, and a personal handwriting style is, for many people, a modest but genuine form of self-expression that typing cannot offer.

**The objections**

The best objection is opportunity cost. The primary day is crowded, and every minute on handwriting is a minute not spent elsewhere. This is fair, but the amount involved is small. Good handwriting instruction takes short, frequent practice, perhaps ten to fifteen minutes a day in the early years, and it is integrated into the writing the children are doing anyway. It is not a large share of the week. The time saved by dropping it would be modest, while the benefits, in fluency, spelling and learning, accrue across the whole curriculum.

A second objection holds that print is sufficient: children can write legibly in print, so why add a second script? Here I would concede that a clear print hand is far better than an illegible cursive one, and that forcing joined writing on a child who cannot yet form letters is counterproductive. Joined writing should be introduced after the child has secure letter formation, usually around ages seven or eight, and taught with patience. Some schools use "pre-cursive" letters with exit strokes from the start, which makes the transition easy. The approach matters more than the label. But once letters are secure, joining is a natural next step, and the evidence on speed and spelling favours taking it.

A third objection is that the evidence is not conclusive, and this is true. Much of the neuroscience is on small samples, and the comparison between joined and printed handwriting specifically is thinner than the comparison between handwriting and typing. I do not want to oversell it. But the burden of proof should lie with change. Handwriting instruction has a long record of use, low cost and plausible benefit; the alternative is a bet that the benefits we can partly measure are not real. When the downside risk falls on the youngest learners, caution is the better policy.

**What good teaching looks like**

If primary schools are going to teach joined writing, they should do it well, which means avoiding the mistakes that gave cursive its bad name. It should not be a punishing exercise in ornamental loops, or a test of neatness used to shame children. Teach a simple, legible, efficient joined hand, with few flourishes. Allow children to develop their own style once the fundamentals are secure. Pair handwriting with typing instruction instead of setting them in competition, so that children leave primary school able to do both well. And give left-handed and struggling writers the extra support they need, since they are the ones most likely to be failed by neglect.

**Conclusion**

The case against teaching joined-up handwriting mistakes a skill for a relic. It is true that few adults write long documents by hand. But primary education is not training for adult tasks alone; it is the construction of the habits of mind and body from which later skills grow. Handwriting is part of that construction. It helps children learn letters, remember spellings, take notes, think while they write, and express themselves in a way that is recognisably their own. It costs little, serves the most vulnerable pupils best, and keeps open the door to a great deal of written human history.

Keyboards deserve their place in the classroom, and every child should learn to use one fluently. But teaching typing is not a reason to abandon the pen. Primary schools should teach both, and they should teach joined-up handwriting with confidence, as a practical and humane skill worth keeping.
