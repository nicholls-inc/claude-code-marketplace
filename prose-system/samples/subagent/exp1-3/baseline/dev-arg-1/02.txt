**Scrap the Parking Mandates**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a vast, invisible hand shaping the landscape: the minimum parking requirement. Written into zoning codes, these rules dictate how many spaces a developer must build for every apartment, restaurant seat, or square foot of retail. A bar may need one space per hundred square feet. An apartment building may need two spaces per unit, whether or not its tenants own cars. Cities should repeal these mandates entirely. Not tweak them, not reduce them by a third, but remove them and let people decide how much parking they actually want.

Start with what the requirements are: a price control in disguise. Parking is expensive to build. A surface space can cost several thousand dollars, and a spot in an underground garage can run past fifty thousand in expensive markets. When a city forces a developer to include those spaces, the cost does not vanish. It gets folded into rents, menu prices, and the cost of everything sold inside. Tenants who own no car subsidize those who do. Renters, who skew younger and poorer, pay for storage of other people's vehicles.

The requirements also mean fewer homes get built at all. Space devoted to parking is space not devoted to housing, and in tight lots the mandate can kill a project outright. A small infill site that could hold eight apartments may only pencil out at five once the required stalls take up the ground floor. At a time when most growing cities are short of housing and watching affordability slip, deliberately constraining supply through parking rules is hard to defend. Researchers who have studied cities that reformed their codes have generally found more housing permitted, and in some cases cheaper units, after the change.

Defenders respond that without mandates, new buildings will flood the neighborhood with cars looking for free curb space. This worry deserves a real answer, but it mistakes the cause of the problem. Spillover happens because street parking is underpriced or free, not because off-street spaces are scarce. Free curb parking is a commons, and the commons gets overgrazed. The fix is to manage the curb: charge market rates where demand is high, issue resident permits where appropriate, and return the revenue to the neighborhood. Buffalo, New York, became the first major American city to eliminate citywide minimums in 2017, and its experience since has been far calmer than critics predicted. Developers still build parking where they believe tenants and customers want it. They just stop building it where they do not.

That last point matters. Repeal does not mean no parking. It means parking decided by people who bear the cost of being wrong. A developer building suburban townhomes will still provide garages, because buyers demand them. A grocery store on a highway will still have a lot. What changes is the downtown corner lot, the building near a train station, the small shop on a walkable street, places where the mandated number is clearly higher than the market would choose. Lenders and tenants are not fools. Where parking has value, it will be built. Where it does not, the land can do something better.

Consider also what the mandates have done to the shape of our cities. Decades of required parking produced seas of asphalt that separate buildings, push destinations farther apart, and make walking unpleasant or impossible. Every added stall spreads things out, which makes driving more necessary, which justifies more parking. It is a loop that the code itself created. Historic main streets, with their buildings pressed against the sidewalk and no lots in sight, would be illegal to build today in most places. We have codified the prevention of the very neighborhoods people pay premiums to live in.

There is a fairness argument too. The standards behind many minimums come from manuals that surveyed suburban sites with ample free parking, often at peak demand, and published the numbers as if they were natural laws. Planners then adopted them with little local evidence. A rule resting on such shaky data should not carry the force of law over millions of building decisions.

Cities worried about transition can phase repeal in gently, starting with downtowns and transit corridors, and pairing it with better curb management. But the destination should be clear. Minimum parking requirements fail on cost, on housing supply, on environmental grounds, and on basic principle: government should not tell a business how many customers will arrive by car.

Places from Minneapolis to Auckland have already taken the step, and the sky has stayed where it was. Cities that remain attached to mandates are not protecting their residents. They are protecting a habit. Let the market and the neighbors decide how many spaces a building needs. In most cases the answer will be fewer than the code demands, and the money saved will go toward homes, shops, and the kind of places people actually want to be.
