On a mild Wednesday, January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusual thaw. Temperatures had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit after days of bitter cold, and around noon many residents were out for lunch or errands. Beside Commercial Street stood a huge steel tank, 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, which would be fermented and turned into industrial alcohol, a trade then in high demand as the nation edged toward Prohibition and as munitions makers needed ethanol.

The tank had never been trusted. Built in 1915 in haste by a company eager to profit from wartime demand, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the seeping syrup in pails. Instead of fixing the seams, the company reportedly painted the tank brown to hide the stains. No one had properly tested it by filling it with water. Its walls were thinner than they should have been, and its rivets and design were poor. Residents also reported that it groaned and shuddered when filled.

That day it held a fresh shipment, and the warmer air had thinned the syrup and likely increased the pressure inside. At about 12:30 in the afternoon, witnesses heard a low rumbling and a sound like a machine gun, perhaps the rivets popping. Then the tank burst.

A wave of molasses, estimated at 15 to 25 feet high at first, rushed out at around 35 miles per hour. It carried a pressure surge ahead of it and the force of thousands of tons of liquid behind it. The wave tore elevated railway supports from their moorings, lifted a train, crushed buildings, and shoved a firehouse off its foundation. Wagons, horses, and people were swept up and flung against walls. Many of those caught were children, workers, and firemen.

The cold of January made the horror worse. As the molasses cooled, it thickened and gripped its victims like glue. Rescuers waded in to their knees, sometimes their waists, struggling to free survivors. Sailors from a nearby ship, police, Red Cross workers, and army and navy personnel joined the effort. Many of the dead were not found for days. In all, 21 people died and about 150 were injured. Horses also perished, and cleanup crews later labored for weeks, using salt water from fireboats and sand to scour the streets. The harbor ran brown into the summer, and locals insisted the area smelled of molasses for decades.

The legal aftermath was a landmark. Residents filed a class action suit against United States Industrial Alcohol, the parent company of Purity Distilling. The company claimed that anarchists had bombed the tank, a plausible-sounding excuse in an era of political violence. But after years of hearings and hundreds of witnesses, an auditor concluded that the tank had simply been built poorly and the company was to blame. In 1925 the firm agreed to pay damages, roughly $600,000 in total, with families of the dead receiving about $7,000 each.

The disaster helped push Massachusetts and other states toward stricter construction rules. Architects and engineers were increasingly required to sign off on plans, and the idea that a company could build whatever it liked and hope for the best began to fade. A sweet, sticky accident became a lesson in how carelessness, when scaled up, can become catastrophe.
