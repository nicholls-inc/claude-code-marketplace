Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, or at least stop treating those estimates as promises. The practice survives because it feels like diligence, yet most engineers have watched it fail often enough to know it is mostly theater. We sit in a room, argue about whether a ticket is a three or a five, and produce a number that nobody will remember by the time the work is finished.

The core problem is that estimates are least reliable exactly where they matter most. Small, familiar tasks can be sized with some confidence, but those are rarely the ones that threaten a deadline. The risky work is the unfamiliar integration, the legacy module with no tests, the requirement that turns out to be two requirements. By definition, we cannot estimate what we have not yet discovered, and software is mostly discovery. Decades of overruns, from tiny startups to government megaprojects, suggest this is not a failure of effort but a property of the work itself.

Worse, estimates do not stay estimates. The moment a number leaves the team, it hardens into a commitment, then a deadline, then a measure of whether the team is doing well. Engineers learn quickly that the safe response is to pad, so the numbers drift upward until they reflect fear rather than evidence. Managers learn to discount whatever they are told, so they push back, and the negotiation begins again. The whole exchange consumes hours of planning meetings and delivers a figure that both sides privately distrust.

The usual defense is that the business needs predictability, and that is a fair concern. But the alternative to estimating is not chaos. Teams can work in small slices, ship them continuously, and watch how much actually gets finished each week. That observed throughput is a far better forecast than any story-point guess, because it reflects what really happens with your codebase, your people, and your interruptions. If you want to know when a project will land, count the finished items, look at the trend, and give a range instead of a date. This costs minutes rather than days of ceremony, and it improves as the data accumulates.

Dropping estimates also forces conversations that estimates let us avoid. When nobody can hide behind a number, the question becomes what the smallest useful version of this feature is and whether it is worth building at all. Scope gets cut earlier, because cutting scope is the only lever that actually moves a delivery date. A team that argues about priorities is spending its time better than a team that argues about points.

There are real exceptions. A fixed-price contract or a coordinated launch with outside partners may require a committed date, and in those cases you should estimate, openly acknowledging the uncertainty and revisiting the figure as you learn. But that is a narrow case, not the default state of every sprint.

For most teams, the honest position is that we do not know how long this will take, and the better response is to find out by building a little, measuring what happened, and adjusting. Managers who can live with a forecast instead of a promise will get more reliable delivery, and engineers who are no longer defending numbers they invented will get back hours of their week. It is time to retire the ritual.
