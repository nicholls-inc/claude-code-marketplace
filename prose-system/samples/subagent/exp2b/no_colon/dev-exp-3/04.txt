# How two strangers can agree on a secret in public

## The problem

Imagine you want to send a private message to someone you have never met. For centuries the only answer was a shared secret, such as a codebook or a password, that both of you already held. You could only get one by meeting in person or by trusting a courier. That is no use if the other person lives on the far side of the world and every message between you passes through hands you do not trust.

In the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around this. Two people can talk openly, with an eavesdropper hearing every word, and still end up holding a secret that the eavesdropper cannot work out. It sounds impossible, and the trick behind it is a lovely piece of school-level arithmetic.

## The paint analogy

Start with colours, because the idea is easiest to see there.

Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow. Everyone can hear this, including a spy named Eve. Alice then secretly picks a private colour, say red. Bob secretly picks his own, say blue.

Each person mixes their private colour into the shared yellow. Alice sends Bob her orange-ish mixture. Bob sends Alice his green-ish mixture. Eve sees both mixtures travel past, but she cannot unmix paint. Taking a mixture apart to find the ingredient colours is far harder than making the mixture was.

Now Alice adds her private red to the mixture she received from Bob. Bob adds his private blue to the mixture he received from Alice. Both of them have now mixed yellow, red and blue, in a different order, and the final colour is identical. That muddy brown is their shared secret.

Eve has the yellow and both half-mixtures, but she lacks a private colour, so she cannot finish either recipe. The whole scheme depends on one fact. Mixing is easy, and unmixing is hard.

To turn this into mathematics, we need an operation that is easy to do forwards and very hard to undo. Clock arithmetic gives us one.

## Clock arithmetic

Think of a clock with 23 hours instead of 12. When you count past 22 you wrap back round to 0. So 23 is 0, 24 is 1, and 30 is 7. Mathematicians call this working "modulo 23", and the answer is just the remainder after dividing by 23. For example, 50 divided by 23 is 2 with 4 left over, so 50 on this clock is 4.

Now take a number and keep multiplying it by itself, wrapping around the clock each time. Here is what happens with the number 5.

- 5 to the power 1 is 5.
- 5 to the power 2 is 25, which wraps to 2.
- 5 to the power 3 is 2 times 5, which is 10.
- 5 to the power 4 is 10 times 5, which is 50, and that wraps to 4.

The results jump around with no obvious pattern. That jumping is what makes the operation useful.

Going forwards is easy. Give me 5 to the power 6 on this clock and I can work it out in a minute. Going backwards is the hard part. If I tell you that 5 to some power lands on 8, and ask which power I used, you have no shortcut. With a tiny clock you could just try every power in turn. With a clock that has hundreds of digits, trying them all would take longer than the age of the universe.

This is the "mixing is easy, unmixing is hard" property we needed.

## The exchange with real numbers

Here is the full conversation, using small numbers so you can check the sums yourself. Real systems use enormous ones.

**Step one, agree on public numbers.** Alice and Bob announce two numbers openly. The first is the size of the clock, 23. The second is the starting number, 5. This is the yellow paint, and Eve hears it.

**Step two, pick private numbers.** Alice secretly chooses 6. Bob secretly chooses 15. These are the red and blue paints, and they are never spoken aloud or sent anywhere.

**Step three, make the public mixtures.** Alice works out 5 to the power 6 on the 23-hour clock. That is 15,625, and dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8. She sends Bob the number 8.

Bob works out 5 to the power 15 on the same clock. The answer comes out as 19, and he sends Alice the number 19.

Eve hears 8 and 19 as they travel. These are the half-mixed paints.

**Step four, finish the recipe.** Alice takes the number she received, 19, and raises it to her private power, 6. That is 19 to the power 6, and on the 23-hour clock it comes to 2.

Bob takes the number he received, 8, and raises it to his private power, 15. That is 8 to the power 15, and on the same clock it also comes to 2.

Both of them now hold the number 2. Neither ever said it aloud. It is their shared secret.

## Why the two answers match

This is the part that feels like a magic trick, so it is worth seeing why it works.

Alice's final number is the starting 5 raised to Bob's power, 15, and then raised again to her own power, 6. Bob's final number is 5 raised to Alice's power, 6, and then raised to his own power, 15. Raising a number to a power and then to another power just multiplies the two powers together. So both people end up with 5 to the power of 6 times 15, which is 5 to the power 90, and it does not matter which order the multiplication happened in. Either way the result on the clock is 2.

That is the same reason red plus blue plus yellow gives the same brown whichever order you pour the paints in.

## What Eve can and cannot do

Eve knows the clock size, 23, the starting number, 5, and the two public mixtures, 8 and 19. To find the secret she needs one of the private numbers, either 6 or 15. To get it, she has to answer the question "5 to what power lands on 8 on this clock?"

With a clock of size 23, she can just try powers one by one and find 6 in a few seconds. This is why the small example is only for learning. In real use the clock has a size with hundreds of digits, and the private numbers are similarly huge. Computers can raise numbers to enormous powers on such a clock very quickly, so Alice and Bob finish in a blink. But nobody has found a fast way to run the process backwards. Eve would be stuck trying astronomically many possibilities.

Nobody has proved that a fast shortcut cannot exist. What we have is decades of very clever people trying and failing to find one. The security rests on that failure, which is the same sort of confidence we place in a lock that no locksmith has ever managed to pick.

## Why it is called public-key cryptography

The numbers 8 and 19 in our example are called public keys. Each person publishes one openly, and it is safe to do so. The numbers 6 and 15 are private keys. Each person keeps one forever, and it never leaves their head or device.

The key insight is that a public key and a private key are linked by a one-way street. Anyone can use your public key to do something that only your private key can complete. This is why strangers can use it. They do not need to meet you or trust a messenger. They only need your public number, which you can print on a billboard.

Other schemes use the same one-way idea in different ways. Some let anyone lock a message with your public key so that only your private key can open it. Others let you stamp a message with your private key so anyone can check with your public key that it really came from you. All of them rely on a task that is easy in one direction and hopeless in the other.

## Where you meet this in daily life

Every time your browser shows a small padlock, something like this exchange has just happened between your device and a website you have never visited before. The two sides agree on a secret in a few thousandths of a second while the data crosses networks full of potential eavesdroppers. They then use that shared secret to scramble everything that follows, such as your messages, your card numbers and your passwords.

So the next time you see the padlock, picture two people mixing paint in public and ending up with a colour that only they can make. It is a trick built on nothing more than remainders on a very large clock.
