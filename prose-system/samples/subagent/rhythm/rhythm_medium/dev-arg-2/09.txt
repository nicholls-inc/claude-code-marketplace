Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, or at least stop treating those estimates as promises, and the sooner engineers and managers admit this, the better their projects will go.

Start with the evidence everyone already carries around in their heads. Nearly every engineer has watched a two-week task stretch into two months, and nearly every manager has been handed a date that turned out to be fiction. We tell ourselves the problem is poor discipline, so we respond with more granular tickets, more planning poker, and more padding. Decades of this have not made estimates reliable, which suggests the practice is failing for a structural reason rather than a lack of effort. Software work is mostly discovery. We estimate the part we can see, and the cost lives in the part we cannot see until we are already inside the code.

The deeper damage comes from what estimates do once they leave the room. A guess made on Monday becomes a commitment by Wednesday and a deadline by the end of the month. Engineers learn that the safest move is to pad the number, so the figure stops reflecting the work and starts reflecting the team's fear. Managers learn that the numbers are inflated, so they push back and negotiate them down, which teaches engineers to pad even more. Meanwhile the team spends hours in meetings producing figures that nobody believes, and those hours come straight out of the time available to build the thing.

Worse, estimates distort the decisions they were meant to inform. When a feature is sized at three weeks, the conversation turns to whether the team can hit three weeks rather than whether the feature is worth building at all. Teams cut corners on testing, skip the refactor that would have made the next change cheap, and ship something brittle to protect a date that was only ever a guess. The estimate becomes the goal, and the customer outcome quietly drops out of view.

The alternative is not chaos. Teams that drop estimates tend to do two things well instead. First, they slice work into small pieces that can ship in a day or two, which makes progress visible without any forecasting at all. Second, they decide what to build by appetite rather than by prediction: leadership says how much time something is worth, and the team shapes the scope to fit, cutting features rather than extending the schedule. When someone needs a forecast, the team's own history of how many small items it finishes per week gives a far more honest answer than any individual's gut feeling.

Some managers will object that the business needs dates, and they are right that coordination matters, since launches, contracts, and marketing campaigns depend on them. But a date derived from observed throughput and protected by flexible scope is more trustworthy than one assembled from a pile of optimistic guesses. If you cannot bring yourself to abandon estimates entirely, at least stop pretending they are accurate. Treat them as rough ranges, never as commitments, and measure what actually ships. Your team will spend less time guessing and more time building, and your stakeholders will finally hear the truth about how long things take: it depends on what we find along the way.
