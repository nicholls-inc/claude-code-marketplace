Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules, and the sooner the better.

A minimum parking requirement is a rule that obliges anyone building a home, shop or office to provide a set number of car spaces, whatever the builder or the future tenants might want. A typical American ordinance asks for one or two spaces per flat, one space per 300 square feet of shop floor and a fixed number per restaurant seat. These ratios spread through American cities from the 1950s, and many were taken from manuals based on a handful of surveys of suburban sites. The economist Donald Shoup, who wrote The High Cost of Free Parking in 2005, found that the figures often rested on very little evidence and that planners usually applied them as if they were settled science.

The first problem is cost. A space in a multi-storey car park in a US city commonly costs somewhere between $25,000 and $50,000 to build, and an underground space costs more. Those costs do not disappear when the law requires the space. They are added to the price of the flat, the rent on the shop unit or the menu price of the meal, and they are paid by everyone, including residents and customers who do not own a car. About a fifth of American households have no car at all in many large cities, and they still pay for spaces they cannot use.

The second problem is land. A surface space takes up roughly 300 square feet once the access lane is counted. On a small urban lot, the parking required can take up more room than the building it serves. Some sites cannot be developed because the required spaces will not fit, and others are built with fewer homes than the zoning would otherwise allow. Old buildings are hit too, since a corner shop in a nineteenth-century street can be unable to change use because it has no room to add spaces. Los Angeles recognised this in 1999 when it exempted conversions of older buildings from parking rules, and the downtown housing that followed is often cited as evidence that the rules were the obstacle.

The usual objection is that without minimums, new buildings will have too few spaces and the overflow will clog nearby streets. Experience so far does not bear this out. Auckland removed its minimums across the city in 2016. Buffalo did the same in 2017, the first large American city to do so, and Minneapolis followed in 2021. In each place developers still build parking where buyers and tenants want it, and lenders still ask for it in many projects. What changes is that the amount is set by the people paying for it and not by a figure in an ordinance. Projects that would have been dropped become possible, and some builders choose to put in fewer spaces and charge less.

Removing a minimum is also different from banning parking, and critics sometimes blur the two. A developer in a suburb with cheap land and a car-dependent customer base will go on providing plenty of spaces, because shops without parking would lose trade. A developer next to a railway station may reasonably decide that half a space per flat is enough. Both choices are made by someone with money at stake, which is a better basis than a rule written for an average site that does not exist.

There is a fair worry about spillover onto public streets, and it deserves a direct answer. The problem there is not that the buildings lack spaces but that the kerb is often free or priced far below what drivers would pay. Where on-street parking is metered at a level that keeps one or two spaces empty per block, as Shoup recommends, nobody can park all day for nothing and the supply works. Residential permit schemes can reserve nearby streets for existing residents. Cities that want to take away the minimums should pair the change with sensible kerbside pricing, and several that have done so report that the combination works.

It is also true that removing minimums will not transform a city overnight. Many developers will keep building much the same amount of parking at first, since banks, habit and local expectations change slowly. The case for reform does not rest on a sudden shift. It rests on removing a rule that adds cost and takes up land without being shown to deliver a benefit, and on letting the amount of parking vary with the place.

This is a view about how to weigh costs, and it is not the only reasonable one. A city that values keeping streets clear of parked cars above cheaper housing might keep some minimums. But those who hold that view should say so plainly, and accept that the price is paid in higher rents and fewer homes. Most cities have never made that choice openly. They inherited a set of ratios and went on applying them. Dropping the minimums would return the decision to builders, buyers and tenants, who know their own needs better than a table in a planning code.
