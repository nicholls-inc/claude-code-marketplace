In the last days of August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district of central London. Cholera is an infection of the gut that causes severe diarrhoea and vomiting, and it can kill a healthy adult within a day or two by draining the body of water. Within ten days of the first cases, about five hundred people living within a short walk of Broad Street had died. Many families left the area, and some streets were close to empty.

John Snow, a physician who lived nearby, had views about the disease that most of his colleagues did not share. He was born in York in 1813, the son of a labourer, and had made his name in medicine as one of the first doctors in Britain to give anaesthetics, including chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. The accepted explanation for cholera at the time was that it spread through bad air, rising from rotting waste and damp ground. Snow thought this was wrong. In 1849 he had published a short book arguing that cholera was caused by something swallowed, and that it passed from the sick to the healthy through contaminated water. He had no microscope evidence for this, because the organism that causes cholera had not yet been identified, and his case rested on the pattern of who fell ill.

When the Soho outbreak began, Snow set out to find that pattern in the streets. He took the names and addresses of the dead from the local register of deaths and visited the houses. He marked each death on a map of the area. The deaths were packed most closely around the water pump in Broad Street, and the further a house stood from the pump, the fewer deaths it had. Snow also noted that, for many houses, the nearest pump was a different one, and that those residents had mostly not been affected.

The exceptions told him as much as the clusters did. A workhouse in Poland Street, which housed 535 people, recorded only five deaths. It was surrounded by the stricken streets, but it had its own well and did not draw from the Broad Street pump. A brewery in Broad Street had no deaths among its workers. They were given a daily allowance of malt liquor and also had a well on the premises, so they had little reason to use the pump. Snow also heard of a widow in Hampstead, several miles away, who died on 2 September along with a niece who had been visiting her. The widow had once lived in Broad Street, and she had water from the pump brought to her each day by cart because she liked the taste. No one else in her neighbourhood was ill.

By 7 September Snow felt he had enough to go to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, the local body that ran the pump. He asked them to take the handle off so that no one could draw water from it. The Board agreed, and the handle was removed the next day. It is often said that this ended the outbreak. The record is less tidy than that, because the number of new cases had already been falling for several days, probably because many residents had fled and others had stopped using the pump out of fear. Snow himself acknowledged this in his later writing, though he continued to hold that the pump was the source.

The explanation for how the well became infected came from someone who had begun as a critic. Henry Whitehead, a young curate at a local church, did not accept Snow's theory at first, but he knew the district and its people far better than Snow did. He carried out his own inquiries and traced the earliest case to a baby girl at 40 Broad Street, who had fallen ill with diarrhoea in late August and died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the child's soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool beneath the house. When the well and the cesspool were later dug up, the brickwork of the cesspool was found to be decayed, and it stood only a short distance from the well. Whitehead came to agree with Snow.

The official response was cooler. A committee of the Board of Health looked at the outbreak and concluded that there was no good evidence for the water theory. The committee suggested the pump's water was not unusual, and the vestry soon put the handle back. Snow's map appeared in the second edition of his book in 1855, and he went on arguing his case until he died of a stroke in 1858, aged 45. Most of the medical establishment was still unconvinced at his death.

The view that cholera travels in water was accepted gradually over the following decades. Filippo Pacini, an Italian anatomist, had described the cholera bacterium in 1854 without gaining much notice, and Robert Koch identified it again in 1883. London's sewers were rebuilt in the 1860s, which separated drinking water from waste far more completely than before. Snow's work is now taught as an early and clear example of finding the cause of a disease by counting cases and mapping where they occurred.
