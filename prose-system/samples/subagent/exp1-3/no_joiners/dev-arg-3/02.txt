Keep the Loop and the Link

Every few years a headteacher or a newspaper columnist announces that handwriting is dead. Children swipe screens before they can speak in full sentences, the argument goes, so why should a seven year old spend precious classroom hours learning to join the letters of her own name? The case sounds modern and practical. It is also mistaken. Primary schools should keep teaching joined up handwriting, and they should teach it properly, with daily time and patient correction, rather than treating it as a decorative leftover from the age of inkwells. The reasons are cognitive, practical and, less obviously, about fairness.

Begin with what happens in a child's head. Writing by hand is not simply a slow way of producing text that a keyboard could produce faster. When a child forms a letter, she has to recall its shape from memory, plan the movement, and guide the pen through a sequence of strokes while watching the result appear. Pressing a key demands none of this. Every letter on a keyboard looks the same under the finger and requires only that the child knows where to press. Researchers who have scanned the brains of young children before they can read have found that those who practise writing letters by hand show stronger activity in the regions linked to reading than those who type or trace the same letters. Studies of older students have found richer patterns of connection across the brain during handwriting than during typing. None of this proves that a pen makes anyone cleverer. It does suggest that the effortful, physical act of making letters helps those letters settle into memory, and that is exactly what a young reader needs.

Joined writing adds something further. In a joined style the pen stays on the page for the length of a whole word, and the hand learns the word as a single flowing movement rather than as a heap of separate marks. Teachers of spelling have long observed that this helps. Common letter patterns such as the ending of a verb or the double letters in a familiar word become a rhythm in the fingers, and a child who has that rhythm tends to spell the pattern correctly without stopping to think. Print asks the child to lift the pen and restart for every letter, which breaks each word into pieces. Joining reduces the number of decisions a child must make, and a child with fewer decisions to make at the point of the pen has more attention left over for the ideas she is trying to express. Studies of handwriting fluency have repeatedly found that children who write quickly and legibly produce longer and better composed work than those who struggle to form their letters. The mechanics, when they are mastered, get out of the way of the thinking.

Speed matters in a very practical sense too. Pupils in most countries still sit their important examinations with a pen in their hand. A secondary school student who must write several pages in an hour needs a hand that is both fast and readable, and a slow printed hand will fail her at precisely the moment she can least afford it. Examiners cannot award marks for words they cannot read. A child who has learned a fluent joined hand in primary school carries that advantage for the rest of her education, and the cost of acquiring it was a few minutes a day at the age when small hands are most ready to learn.

The strongest objection is that print is good enough. Many countries teach a simple printed hand, and children seem to survive. Some educators argue that a clean, upright print is easier to learn, easier to read and closer to the typeface children see in books. There is something in this. Print is a sensible first stage, and nobody should rush a five year old into loops and links before she can control a pencil. But the argument proves less than it appears to. When schools teach print alone and say nothing more, children do not stay with print. By the age of ten or eleven most of them invent their own half joined scrawl, copied from older pupils and shaped by haste, with no consistent logic and little regard for the reader. The choice is therefore not between joining and not joining. It is between a joined style that is taught well and a joined style that is picked up by accident. A taught hand has sensible shapes, sensible entry and exit strokes, and a consistent slope. An accidental hand has none of these, and it is the pupils who struggled most with fine control who suffer the worst outcomes.

This brings me to the question of fairness, which is the point I find most persuasive. Children arrive at school with enormously different experiences. Some have a parent who sits with them at the kitchen table, a drawer full of pens and a tablet loaded with typing games. Others have none of these things. Handwriting is one of the few skills for which school is the main teacher for almost every child, and the classroom can therefore close a gap that the home would otherwise widen. If schools step back, the children who benefit from private practice will still learn to write well, while those who relied on school to teach them will be left with an untidy and tiring hand, and with the quiet embarrassment that follows. There is also the matter of children with conditions such as dyspraxia or dysgraphia. For many of them a continuous flowing line is actually easier than a series of lifted and restarted marks, because the movement has a single shape to remember rather than dozens. Taking joined writing away from the curriculum removes a tool that some of the children who most need it have found helpful.

A further reason is cultural, and it is easily overlooked. A great deal of written history is joined up. Letters from grandparents, the margins of old school books, census entries, wartime diaries and the signatures on a family's marriage certificates are all written in a running hand. A child who cannot read cursive is shut out from this material unless somebody translates it for her. She is also shut out of a small but real pleasure, which is the ability to write a note to a friend that looks like it came from a person and not from a printer. Cards, condolence letters, shopping lists and the notes that people leave one another on the fridge will go on being handwritten for a very long time, and most adults will go on writing them in a joined style whether or not they were ever taught one.

Then there is the argument about time. The curriculum is crowded, say the critics, and every minute spent on loops and links is a minute taken from reading, mathematics or computing. This would be a serious point if joined writing required lengthy lessons. It does not. A short daily session of ten minutes, spread across the first four or five years of school, is enough to teach a fluent hand, and that session pays for itself many times over. A child who writes easily completes written tasks in every subject more quickly and with less fatigue. The time that handwriting takes is returned to every other lesson. By contrast the time spent on typing is not saved at all, because keyboard skills also have to be taught. Nobody suggests that children will simply absorb touch typing from the air. If the argument is that we should teach keyboard skills because they matter, the same argument supports teaching handwriting, since both matter and neither arrives by itself.

None of this means schools should cling to the habits of the past. The best handwriting teaching today is not the grim copying of pages that older readers may remember. It begins with large movements and patterns, moves to simple letter shapes with exit strokes built in from the start, and introduces joins gradually as the hand becomes steady. It uses a single consistent model across a school, so that a child does not have to relearn her letters each time she changes class. It lets children write with real purpose, in letters, stories and lists, so that practice never feels like punishment. It also accepts that a child's final hand will be her own, with some personal quirks, so long as it is quick and clear.

Nor does teaching handwriting mean abandoning technology. Children should learn to type, and they should use screens where screens do the job better, for editing drafts, researching, drawing, making films and building things. A balanced curriculum has room for both. The point is that one skill does not replace the other, and that the older one has benefits the newer one cannot supply. Asking a child to choose between them would be like asking her to choose between walking and cycling. She will want both, and she will find that each supports the other.

So the answer to the question is yes. Primary schools should still teach joined up handwriting, and they should do it with confidence. It strengthens the link between hand, eye and memory at the age when that link is forming. It speeds up writing and frees attention for ideas. It protects the children who have the least support at home, and it keeps open the door to a long written heritage. It costs a few minutes a day. A society that can afford to spend years teaching children to read can afford a few minutes to teach them to write in a way that lets them be read, and that gives them the quiet satisfaction of a line of their own making running smoothly across the page.
