Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon. The rules look like technical details in a zoning code, but they quietly shape how much a city costs to live in, how far people must travel, and what can be built at all. Removing them does not ban parking. It simply returns the decision to the people who know most about what a given building needs.

Start with what a parking mandate actually is. A city decides that every apartment, restaurant, or office must come with a fixed number of off-street spaces, usually calculated from a formula drawn from national manuals that were built on suburban sites with abundant free parking. Those formulas often assume that demand peaks on the busiest imaginable day, so lots are sized for a rush that comes a few times a year and sit mostly empty the rest of the time. Planners are not being foolish. They are guessing, and a guess written into law becomes a requirement that every builder must satisfy whether or not it makes sense.

The first cost of that guess is money. A surface space takes up several hundred square feet of land, and a structured or underground space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into the rent on the apartment, the price of the sandwich, and the lease on the office, and they are paid by everyone, including the households that own no car. A tenant who gets by on a bus pass and a bicycle ends up subsidizing storage for other people's vehicles. In a housing market already short of affordable homes, forcing every new building to carry that burden is hard to defend.

The second cost is what never gets built. Parking requirements eat into the buildable area of a lot, and on small or awkward sites they can make a project impossible. A corner lot that could hold a few apartments over a shop instead stays vacant or becomes a drive-through, because the required spaces would consume the site. Old neighborhoods are full of charming buildings that could not legally be built today, since they sit on tiny lots with no off-street parking at all. We have effectively outlawed the form of development that produced many of our most loved and walkable places.

Defenders of the rules raise a reasonable worry, which is that without mandates, new buildings will push their cars onto neighboring streets and make life harder for existing residents. That concern deserves a serious answer, and the answer is that spillover is a problem of pricing, not of building codes. When street parking is free, it will always be overrun, with or without requirements. Cities that manage curb space with permits, meters, or time limits can protect residents directly, and they can use the revenue to improve the neighborhood. Requiring developers to oversupply private spaces is a clumsy substitute for managing a public resource properly.

It also helps to look at what happens when cities actually drop the rules. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and Minneapolis and several other cities have followed in recent years. Developers there did not stop building parking. They built what they believed their tenants and customers would want, and in many cases that was less than the old formulas demanded. Projects that were previously stuck began to pencil out, and developers gained room to include more homes or ground-floor shops in place of concrete. The sky did not fall, and the cars did not take over the sidewalks.

The strongest argument, though, is about freedom and information. A developer who wants to attract buyers has every reason to provide enough parking, because a building that tenants cannot use will not fill. A lender will insist on it. A grocery store knows far better than a city hall committee how many customers will arrive by car. Where parking is scarce and valuable, the market will supply it, and where it is not needed, nobody will be forced to pay for it. This is simply the ordinary principle that those closest to a decision should make it, applied to a corner of the economy where we have long ignored it.

There is a broader public interest too. Abundant required parking spreads buildings apart, lengthens walking distances, and makes driving the only practical way to get anywhere. That raises emissions, worsens traffic, and leaves residents who cannot drive, whether because of age, disability, or income, stranded. A city that wants compact, lively, and affordable neighborhoods cannot keep mandating the opposite in its zoning code. The rules push in one direction and the city's stated goals in another.

None of this means cars will disappear or that parking never matters. It means that the number of spaces should be a business decision and a neighborhood management question, not a number copied from a manual. Cities that remove their minimums keep every tool they need to handle real problems, and they gain cheaper housing, more flexible sites, and a built environment shaped by actual demand. The case for keeping the mandates rests on a habit rather than on evidence. It is time to let the habit go.
