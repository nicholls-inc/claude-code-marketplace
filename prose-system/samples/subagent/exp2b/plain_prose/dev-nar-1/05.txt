In late August 1854, a terrible illness arrived in Soho, a crowded, working-class district of London. Cholera is a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhoea, and it can drain the body of water so quickly that a healthy person may be dead within a day or two. In the space of a few days, hundreds of people living around Broad Street fell ill and died. Entire families were lost. The neighbourhood emptied as those who could afford to leave fled, and those who stayed buried their dead in numbers the parish had never seen.

At the time, almost every educated person believed they knew what caused cholera, and they were wrong. The prevailing idea was the miasma theory: that disease spread through foul air, the stench rising from rotting rubbish, sewers and graveyards. London in the 1850s certainly smelled dreadful, so the theory seemed to fit common experience. Nobody yet knew about germs. The microscopic organisms that cause infectious disease had not been accepted as an explanation, and the idea that an illness could be passed along by something invisible in the water was regarded with suspicion.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked nearby, was one of the few who doubted the official view. He had been thinking about cholera for years. Snow noticed that the disease began with symptoms of the stomach and bowels rather than the lungs, which seemed odd if people were breathing in a poisonous vapour. He suspected instead that something was being swallowed, and that sufferers then passed it on through their waste, which could contaminate food or drinking water. He had published his argument in 1849, but few were persuaded. The outbreak on his own doorstep gave him a chance to prove it.

Snow began in the most direct way possible: he walked the streets and asked questions. He wanted to know where the dead had lived and, crucially, where they had gotten their water. In those days most households had no taps. People collected water from public pumps and carried it home, and the quality of that water varied from pump to pump. Snow obtained the list of deaths from the local registrar and visited the homes of the victims, talking with surviving relatives and neighbours.

What he found was striking. Nearly all of the deaths were clustered close to the pump on Broad Street. Snow marked each death on a map of the district, building a picture that made the pattern easy to see. The cases piled up around the pump like iron filings around a magnet. Householders nearer to other pumps were far less affected.

The exceptions proved most persuasive of all. A workhouse nearby, housing hundreds of poor residents, had suffered remarkably few deaths. Snow discovered it had its own well, so its inmates had no need to use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped almost entirely, and the workers there, it turned out, drank the beer they brewed, and had their own well too. Meanwhile, Snow heard of a woman who lived far away in Hampstead and had died of cholera. Her family told him that she had always liked the taste of the water from the Broad Street pump, so a servant had been sent to fetch bottles of it for her. Her niece, who drank it too, also died.

Snow took his evidence to the local board of guardians, the officials who governed the parish. On 8 September, he urged them to take action. They agreed, in the end, to remove the handle from the pump, so that no one could draw water from it. The outbreak was already fading by then, partly because so many residents had fled and perhaps because the worst of the contamination had passed, so the removal of the handle cannot be said to have ended it on its own. Still, the gesture became famous.

The source of the contamination was later traced with the help of a local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who had initially doubted Snow's theory. Whitehead investigated further and found that a baby had fallen sick with cholera in a house on Broad Street just before the outbreak, and that the family had washed its soiled nappies in a cesspool that, as an inspection revealed, leaked toward the well. The pump's water had been poisoned by the household's own waste.

Officials were slow to accept Snow's conclusion. A formal inquiry reported that there was insufficient evidence to blame the water, and the miasma theory held on for years. Snow died in 1858, never having seen his ideas widely accepted. Yet his careful mapping, his questions, and his attention to the odd cases that did not fit became the foundation of modern epidemiology, and later research confirmed that he had been right about how cholera spreads.
