**Agreeing on a Secret in Public**

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They can only talk over a line that Eve, a stranger, can hear word for word. They want a shared secret that Eve cannot work out, even though she hears everything. This sounds impossible. It is possible, and the idea behind it is one of the cleverest in modern mathematics. It protects almost everything you do online, such as bank logins and private messages.

**The paint analogy**

Start with colours. Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow. Eve hears this, so yellow is public.

Alice secretly picks a colour of her own, say red. She mixes it into the yellow and sends the resulting orange mixture to Bob. Bob secretly picks blue, mixes it into the yellow, and sends the green mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures travel along the line.

Now Alice adds her secret red to Bob's green mixture. Bob adds his secret blue to Alice's orange mixture. Each ends up with yellow, red and blue mixed together, so they hold the same final colour. Eve has yellow, orange and green. She cannot add red or blue, because she never learned them. Worse, you cannot easily unmix paint to find out what went in.

The whole trick is a process that is easy in one direction and very hard to reverse. Mixing is easy, and unmixing is hard. We need a mathematical version of that.

**Clock arithmetic**

The mathematical version uses clock arithmetic, which you may know already. On a 12-hour clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, not 15. You divide by 12 and keep only the remainder. Mathematicians call this "mod 12". So 15 mod 12 is 3.

We can use any clock size. Alice and Bob will use a clock with 23 hours, so every answer is a whole number from 0 to 22. They also need a starting number, which plays the role of the yellow paint. They choose 5. Both numbers, 23 and 5, are announced openly. Eve hears them.

**The exchange, step by step**

Here is the process with the actual numbers. The notation 5^6 means 5 multiplied by itself six times.

1. Alice secretly chooses the number 6. She computes 5^6, which is 15,625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 with remainder 8. So she sends Bob the number 8.

2. Bob secretly chooses 15. He computes 5^15 on the 23-hour clock, which comes out to 19. He sends Alice the number 19.

3. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power: 19^6. On the 23-hour clock this is 2.

4. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power: 8^15. On the 23-hour clock this is also 2.

Both now hold the number 2. They never said it aloud, and they never met. This is their shared secret, and they can use it as the key to lock their messages.

**Why do both get the same answer?**

This is the part that feels like a magic trick, but school algebra explains it. Alice's final number is (5^b)^a, where a is her secret and b is Bob's. Bob's final number is (5^a)^b. Raising a power to a power means multiplying the exponents, and multiplication does not care about order. So a times b equals b times a, and both people land on 5 raised to the power (a times b). The clock arithmetic carries through the whole calculation without changing this.

Neither person ever needed the other's secret. Each needed only their own secret and the other person's public number. That is the paint trick in numbers.

**What Eve sees**

Eve knows the clock size 23, the starting number 5, Alice's public number 8, and Bob's public number 19. To find the secret, she needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. In other words she must answer this question: "5 to what power gives 8 on the 23-hour clock?"

With a clock this small, she could simply try every possibility. Raise 5 to the powers 1, 2, 3 and so on, and see which gives 8. There are only 22 possibilities, so she would finish in minutes with a pencil. That is why this example is only an illustration.

**Why real systems are safe**

In real use, the clock is not 23 hours. It has a number with hundreds of digits, and the secret powers are similarly enormous. The "try every power" approach would then need more steps than there are atoms in the universe. No computer ever built could finish it.

The reason clock arithmetic works as one-way mixing is that it scrambles things. On an ordinary calculator, bigger powers give bigger answers, so you could guess, check whether you overshot, and home in. On a clock, the answers jump around the dial in what looks like a random pattern. Knowing that 5^6 gives 8 tells you nothing useful about whether 5^7 is bigger or smaller. There is no "getting warmer". The only known general way forward is to try values one by one, or to use cleverer methods that are still far too slow at these sizes.

Meanwhile, the forward direction stays quick for honest users. Raising a number to a huge power sounds like a lot of multiplication, but there is a shortcut: square the number repeatedly, then combine only the squares you need. A power in the hundreds of digits takes a few hundred steps, which a phone does in a blink. The gap between easy and hard is what makes the scheme useful: quick for Alice and Bob, hopeless for Eve.

**Why this is called public-key cryptography**

Notice what each person has. Alice's secret number is her **private key**. The number she sends openly, 8, is her **public key**. Anyone may see it, and seeing it does not help them. The two keys are mathematically linked, but you cannot easily go from the public one back to the private one.

Before this idea appeared in the 1970s, two people wanting a secret code had to meet beforehand and swap a key in person, or send it by a trusted courier. That does not work for a shop you have never visited, or a friend on another continent. Public-key methods removed that need. Your browser and a website you have never visited can agree on a secret in a fraction of a second, in full view of everyone else on the network.

Real systems usually add more pieces. The shared secret from an exchange like this typically becomes the key for a faster scrambling method that handles the actual messages. A related family of public-key schemes lets you sign messages so others can verify who sent them. But the core idea is the one above.

**What can still go wrong**

The scheme defeats an eavesdropper who only listens. It does not stop someone who actively sits in the middle, pretending to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, and running a separate exchange with each. To stop that, people need some way of confirming they are talking to the right party, which is the job of digital certificates and signatures. The mathematics of the secret-sharing step is sound. The remaining weakness is making sure you are talking to the right person.

**The idea in one sentence**

Two people each combine a private secret with a shared public starting point, swap the results in the open, and each combine the other's result with their own secret. Because of the way powers multiply, they reach the same answer, while a listener who sees only the swapped results would have to undo a one-way scrambling that nobody knows how to undo quickly.
