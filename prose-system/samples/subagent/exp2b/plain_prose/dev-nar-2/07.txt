On the evening of April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise. It was the third mission meant to land humans on the Moon, and by then the public had grown a little bored with the idea. Apollo 11 had been a triumph, Apollo 12 a confident repeat, and television networks had stopped carrying the flights live from start to finish. Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights and the commander, was the most experienced astronaut in the program. Swigert was a late addition: the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been grounded days before launch after exposure to German measles, and Swigert, the backup, took his seat. Haise was to pilot the lunar module, named Aquarius, and walk on the Moon's Fra Mauro highlands. The command module was named Odyssey.

The first two days were routine. The crew separated from the spent third stage, turned around, docked with the lunar module and pulled it free, then settled into the long coast toward the Moon. They gave a television broadcast on the evening of April 13, showing viewers around the spacecraft and floating in the tunnel between the two vehicles. Almost nobody on the ground was watching closely. Within an hour, that would change.

At about 9:07 that night, Houston asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that mixes the supercooled oxygen and hydrogen so that the sensors read accurately. Swigert flipped the switch. Roughly ninety seconds later the crew heard a loud bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Lovell, looking at his instruments, saw a problem with electrical power and, soon after, a warning on one of the oxygen tanks. Then came the sentence that has become shorthand for the whole episode: Swigert reported, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, adding that there had been a main bus B undervolt.

The cause lay in oxygen tank number two in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the command module that held the mission's power and life support. Years earlier, during ground testing, the tank had been damaged, and a later heating test had burned away the Teflon insulation on the wires of its fan motors. Nobody knew. When Swigert switched on the fans, a spark in the bare wiring ignited the oxygen in the tank, the pressure spiked, and the tank burst, blowing off a panel of the service module's outer skin. Oxygen began venting into space, and the second tank was damaged too. Without oxygen the fuel cells that supplied electricity and water could not run, and the command module, powered by its own batteries, had only a few hours of life.

In Mission Control, flight director Gene Kranz and his team worked to understand a spacecraft that seemed to be dying piece by piece. Lovell, peering out a window, saw gas streaming into space and told Houston so. The crew attempted to isolate the failing tanks, but the pressure kept dropping. Within about three hours the oxygen was gone. The decision was made to use the lunar module as a lifeboat, a possibility engineers had studied but never expected to need. Haise and Lovell powered up Aquarius, while Swigert shut down Odyssey to conserve its batteries for the return to Earth's atmosphere. A landing on the Moon was no longer in question. The question was whether the three men could get home.

The spacecraft was on a free-return trajectory, a path that would swing it around the Moon and back toward Earth, but it had been adjusted to aim at the lunar landing site and would no longer bring the crew near home. Engineers decided to fire the lunar module's descent engine, designed for landing, to put the craft back on course. After sweeping behind the far side of the Moon, where the crew passed farther from Earth than any humans had been, a second burn about two hours later sped the return and moved the landing zone to the Pacific Ocean.

Then the long, cold wait began. Aquarius was built to support two men for about two days on the lunar surface, not three men for four days. To save power, the crew switched off almost everything, including the heaters. The cabin temperature fell toward freezing, water condensed on the walls, and the men slept little. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection after rationing his water. Water itself was scarce, and the crew drank only a few ounces a day.

The most urgent danger was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrub the gas from the air, were designed for two people and were filling up. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square, while the lunar module's receptacles were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the items the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a spacesuit hose and duct tape, and worked out a way to join the square canister to the round opening. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the three men built the adapter themselves. Within hours, the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

One more problem remained. With the main guidance computer in the command module powered down, the crew would have to restart it and align the spacecraft before reentry, using a procedure never meant to be performed under these conditions. A course correction burn was also needed, and it had to be done manually, because the alignment platform in Aquarius was off. Lovell steered by using the Earth's terminator as a reference, while Haise timed the burn. It worked. Mattingly, who had been bumped from the flight, joined engineers in a simulator and helped develop the sequence for powering Odyssey back up with the little battery power that remained, a procedure that had to be written, tested and read up to the crew within a day.

On April 17, the crew prepared for the end of the journey. They jettisoned the damaged service module and got their first look at it. Lovell reported that an entire panel was missing and the side was blackened and wrecked, a sight that stunned everyone who saw the photographs. They then released Aquarius, which had saved their lives, and it burned up over the Pacific.

Reentry carried one last uncertainty. Blackout from the plasma around the capsule normally lasts about three minutes, and it stretched longer than expected, leaving Houston waiting in silence. Then Swigert's voice came through, and parachutes appeared on television. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa at about 1:07 in the afternoon, Houston time, on April 17. The three men were picked up by the USS Iwo Jima and found to be tired, dehydrated and cold, but otherwise well.

The investigation that followed pinned the failure on the tank and its history, and the design was changed for later flights. Apollo 14 flew the following year, landing at the Fra Mauro site that Haise and Lovell had never reached. Lovell later said that the lesson he took was not about the explosion but about the people on the ground. The flight was never officially a failure, in the language of NASA; it was called a successful failure.
