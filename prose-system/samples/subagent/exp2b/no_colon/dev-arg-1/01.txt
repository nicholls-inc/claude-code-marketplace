**Let Builders Decide How Much Parking Is Enough**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet rule shaping the ground beneath your feet. A restaurant must provide a set number of parking spaces per thousand square feet. An apartment building must provide one or two per unit. These minimums were mostly written in the middle of the last century, often copied from manuals built on traffic counts at suburban shopping centers. They have outlived their logic, and cities should repeal them.

The first problem is that the numbers are guesses dressed up as science. The standard reference for parking demand rests on a small number of sites, mostly suburban and mostly with free parking, and the figures it produces are treated as the minimum every project must meet. Donald Shoup, the UCLA scholar who made this his life's work, found that the requirements often bear little relation to how people actually travel. When a city demands enough spaces to serve the busiest hour of the busiest day of the year, it guarantees that lots sit mostly empty the rest of the time. Anyone who has seen a vast, vacant lot beside a half-built neighborhood has seen the result.

The second problem is cost. A surface space takes up roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles, and a space in a garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, far more underground. Those costs do not vanish. They are passed to tenants and buyers, including the many households that own no car at all. A person who rides the bus pays for parking they never use, folded into the rent. Requirements that sound like neutral technical standards are really a hidden tax on housing, and it falls hardest on people with the least money.

The third problem is what the rules do to the shape of a city. Land is finite. Every acre given to asphalt is an acre not holding homes, shops, or offices. Minimums spread buildings apart, which makes walking less pleasant and transit less viable, which makes driving feel more necessary, which seems to justify still more parking. It is a loop, and the rulebook keeps it spinning. Small businesses suffer most. A corner lot that once could have held a bakery cannot be reused, because the required spaces would swallow the site. Old buildings in historic downtowns often could not be built today, because the code would forbid the very density that made them valuable.

Defenders of minimums raise a fair worry. If developers build too little parking, will cars flood the surrounding streets and make life worse for existing residents? The fear is understandable, but it blames the wrong thing. Spillover happens because street parking is free or nearly free, so everyone treats it as a commons to be grabbed. The remedy is to manage the curb, with permits for residents, meters that rise with demand, and revenue returned to the neighborhood. Where cities have done this, the curb stops being a daily fight. Requiring every private building to hide a giant lot is an expensive and blunt way to solve a pricing problem.

Removing minimums also does not ban parking. This point gets lost in the argument. When a city repeals its requirements, developers remain free to build as many spaces as their tenants and lenders want. Lenders often insist on a healthy amount, and shoppers still expect somewhere to leave a car. Suburban strip malls and big employers will keep building lots, because the market asks for them. What changes is that a builder in a walkable neighborhood near a train line can offer fewer spaces and charge lower rents, or add units where garages would have been. The rule stops forcing one answer on every site and lets the particular place decide.

The evidence from cities that have tried this is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide requirements in 2017, and Minneapolis and Anchorage have done likewise. Reports from these places point to new projects with more flexible parking, more homes on small lots, and no wave of gridlock or chaos. Some developers still build plenty of parking. Others build less. All of them now make the choice based on cost and demand and not on a formula nobody can defend. Several states have since moved to limit or ban minimums near transit, a sign that this is no longer a fringe idea.

The deeper point is about who should bear a risk. If a builder misjudges demand and supplies too little parking, that builder loses money and learns from it. If a city misjudges and mandates too much, everyone pays, forever, in higher prices and wasted land. We already trust markets and individual judgment to decide how many bathrooms a restaurant needs or how large a shop window should be. Parking deserves no special exemption.

Cities face a housing shortage, a climate challenge, and tight budgets. Scrapping parking minimums costs the public treasury almost nothing and frees private money to build what people actually want. It is among the cheapest reforms available. Cities should stop legislating the number of spaces and let people who know their sites decide.
