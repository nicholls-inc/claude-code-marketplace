**Keep Teaching the Join**

Every autumn, a primary teacher somewhere hands out a sheet of loopy letters and asks seven-year-olds to trace them. The exercise can look quaint. Most adults now write by hand only for a signature or a shopping list, and a child who will spend her working life typing has a fair question: why spend hours on joins at all? I think the question deserves a serious answer. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, but they should stop treating it as a badge of achievement and start treating it as a tool for spelling, reading and fluent writing.

The strongest objection comes first, because it is a good one. Keyboards are everywhere, speech recognition is improving, and a child who leaves school able to produce clear text on a device has the skill most adults actually use. Curriculum time is finite. Every hour spent on letter formation is an hour not spent on reading, number or science. If handwriting is only a delivery mechanism for words, the argument runs, we should optimise for the mechanism people will really use, and that is not cursive.

I accept most of that premise and reject the conclusion. The objection assumes handwriting is merely a delivery mechanism, and the evidence suggests it is more than that, at least in the early years. Virginia Berninger and her colleagues have spent two decades studying children's writing, and their work points in a consistent direction: children who practise forming letters by hand tend to become better spellers and more fluent composers than children who learn mainly through typing. One plausible reason is that producing a letter engages motor and visual memory in a way that pressing a key does not. The picture is not tidy. Some of the more dramatic claims, such as the suggestion that taking longhand notes deepens conceptual understanding, have run into replication problems. But the modest finding is robust enough to matter. Practice in making letters supports the link between sounds and symbols, which is exactly the link young children are still building.

Joined handwriting has a case beyond handwriting in general. A join is not decoration. It turns each word into something closer to a single continuous movement, so the hand does not lift between letters and the child is not repeatedly deciding where the next letter begins. For a child who struggles with spacing, letter order or reversals, a continuous stroke can reduce the number of small decisions made per word. Many teachers report that children who learn joins write more evenly and more quickly after a few months. That is anecdote rather than controlled evidence, and I would not build policy on it alone. But the mechanism is plausible, and a plausible mechanism is a reasonable basis for teaching something that costs little.

Speaking of cost, the objectors tend to overestimate it. Teaching joins is rarely a separate lesson. In most schools it is a slice of a daily handwriting routine that might run for ten minutes, sometimes less once children can form the letters. Set that against the rest of the writing curriculum, which is substantial, and the marginal cost of keeping joins is small. Dropping them saves a few sessions a term at most. It is hard to believe that those few sessions are the thing that decides whether a child will go on to write well or badly. If anything, the bigger time costs come from children who never acquire a fluent hand and then spend years working around it, slowly, in every subject.

There is also a practical argument that advocates of dropping cursive often skip: reading. Children meet handwriting constantly, even if they rarely produce it. Birthday cards, letters from grandparents, the notes a teacher writes on a whiteboard in a flowing hand, the signatures and annotations on forms, and the historical documents that museums and archives display are all written in joined script. A child who cannot read joined writing is cut off from a whole category of text that was written by hand for centuries. This does not mean every child must write beautifully. It does mean every child should be able to decode the script that surrounds them, and teaching the join is the most direct route to that ability. Print-only instruction leaves a gap that children eventually have to fill for themselves, usually without much help.

Then there is something less measurable but not trivial, which is the sense of competence a child gets from a neat hand. Handwriting is personal. A child who can produce a clear, fluent script has a small, real experience of mastery, and it is visible to her, to her classmates and to the adults who read her work. Schools that abandon the join often replace it with a print style that is perfectly legible but leaves children feeling that writing by hand is something they are simply bad at. Once that belief takes hold, it shapes how willingly children write at all, and willingness to write is hard to rebuild once it is lost. That outcome is worse than the one the reformers are trying to avoid.

I want to be careful about what I am not claiming. I am not arguing that primary schools should spend most of their time on handwriting, that children should be marked down in history or science for untidy joins, or that typing should be neglected. Typing is a skill in its own right, and it should be taught deliberately, probably from the upper primary years, with attention to the posture and key layout that make it sustainable. I am also not arguing against flexibility. A child with dysgraphia, a motor difficulty or a physical disability may need a different route, including a keyboard, and the school should provide one without fuss or stigma. My claim concerns the default, not the exceptions.

Nor should joins be taught as a rigid orthodoxy. Some letter pairs are clearer apart than joined, and the English national curriculum, like many others, expects children to learn when to leave letters unjoined as well as how to join them. A sensible school policy respects that judgement and does not insist on a single style for every letter in every word. The aim is legibility and speed, not conformity to a model alphabet, and teachers should be willing to accept a child's personal variation as long as the writing is readable and efficient.

So the strongest version of my position is this. Primary schools should teach joined-up handwriting to all children as a fluency skill, beginning once letter formation is secure, probably in the second or third year, with short and regular practice. They should judge it by legibility and speed rather than by perfect copperplate. They should teach children when joins help and when they do not. And they should teach typing alongside, so that children learn both tools instead of being forced to choose between them on the basis of a fashion.

What would change my mind? Better evidence that the spelling and composition benefits come from something other than the join would weaken the case for joins specifically, though not for handwriting in general. A well-designed trial showing that children taught print-only and children taught joins reach the same fluency, spelling and reading outcomes by age eleven would make me reconsider the time cost, and I would welcome such research. Schools should be willing to change when it arrives. But the evidence we have does not justify dropping the join. It justifies teaching it well and keeping it in proportion.

The question is ultimately about what primary school is for. If its only purpose is to prepare children for the tasks of the next decade, the join looks like an antique, and so, perhaps, does much of the handwriting curriculum. But if primary school also gives children a stable foundation for reading and spelling, a sense of their own competence, and access to the written world in all its forms, then a short, sensible daily practice of joined handwriting earns its place. Keep teaching the join. Teach it briefly, teach it well, and stop pretending it is either the most important thing a child learns or the least.
