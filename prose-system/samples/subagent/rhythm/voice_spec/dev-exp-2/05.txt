Two kinds of timekeeping are involved. One is astronomical: a day is the time the Earth takes to rotate, and that has long been measured against the sun and stars. The other is atomic. Since 1967 the second has been defined by the vibrations of a caesium atom, exactly 9,192,631,770 of them, and clocks built on that standard keep a steadier beat than the planet does.\n\nThe Earth does not rotate at a constant rate. Tides caused by the moon act as a brake and make the day longer by roughly two thousandths of a second each century. Earthquakes, movements of the molten core and changes in ice and ocean also shift the rate from year to year. Left alone, an atomic clock would slowly drift away from the time shown by the sky. In 1972 the authorities agreed to keep the two within 0.9 of a second by adding a leap second when needed, usually on 30 June or 31 December. The International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service in Paris decides when, and announces it some months ahead. Twenty-seven have been added, the most recent on 31 December 2016. On those days the final minute has 61 seconds, and the clock reads 23:59:60.\n\nThe trouble is mostly with computers. Most software assumes that every minute has 60 seconds, and a minute with 61 can cause errors, duplicated timestamps or crashes. Several well-known outages have been linked to leap seconds, including problems at Reddit and Qantas in 2012 and at Cloudflare at the end of 2016. Large technology companies have dealt with this by spreading the extra second across a day, a method known as smearing, but different companies do it differently, which leaves their clocks briefly out of agreement.\n\nA further difficulty has appeared recently. Since about 2020 the Earth has been turning slightly faster than before, so the days are a little shorter. If that continues, a second would have to be removed rather than added.
