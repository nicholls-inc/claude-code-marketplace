Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and who can only talk through a channel that anyone may overhear, perhaps a long row of tin cans on strings with a stranger sitting somewhere along it. They want to end up holding the same secret number, one that the stranger, who hears every word, cannot work out. This sounds impossible, because whatever Alice says to Bob the stranger hears too, and for most of history the only way to share a secret was to meet first and agree on it in private, or to carry a codebook from one place to another. The trick that removed that requirement, published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, rests on a kind of arithmetic in which some calculations are easy to do forwards and practically impossible to undo, and the way it works can be followed with nothing more than multiplication and remainders.

The easiest picture to start from is mixing paint. Suppose Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common colour, say yellow, which the stranger therefore knows as well. Each of them then secretly picks a private colour of their own, Alice choosing red and Bob choosing blue, and each mixes their private colour into a pot of the shared yellow and sends the resulting pot across the line, so that the stranger sees an orange-ish pot going one way and a greenish pot going the other. Once Alice receives Bob's mixture she stirs in her own secret red, and Bob, on receiving Alice's mixture, stirs in his own secret blue, and both of them now hold a pot containing yellow, red and blue in equal measure, which is the same muddy brown because the order of mixing makes no difference to the result. The stranger holds only the yellow and the two half-mixed pots, and since paint cannot be unmixed there is no practical way to pull the red or the blue back out of them, so the final brown is out of reach. The whole scheme depends on mixing being easy and un-mixing being hopeless, and numbers have an operation that behaves in exactly that way.

The operation needs a little preparation, starting with the idea of a remainder. When you tell the time on a clock face you already do this arithmetic, since five hours after ten o'clock is three o'clock, because after reaching twelve the count wraps around and starts again. Mathematicians describe this by saying that fifteen leaves a remainder of three when divided by twelve, and they write the process of dividing and keeping only the remainder as mod, so that 15 mod 12 equals 3. We can use a clock with any number of positions we like, and for the scheme we will use a clock with twenty-three positions, which means that every calculation ends by dividing by 23 and keeping only the remainder. Because the answers always land between 0 and 22 the numbers never grow out of hand, but they also appear to jump about unpredictably, and that apparent randomness is where the secrecy comes from.

The operation we need is repeated multiplication, which you will have met as powers. Five to the power of three means five times five times five, which is 125, and on our 23-position clock that becomes 125 mod 23, which is 10, because 23 goes into 125 five times, making 115, and leaves 10. Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers, the clock size 23 and a base number 5, and both of these may be shouted down the line for the stranger to hear. Alice now privately chooses a number, 6, and works out 5 to the power of 6 on the clock, which is 15,625, and dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8. She sends the 8 to Bob, keeping the 6 to herself. Bob privately chooses 15, works out 5 to the power of 15 on the same clock, gets 19, and sends the 19 to Alice while keeping his 15 hidden.

The moment of agreement comes next, and it is simpler than it sounds. Alice takes the 19 that Bob sent her and raises it to her private power of 6, which gives a remainder of 2 on the clock. Bob takes the 8 that Alice sent him and raises it to his private power of 15, and he also lands on 2. They have arrived at the same number without ever transmitting it, and the reason is that both calculations are really the same one in disguise: Alice computed (5 to the power of 15) to the power of 6, Bob computed (5 to the power of 6) to the power of 15, and both equal 5 to the power of 6 times 15, since multiplying the powers together gives the same total whichever of them you do first. This corresponds to the paint pots, where the order of stirring did not matter, and the number 2 is their shared brown.

What the stranger has heard is 23, 5, 8 and 19, and to reach the secret from those the stranger would have to discover either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15, meaning that they would have to answer the question of which power of 5 on this clock gives 8. With a clock this small the stranger could simply try every power in turn, 5 to the power of 1, 5 to the power of 2 and so on, until the 8 turns up, and that would take seconds, which is why our example is only a toy. Real systems use a clock with a size of several hundred digits and private numbers just as large, and then the situation is very different. Raising a number to a huge power on a clock is still quick for a computer, because it can be done by repeated squaring, which needs only a few hundred steps even when the power has hundreds of digits. Going backwards, however, has no known shortcut for a well-chosen clock, and the stranger is left with an effectively endless guessing game, since the number of possible private numbers is greater than the number of atoms in the visible universe and the remainders give no hint about whether a guess is close to the truth.

This lopsidedness between a quick forward calculation and a hopeless backward one is the heart of what is called public-key cryptography, and the name comes from the fact that each person publishes something, the 8 or the 19, that anyone may see, while keeping a matching private number that never leaves their head. A closely related family of methods, of which RSA is the most famous, uses a different one-way trick: multiplying two enormous prime numbers together is easy, while taking the product and finding the two primes that made it is not. In that arrangement a person publishes the product as a lock that anybody may snap shut around a message, and only the holder of the primes can open it, so a stranger can send them a secret without ever having met them. Both approaches let strangers begin from nothing, and in practice they are used just to agree a shared number like our 2, which both sides then feed into a fast ordinary cipher that scrambles the real conversation, so the slow public-key step happens once at the start and everything afterwards is quick.

There is one weakness that the arithmetic by itself does not fix, which is that Alice and Bob must be sure they are really talking to each other. If the stranger were not merely listening but sitting in the middle of the line and could cut it, the stranger could run one exchange with Alice, pretending to be Bob, and a second with Bob, pretending to be Alice, and would then be able to read and pass along everything while each of the two believed they had a private channel. Real systems therefore add a way of proving identity, usually by having a trusted authority vouch that a given public number really belongs to a given person or website, in the same way that a passport office vouches for a photograph, and that is the padlock your browser shows when you visit a bank. With that check in place, two people who have never met can build a secret in full view of everyone, because the only things ever sent were mixtures that nobody can unmix.
