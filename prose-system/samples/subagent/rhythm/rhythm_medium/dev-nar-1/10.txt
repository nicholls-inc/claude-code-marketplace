In the late summer of 1854, a terrible outbreak of cholera struck the crowded Soho district of London, and within a few days hundreds of people living near Broad Street were dead. Cholera is a disease that causes violent diarrhea and vomiting, and it can kill a healthy adult within a day or two by draining the body of fluid. In those years nobody knew what caused it. Most physicians believed in the miasma theory, which held that diseases spread through foul air rising from rotting waste and filthy ground, and the smells of Victorian London gave that idea plenty of apparent support.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked nearby, was not convinced. He had already spent years thinking about cholera, and during an earlier epidemic in 1848 and 1849 he had come to suspect that the illness entered the body through the mouth, not the lungs. His reasoning was simple. Cholera attacked the bowels first, and he thought it unlikely that a poison breathed in from the air would behave that way. He believed instead that people swallowed something from contaminated water or from hands and food touched by the sick, and he had published a short pamphlet saying so. Most of his colleagues had paid it little attention.

When the new outbreak began in the last days of August, Snow set out to test his idea against the facts on the ground. He walked the streets near Broad Street and went from door to door, asking about the dead and about what the households had eaten and drunk. He also obtained the death records from the local registrar, which let him match each victim to an address. What he found was that the deaths were packed tightly around one spot, the public water pump on Broad Street, from which many local families drew their drinking water.

Snow marked the deaths on a map of the neighborhood, drawing a small bar at each house where someone had died. The bars piled up thickest around the pump, and the pattern seemed to him to make the case before he had said a word. Maps of this kind were not entirely new, but Snow used his to argue a specific point, and it has since become one of the most famous maps in the history of public health. He also noticed that the pattern had interesting exceptions, and he understood that these were just as important as the clusters.

Near the pump stood a workhouse, a charitable institution housing several hundred poor residents, and it had remarkably few deaths. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own well on the premises, so its inmates had no need to use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on nearby Broad Street also escaped almost untouched, and its workers were given a ration of malt liquor to drink. Meanwhile, he heard of a woman living far away in Hampstead who had died of cholera after her family sent a cart to fetch water from the Broad Street pump, because she liked its taste. Each of these cases fit his theory better than it fit the idea of bad air.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow went to a meeting of the local board of guardians, the officials who governed the parish, and presented his findings. He asked them to remove the handle of the pump so that nobody could draw water from it. The officials were skeptical, but they agreed, and the handle came off the following day. The popular story says that this ended the epidemic, but the truth is more complicated. The outbreak had already begun to fade by then, since many residents had fled the district and many of the most vulnerable people had already died. Snow himself admitted that the decline could not be attributed to the pump handle alone.

His case did not rest on that gesture anyway. Snow also pursued a second line of evidence, which he had begun before the Soho outbreak and which many historians regard as even stronger. Two water companies supplied the south of London, and one of them had moved its intake upriver to a cleaner stretch of the Thames, while the other still drew water downstream from the sewage of the city. Snow compared the death rates in households served by each company and found that customers of the dirtier supplier died at several times the rate of the others. Because the two companies served mixed neighborhoods, the households were otherwise much alike, which made the comparison close to a natural experiment.

The final piece fell into place with the help of a local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, who had begun as a doubter of Snow's theory and set out to disprove it. Whitehead knew the neighborhood and its families far better than Snow did, and his own inquiries led him to a baby who had fallen ill just before the outbreak, in a house on Broad Street. The cesspool beneath that house, it turned out, lay very close to the well and was leaking into it. Whitehead became convinced, and he helped Snow to see how the poison had reached the water.

The authorities were slow to accept the result, and the miasma theory held on for years. But Snow's careful method, which combined maps, counts, and patient questioning, became a model for the study of disease in populations, and he is now remembered as one of the founders of modern epidemiology. Snow died in 1858, only four years after the epidemic and before most of London had been won over. Within a generation, though, the idea that dirty water carries disease had become the basis of the great sewer and water works that made cities safer to live in.
