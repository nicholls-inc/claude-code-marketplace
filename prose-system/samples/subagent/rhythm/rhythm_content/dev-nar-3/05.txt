On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unseasonably mild, with the temperature near forty degrees after a spell of bitter cold, and the neighborhood's mostly Italian and Irish residents, along with city workers and dockhands, were going about their midday business near the waterfront when a steel tank some fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, gave way at Commercial Street. The tank held about two and a half million gallons of crude molasses, which the company intended to ferment into industrial alcohol, a business that had grown urgent with wartime demand for munitions and that was about to be cut short by Prohibition, and which had been filled to near capacity the day before with a fresh shipment from the Caribbean that was warmer than the cold-soaked tank around it.

Residents had long known the tank was troubled, since it leaked so freely that neighbors collected the brown seepage in pots, and children scooped it up with sticks, while the company, rather than repair it, painted it brown so the stains would not show; the plates had never been properly tested, the rivets were later judged too few and the steel too thin, and the structure had been built quickly by a man with no engineering training. Shortly after half past noon, witnesses heard a low rumbling and a sound some compared to a machine gun, which was the rivets popping, and then the tank burst open and released a wave that, by most accounts, rose between eight and fifteen feet and moved at around thirty-five miles an hour, destroying everything in its path.

The wave tore a section of the elevated railway off its supports, pushed a firehouse off its foundation, and crushed homes and warehouses, and the air it displaced was strong enough to knock people off their feet before the molasses itself reached them. Twenty-one people died, some drowned in the syrup and some crushed by debris, and about 150 were injured, among them firefighters, horses, and children who had been playing in the street, and rescuers found that the cold air quickly turned the substance thick and sticky, so that it clung to the limbs of the trapped and made every step slow and exhausting, and some victims were not found for days. Boston Harbor ran brown well into the summer, and the smell of molasses lingered in the neighborhood for decades, particularly on hot days.

What followed became one of the earliest major class action suits in American history, as survivors and families of the dead sued the company, which insisted that anarchists had planted a bomb, a claim consistent with the fears of the period; but an auditor named Hugh Ogden spent years hearing about three thousand witnesses and concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, and in 1925 the company was ordered to pay damages of roughly six hundred thousand dollars, most of it eventually going to families as individual payments of about seven thousand dollars for each death. The disaster also helped bring about stricter building regulations in Massachusetts, which began requiring that architects and engineers sign off on plans, and it stands today as a reminder that corporate negligence can turn something as ordinary as a sweetener into a lethal flood.
