The Day Boston Drowned in Molasses

On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unusually mild for winter. Along Commercial Street, lunchtime traffic moved past horse-drawn wagons and dock workers. Looming over the street was a steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company, about fifty feet tall and filled with roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The syrup was bound for a distillery, where it would become alcohol for industrial use and, in wartime, for explosives.

The tank had problems. It had been built quickly, its riveted seams were thin, and it leaked often enough that it was reportedly painted brown to hide the stains. Neighbors complained of sticky puddles and a sweet smell in the air. Warnings went unheeded, and the structure had never been properly tested.

Shortly after 12:30 p.m., a deep rumble sounded. Then the rivets gave way, and the steel split open with a noise witnesses likened to gunfire. The molasses burst out as a wave estimated at 15 to 25 feet high, moving at perhaps 35 miles per hour. It flattened buildings, twisted the supports of the elevated railway overhead, crushed a firehouse, and swept horses, wagons, and people off the street.

Men and women were pinned under debris or caught in a thick, sticky flood far too viscous to swim through. Rescuers waded into the goo, which dragged at their boots and clothing. Some tried to climb poles and lampposts to escape. Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Several horses were killed.

Cleanup took weeks. Crews used sand, seawater, and shovels to scrape the residue from streets and building fronts, and the sweet smell lingered in the neighborhood for years. Children were reportedly seen licking molasses from the cobblestones before the work was done.

The fallout was legal as much as physical. Lawsuits argued that the company had been negligent, and in 1925 a Massachusetts court held the successor firm, United States Industrial Alcohol, liable for the damage. The verdict was an unusual case of a corporation answering for a disaster of its own making, and it encouraged engineers and regulators to take structural safety more seriously.

Today the disaster survives in local lore, in a plaque, and in the phrase "molasses flood," a reminder that an ordinary substance can become deadly when its dangers are ignored.
