Every city that still requires a minimum number of parking spaces for each new apartment, shop, or restaurant should repeal those rules. The case is not that parking is bad or that cars should disappear. It is that a city government has no business deciding, lot by lot and use by use, how much parking a private building must contain, especially when the people making that decision have no money at stake and the people paying for it never get a vote.

Consider where the numbers come from. Most minimum parking ratios trace back to a handbook published by traffic engineers, which compiled counts of how many cars showed up at suburban sites that already offered free parking, often measured at peak hours in places with few alternatives. Cities then turned those observations into law, requiring, say, two spaces per apartment or one for every 200 square feet of retail. The sample was small, the sites were unrepresentative, and the logic was circular: free parking attracts drivers, so planners concluded that free parking was needed. A rule built on that foundation has no claim to scientific authority.

The cost of the rule is real, and it is hidden in everything else. A surface space takes roughly three hundred square feet once you count the drive aisles, and structured or underground parking can cost tens of thousands of dollars per space to build. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, home prices, and the price of a sandwich. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the garage, because the developer had to build it to win approval. Economists who have studied this call it a bundling problem, and it works like a tax that falls hardest on the poor, who are the most likely to go without a car and the least able to absorb an extra few hundred dollars a month in housing costs.

Parking mandates also quietly decide what gets built at all. A small lot downtown might comfortably hold a six-unit building, but once you subtract the required parking, only three units pencil out, or none. Older neighborhoods that people love, with corner stores, narrow lots, and apartments over shops, are frequently illegal to rebuild under current rules, because they could never satisfy today's ratios. We have written our own best urban places out of the law. Meanwhile the land that does get developed fills up with asphalt, spreading destinations apart and making it harder to walk, bike, or run a viable bus line, which in turn makes people feel they need the car and the parking the rule demanded. The mandate manufactures its own justification.

The standard objection is that without requirements, new buildings will flood the street with cars and make parking impossible for everyone. This deserves a serious answer, and the answer is that it mostly confuses a pricing problem with a supply problem. Street parking feels scarce because it is usually free or nearly free, and anything free is overused. The fix is to price curb space sensibly, through meters, residential permits, or performance-based rates, so that a few spaces always stay open. Cities that have done this, such as San Francisco with its demand-responsive meters, found that cruising for spots fell and the sky did not fall.

A second objection is that developers will build too little parking and the market will fail. But lenders, tenants, and developers all care intensely about whether a building can attract customers. Where parking is valuable, builders will provide it. Removing the mandate does not ban parking; it lets the amount be set by demand instead of a formula. Plenty of developers in suburban and car-oriented areas will keep building generous lots because their tenants want them. The difference is that a building near a train station, where half the residents do not own cars, will no longer be forced to waste money and land pretending otherwise.

There is evidence that this works. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing list of other places have followed. Early results in Buffalo showed developers building less parking than the old rules demanded in some projects, and more than the old rules demanded in others, which is exactly what you would expect if the old number was arbitrary. Meanwhile, no wave of catastrophe has followed. Cities that act get cheaper projects, more flexible sites, and the option to turn leftover parking land into housing, shops, and public space.

Skeptics are right about one thing: removing mandates will not fix housing affordability or traffic by itself. Zoning, permitting delays, and construction costs matter enormously. But this is among the cheapest reforms available, because it requires the city to spend nothing and to stop doing something. It lets people who actually pay for buildings, and who actually live in them, weigh the tradeoffs themselves. A city that trusts residents to decide how many shoes to own, how many rooms to rent, and which neighborhood to live in can trust them, and the people who build for them, to decide how many parking spaces to provide. Repeal the minimums, price the curb, and let the market and the street teach us what the right number actually is.
