Park the Mandate: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will see the same thing. Vast fields of asphalt sit empty beside strip malls, apartment blocks and offices. They were not built because anyone wanted them. They were built because a rule said so. Minimum parking requirements tell developers how many spaces they must provide for every apartment, restaurant seat or square foot of retail. Cities should abolish them, and the sooner the better.

Start with how these rules came to exist. Most were not the product of careful study. Planners in the mid twentieth century copied numbers from one another, leaning on a handbook of parking generation rates that was based on a small number of suburban sites with free parking and little transit. Those figures hardened into law. A city that wanted fewer spaces had no way to say so, because the code demanded more. Nobody ever voted on the idea that a bowling alley needs a set number of spaces per lane. It just became the default.

The deeper problem is that the rules hide a cost. Parking is not free. A surface space takes up land that could hold housing or shops. A space in a structure can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground space can cost far more. Developers do not absorb that cost out of generosity. They pass it on to tenants and buyers, who pay for parking whether they own a car or not. Research on mandated parking has repeatedly found that it raises rents and cuts the number of homes that get built. Households without cars, who tend to be poorer, end up subsidizing the storage of other people's vehicles. A rule that sounds neutral turns out to be a quiet tax on the people least able to bear it.

Housing is where the damage shows most. Cities across the country are short of homes, and prices reflect it. Yet in many places a small apartment building cannot be built because the required parking will not fit on the lot. Small sites go undeveloped. Older buildings that were erected before the rules existed could not legally be built today. Beautiful, walkable neighborhoods, the ones people pay a premium to live in, are often illegal to replicate. If we want more homes near jobs and transit, we cannot keep demanding that each one come with a garage.

Defenders of the mandates raise a fair worry. If cities stop requiring parking, will streets fill with cars and neighbors fight over spaces? It is a real concern, but it points to the wrong fix. The answer to scarce curb space is to manage the curb, not to force every builder to oversupply private lots. Cities can price street parking so that a space or two is always open, and they can use the revenue to improve the neighborhood. Residential permit programs can protect existing residents. These tools target the actual problem directly, and they do so without inflating the price of every new building.

It also helps to be precise about what repeal means. Abolishing minimums does not ban parking. It simply returns the decision to the people who know the most about it. A developer building near a train station might decide that few spaces are needed. A suburban grocery store might still build a large lot, because its customers drive. The market is perfectly capable of supplying parking where parking has value. What it cannot do is respond to the reality of a specific site when a one size rule overrides it. Lenders, who want projects to succeed, will still insist on sensible amounts of parking. Reform opens up choice. It does not impose a car free future.

The evidence from places that have tried it is encouraging. Cities that have removed their requirements, from Buffalo to Minneapolis and from Auckland to parts of California, have seen developers build less parking than the old rules demanded, often on projects that would not have been feasible otherwise. The sky has not fallen. Buildings still get built with parking where it makes sense, and some now include more homes or more ground floor shops in its place. Where reform has been tried, the common result is more flexibility and more housing, not chaos.

Climate and public health point the same way. Abundant free parking is a subsidy for driving. It spreads destinations apart, makes walking unpleasant and makes transit harder to run. Every extra space built encourages one more trip by car. A city serious about emissions, traffic deaths and clean air cannot sensibly keep a law that guarantees car storage everywhere. Removing the mandate will not end driving, but it will stop the code from tilting the field.

There is also a plain argument from fairness and freedom. A property owner who wants to build an apartment building with a modest number of spaces, for tenants who do not own cars, should not need to beg permission or buy a variance. The burden should fall on the rule, not on the builder. When a regulation raises costs, blocks homes and rests on shaky numbers, the sensible response is to question whether it should exist at all.

Some cities will worry about moving too fast. They can phase in reform, starting with areas near transit or downtown districts, and expand from there. But half measures should be understood as steps toward a goal, not as the goal. The destination is a planning code that no longer tells people how many cars they must house.

We spent decades building cities around the assumption that everything must come with a parking lot. The result is places that are expensive, spread out and dependent on one mode of travel. Repealing minimum parking requirements will not fix all of that, but it is one of the cheapest and most direct reforms on offer. It costs the public treasury nothing. It frees land, lowers costs and lets neighborhoods grow according to what people actually need. Cities should stop mandating parking and start trusting their residents to decide.
