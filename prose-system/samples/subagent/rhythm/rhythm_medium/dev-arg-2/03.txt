Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, at least in the form most of them practice today. The usual estimate is a guess made at the moment we know the least, dressed up as a commitment and then used to judge people when reality turns out differently. We have decades of evidence that these guesses miss, and the misses are not random noise that better technique will remove. Most real work in software is discovery. If you already knew exactly how to build something, you would have reused it or automated it, so the interesting work is by definition the part you cannot yet size.

The defenders of estimation usually say that the number is not the point, that the conversation is what matters. That may be true, but then we should have the conversation without the number. Talking through what could go wrong, what is unclear, and what could be cut is valuable, and none of it requires someone to write down eight points or three weeks. Once a figure exists, it escapes the room where it was born. It lands in a roadmap, a quarterly plan, or a promise to a customer, and nobody remembers the caveats that were spoken alongside it.

Estimation also carries a real cost that rarely gets counted. Teams spend hours each sprint debating whether a ticket is a three or a five, and those hours produce a forecast that is barely better than simply counting finished tickets. Studies of throughput in many teams show that the number of items completed per week is a steadier predictor than any sum of estimated points. If a cheap measurement beats an expensive ritual, the ritual should go.

Worse, estimates change behavior in ways that hurt the product. Engineers who know they will be held to a number pad it, cut corners to hit it, or quietly stop raising problems that would blow the schedule. Managers learn to negotiate the number down, and the cycle repeats. The deadline stops being information and becomes a source of pressure.

None of this means abandoning planning, and that is the objection managers will raise first. Businesses do have dates that matter, such as a launch tied to a conference or a contract with a regulatory cutoff. The better response is to fix the date and flex the scope. Break work into small slices that ship independently, order them by value, and cut from the bottom when time runs short. Track how many slices finish each week, and use that history to say honestly what is likely by a given date. This is forecasting from evidence rather than prophecy from opinion.

It will feel uncomfortable at first, because an estimate gives managers a feeling of control. But that feeling is mostly an illusion, and it is paid for with engineers' time and trust. A team that ships small pieces continuously, shows its progress openly, and talks plainly about uncertainty gives its leaders something better than a number. It gives them the truth, early enough to act on. Stop estimating, start slicing, and measure what actually happens.
