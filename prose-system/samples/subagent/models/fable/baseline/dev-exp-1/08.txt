How a heat pump heats your house when it is colder outside

A gas boiler makes heat by burning fuel. A heat pump does something different: it moves heat that already exists from outside your house to inside. That difference explains both why it works in winter and why it can be cheaper to run.

There is heat in cold air

Air at 0°C feels cold to us, but in physical terms it still holds a great deal of heat energy. Heat only runs out at absolute zero, which is −273°C. Winter air is, in that sense, about 90% as "warm" as summer air. The challenge is not finding heat outdoors; it is persuading it to flow into a warmer building, since heat on its own only flows from hot to cold.

The trick: make something colder than the outdoors

A heat pump solves this with a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, circulating in a sealed loop. The loop has four stages:

1. Absorb. In the outdoor unit, the refrigerant is a liquid at around −10°C or lower. Because the outdoor air is warmer than that, even on a freezing day, heat flows naturally from the air into the refrigerant, which boils into a gas. A fan pulls plenty of air across the coil to keep this going.

2. Compress. An electric compressor squeezes the gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature, as you notice when a bicycle pump gets warm. The refrigerant leaves the compressor at 50°C or more.

3. Release. This hot gas passes through a heat exchanger connected to your heating water. It is now hotter than the water, so heat flows into it and on to your radiators or underfloor pipes. As it gives up heat, the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid.

4. Expand. The liquid passes through a valve that drops its pressure, which makes it very cold again, ready to absorb more heat outdoors.

Your fridge does exactly this in reverse: it pulls heat out of a cold box and dumps it into your warmer kitchen. A heat pump treats the outdoors as the inside of the fridge and your house as the warm coil at the back.

Why this matters for your bills

The electricity you pay for runs the compressor and the fan; it does not create the heat. For each unit of electricity, a typical modern heat pump delivers around three units of heat over a heating season, with the other two taken from the outside air. A gas boiler delivers at best about 0.9 units of heat per unit of gas.

Whether that saves you money depends on your local prices. Electricity usually costs several times more per unit than gas, so a heat pump delivering three to one may come out roughly level with a boiler, and better if you have a heat pump tariff or solar panels. Check your own unit rates before deciding.

What changes in cold weather

The colder it is outside, the harder the compressor has to work to lift the heat to a useful temperature, so efficiency drops. On a very cold day you might get two units of heat per unit of electricity instead of three or four. A correctly sized system still keeps the house warm; heat pumps are widely used in Norway, Sweden and Finland. The outdoor unit will also occasionally run a short defrost cycle to clear ice from its coil, which is normal.

What it means for your house

Heat pumps are most efficient when they produce water at 35–50°C, cooler than the 60–75°C a boiler typically sends out. To deliver the same warmth with cooler water, you may need larger radiators in some rooms, and good insulation helps. The system also runs differently: long, steady, low-level heating instead of short hot bursts. You will need space for an outdoor unit and, usually, a hot water cylinder.

The key thing to get right is the installer's heat loss survey. A properly sized system with suitable radiators is what determines whether you get the comfort and the running costs you are hoping for.
