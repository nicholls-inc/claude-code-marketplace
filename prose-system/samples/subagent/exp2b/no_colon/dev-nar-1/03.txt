In late August 1854, a terrible sickness fell upon Soho, a crowded district of London. The disease was cholera, which causes violent vomiting and diarrhea so severe that victims can lose most of the water in their bodies within hours. Their skin turns bluish and wrinkled, and many die within a day or two. Within ten days of the first cases in the streets around Broad Street, more than five hundred people were dead. Families fled. Shops closed. The neighborhood fell silent.

At the time, nearly everyone of authority believed cholera spread through "miasma," meaning foul air rising from rotting waste, swamps, and filth. The theory was reasonable on its face. Cholera thrived in the stinking, overcrowded slums, and the smell of London was famously dreadful. Doctors prescribed ways to avoid bad air and local officials burned tar in the streets to purify it.

One physician disagreed. John Snow, a thirty-nine-year-old doctor who lived nearby, had been pondering cholera for years. He was already known in medicine for his work on anesthesia, having given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. But cholera nagged at him. Snow noticed that the illness began with vomiting and bowel trouble, not with breathing problems. If bad air were the culprit, he reasoned, why did the sickness strike the gut first? To him it made more sense that people were swallowing something. He suspected a poison in food or water that multiplied inside the body and passed out of it, ready to spread again.

He had tested this idea during an earlier outbreak in 1849, and now he had a chance to prove it. Living only a short walk from the afflicted streets, Snow began to investigate. He did not stay at his desk. He walked the neighborhood and knocked on doors, asking who had fallen ill, when, and where the families got their drinking water.

In those days London had no water pipes in people's homes. Residents drew water from public pumps, and in the area around Broad Street, the pump on that street was popular. Its water was cool and clear, and many believed it tasted better than that of other pumps nearby. Snow suspected it was the source of the trouble.

To make sense of the deaths, he did something that seems simple today but was striking then. He obtained a list of the dead from the local registrar, and he marked each death on a map of the neighborhood, one small bar for each victim at each address. The pattern jumped off the page. The marks clustered thickly around the Broad Street pump, and thinned out with distance from it.

Clustering alone could be argued away, so Snow chased down the exceptions, and they proved the most persuasive evidence of all. Near the pump stood a workhouse housing some five hundred people, yet only a handful had died. Snow discovered the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street had almost no cases either. Its workers drank the beer they brewed, and the company had its own well, too. Then there was a widow who lived far from Soho in Hampstead. She died of cholera after her family sent a cart to fetch water from the Broad Street pump, because she had grown fond of its taste. A niece who drank the same water also fell ill. Few others in her area did.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow took his findings to the local board of guardians. He urged them to act. The next day, they ordered the handle removed from the Broad Street pump, so that no one could draw water from it. Residents had to go elsewhere.

The outbreak was already fading by then, since many people had fled and the worst was over. Skeptics have long pointed out that removing the handle may not have ended it. But Snow did not stop with the handle. He kept working through the following months, and the outbreak gave him more to examine.

Help came from a local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who had doubted Snow's theory and set out to disprove it. Whitehead knew his neighbors and discovered what Snow could not. The first victim, a baby girl, had fallen ill just before the outbreak. Her mother had washed the child's soiled diapers and thrown the water into a cesspool, which lay only feet from the pump's well. Inspection showed the brickwork lining the well had decayed, allowing waste to seep in. Whitehead, once a skeptic, became a convert.

Snow's ideas did not win over officials immediately. A government inquiry rejected his theory. Yet over time, the evidence gathered and the case grew stronger. Decades later, scientists identified the bacterium that causes cholera and confirmed what Snow had seen. John Snow died in 1858, long before his vindication. Today he is remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the study of how diseases spread through populations, and his map remains a classic example of how patient observation can reveal what theory conceals.
