The Boston molasses flood of 1919 began on a mild winter day in the city's North End. On January 15, the temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees after a stretch of bitter cold. Around half past noon, workers were eating lunch, children were playing near the waterfront, and horses and wagons filled the streets.

Looming over the neighborhood stood a steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company. It rose about fifty feet and measured ninety feet across. It had been built in 1915 to hold molasses, which was fermented into industrial alcohol used in munitions. On that day it held more than two million gallons. The tank had always seemed unwell. It groaned, and it leaked so steadily that neighborhood children collected the syrup in cups. Rather than repair it, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. It was never properly tested by being filled with water first.

Residents later described a low rumble and a sound like machine gun fire as the rivets sheared loose. Then the tank gave way. A wave of molasses about twenty five feet high surged through the streets at roughly thirty five miles an hour. It was heavy, sticky and cold enough to thicken as it moved. It tore buildings from their foundations and lifted a freight car off its tracks. The supports of the elevated railway buckled. A firehouse was crushed, trapping men inside. Victims found themselves caught in a syrup that grew harder to move through every second. Rescuers could barely wade in to reach them.

In the end, twenty one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Some of the dead were children. Others were laborers, teamsters and firefighters going about an ordinary afternoon. The molasses was so thick that recovering the bodies took days.

Sailors from a navy ship in the harbor joined police and the Red Cross in the rescue. Crews sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats and covered them in sand and sawdust. The cleanup lasted weeks. Molasses clung to everything it touched, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer. For decades afterward, locals insisted that on hot days the North End still smelled faintly sweet.

The company had a ready explanation. Its leadership claimed that anarchists had bombed the tank, a believable story in a nervous year of labor unrest. Survivors and their families sued, and the case became one of the earliest major class action suits in Massachusetts. An auditor appointed by the court spent years hearing testimony from expert witnesses. Engineers showed that the steel was too thin and that the design had been poor. There had been little oversight to catch any of it. In 1925 the auditor ruled the tank had failed because of structural weakness, not sabotage. The company paid out settlements to the victims' families.

The disaster had a lasting effect on how America builds. It helped push states toward requiring that engineers and architects sign off on their plans, and it contributed to the modern idea that public safety depends on tested standards and honest inspection.
