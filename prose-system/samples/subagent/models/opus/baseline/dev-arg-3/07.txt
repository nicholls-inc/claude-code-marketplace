Why Children Should Still Learn to Join Their Letters

Every few years someone declares handwriting dead. The typewriter was meant to finish it off, then the word processor, then the smartphone, and now the voice assistant and the chatbot. Each time, the same question reaches primary schools: why spend hours of a crowded timetable teaching seven-year-olds to join their letters when they will spend their adult lives tapping on glass? The question is fair, and the people who ask it are usually not cranks. Finland made cursive optional in 2016 so that schools could put more effort into keyboard skills. The American Common Core standards, published in 2010, left cursive out altogether. Plenty of teachers in Britain would quietly drop it tomorrow if the curriculum let them.

I think they would be wrong. Primary schools should go on teaching joined-up handwriting. My reason is not nostalgia for fountain pens and copybooks. Joining is the most practical route to the thing children actually need, which is a hand fast and automatic enough that writing stops being a physical chore and becomes a way of thinking. The case against it rests on a misunderstanding of what joined handwriting is for.

What the skill is for

Start with a distinction that often gets lost. When people picture "joined-up handwriting" they tend to imagine looped copperplate, with elaborate capitals and every letter tied to the next. That is not what most primary schools teach, and it is not what anyone should defend. The English national curriculum asks children to learn which letters are best left unjoined and to develop a legible, fluent, personal style by the end of primary school. The aim is a working hand.

That aim matters because handwriting is a bottleneck. A young child writing a story has to do several hard things at once: hold an idea, find the words, spell them, punctuate the sentence, and physically form each letter. Working memory is small at that age. If letter formation still takes conscious effort, it eats into the attention available for everything else. Researchers who study children's writing have made this point for decades. Virginia Berninger, Steve Graham and their colleagues found that handwriting fluency in the early years predicts the quantity and quality of what children compose, and that extra handwriting instruction for struggling writers can improve their composition as well as their neatness. The hand that moves without thought frees the mind to write.

Joining helps produce that kind of hand. When letters are formed as separate units, each one is a fresh start: lift the pencil, find the next starting point, begin again. Joining turns common letter sequences into single movements. A child who has practised "th", "ing" and "ed" as connected strokes can produce them almost as one gesture, the way a pianist plays a familiar chord. This is why fluent adult handwriting is nearly always partly joined, whatever the writer was taught. Studies of handwriting speed, including work by Graham, Weintraub and Berninger in the late 1990s, found that the fastest writers tended to use a mixed style, joining some letters and not others. That finding is sometimes quoted against cursive teaching. It actually supports teaching children to join, because the mixed style of a fluent adult is what joining naturally decays into. You cannot settle into a sensible half-joined hand if you were never taught how letters connect in the first place.

The brain and the pen

The second argument concerns learning more broadly. A growing body of research suggests that forming letters by hand does something for young children that typing and tracing do not. In a well-known set of studies, the psychologist Karin James at Indiana University scanned the brains of pre-literate children after they had learned letters in different ways. Children who had practised writing the letters freehand showed more activity in brain regions associated with reading when they later looked at those letters than children who had typed or traced them. The likely explanation is that producing a letter by hand, with all its small variations, forces the child to work out what makes an "a" an "a". The motor act teaches the visual category.

Other researchers, including Audrey van der Meer and Ruud van der Weel in Norway, have used EEG to compare handwriting with typing and found broader patterns of brain connectivity during handwriting, which they link to memory and learning. None of this research is conclusive, and enthusiasts sometimes stretch it too far. The famous 2014 study by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer, which found that students who took notes by hand understood lectures better than those who used laptops, has had a mixed record when others tried to replicate it. Even so, the overall direction of the evidence is fairly consistent: writing by hand is a richer cognitive activity than pressing keys, especially for young learners. Strictly speaking, that is an argument for handwriting in general rather than for joining in particular. But schools that abandon joined handwriting rarely replace it with more careful print practice. In practice, dropping cursive tends to signal that handwriting as a whole is no longer a priority, and the time goes elsewhere.

Spelling and the shape of words

There is also a more specific benefit. Joining encourages children to treat a word as a unit with its own shape and movement rather than as a row of separate characters. Many teachers report that once children join common letter strings, those strings stick in their spelling. The hand remembers "ough" and "tion" as patterns. This is the reasoning behind the long-standing advice from many specialist dyslexia teachers that a continuous cursive style can help children who struggle with spelling and with reversing letters such as b and d. In a joined script, every letter begins on the line and flows into the next, so there is less room for a letter to face the wrong way. The evidence for this is more practitioner experience than controlled trial, and I would not claim that joining cures dyslexia. But it is striking that the people who work most closely with children who find writing hard are often among cursive's strongest defenders, not its critics.

Answering the objections

The strongest objection is time. The primary curriculum is crowded, and every hour spent on letter joins is an hour not spent on reading, maths or, critics say, keyboarding. I take this seriously, but it overstates the cost. Handwriting practice in a good primary school is a matter of short, frequent sessions, ten or fifteen minutes a few times a week in the early years, tapering off as the skill becomes automatic. The payoff lasts for every handwritten task a child does for the rest of their schooling. And children still sit most of their exams by hand. In England, GCSE and A level papers are overwhelmingly handwritten, often for two hours or more at a stretch. A pupil whose writing is slow or tiring is at a measurable disadvantage in exactly the moments that decide their future. Teaching a fluent joined hand at seven is a cheap way to protect them at sixteen.

A second objection is that joining is hard for some children, particularly those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties, and that forcing it on them causes misery. This is true, and the answer is flexibility rather than abolition. Good teachers already allow children who cannot manage a joined style to use a clear print, and assessment frameworks have moved in that direction too. A sensible policy teaches joining as the default and makes reasonable exceptions. A policy built around the exceptions would deny the majority of children a skill most of them can learn without difficulty.

A third objection is that keyboards and voice input make the whole thing obsolete. But the choice is not handwriting or keyboarding. Children should learn both, and the keyboard is easier to pick up later; plenty of adults learn to touch-type in a few weeks. Fine motor control and the link between hand and letter are much harder to build after the window of early childhood has passed. Nor has handwriting disappeared from adult life. People still write notes in meetings, fill in forms, annotate drafts, sign documents and write cards. A legible, quick hand is a small but real form of independence.

Finally, there is the argument from reading. A generation that is never taught joined writing may struggle to read it. That sounds trivial until you remember how much of the written record is in cursive: letters from grandparents, historical documents, a teacher's comment on an essay, the handwritten note from a doctor or a colleague. Some American states, including California, have reversed course and restored cursive to their curricula, partly for this reason. A literate person should be able to read the handwriting of other people.

What good teaching looks like

Defending joined handwriting does not mean defending the worst of how it used to be taught. Rows of identical loops copied in silence, children marked down for an untidy descender, left-handers forced to write with their right hand: none of that needs to come back. The best current practice teaches letter formation carefully in Reception and Year 1, introduces joins once the letters themselves are secure, focuses on comfort and speed rather than ornament, and encourages older children to develop their own legible style. That approach costs little and produces children who can write quickly and clearly without thinking about it.

Conclusion

The case for joined handwriting is not sentimental. It is about fluency, and fluency is about freeing children's minds for the harder work of composing, spelling and thinking. Joining is how most people get from the laborious printing of a five-year-old to the quick, practical hand of a confident writer. The evidence on handwriting and learning points in the same direction, and exams remain stubbornly handwritten. Children will learn to type whatever schools do. Few of them will learn to write well by hand unless someone teaches them, and that job belongs to the primary school. Schools should keep doing it.
