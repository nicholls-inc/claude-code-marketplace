Apollo 13: The Successful Failure

On the evening of April 13, 1970, the three men aboard Apollo 13 had every reason to feel relaxed. They were nearly 56 hours into a mission to the Moon and about 200,000 miles from home. Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier spaceflights, was set to become the fifth person to walk on the lunar surface. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, would go with him. Jack Swigert would stay in orbit aboard the command module. Swigert had joined the crew only three days before launch, after the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, was exposed to German measles and grounded as a precaution. Mattingly never caught the disease.

The flight had gone smoothly enough that the public had mostly stopped watching. The crew had just finished a television broadcast showing life aboard the spacecraft, and none of the major networks had aired it live. Shortly after the cameras went off, Mission Control in Houston asked Swigert to do a routine chore: turn on the fans that stirred the supercooled oxygen in the service module's tanks.

He flipped the switch. A few seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights came on. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." When the ground asked him to repeat, Lovell said it again: "Houston, we've had a problem."

The problem at first looked like an instrument fault. Readings jumped around, and one of the spacecraft's two main electrical circuits had lost voltage. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming away from the ship. "We are venting something out into space," he reported. The gauges explained it. Oxygen tank 2 read empty, and oxygen tank 1 was slowly losing pressure.

That oxygen did more than supply air. It fed the three fuel cells that generated the command module's electricity and water. Within a couple of hours two of the fuel cells were dead and the third was failing. The command module, named Odyssey, was dying. It had batteries, but they were meant for the last hours of the flight, when the crew came back through the atmosphere. If those batteries ran down now, the men would have no way to get home.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his team worked through what was left. Fairly quickly the answer became clear: the crew would have to abandon Odyssey and move into the lunar module, Aquarius, which was still attached to the nose of the spacecraft. Aquarius had its own oxygen, water, batteries and engine. It had been built to carry two men down to the Moon's surface and back up again over about two days. Now it would have to keep three men alive for about four.

The crew powered Odyssey down to save its batteries for reentry, and Lovell and Haise brought Aquarius to life. Swigert copied the command module's navigation data over before shutting it off. The landing was cancelled. The only goal now was getting home.

The first question was which way to go. Turning around directly would have needed the large engine in the service module, and nobody knew how badly that part of the ship was damaged. If the engine exploded, the crew would die. Flight controllers instead chose to send the spacecraft around the Moon and let lunar gravity sling it back toward Earth. Apollo 13 had already left its "free-return" path, a course that would bring it home without a major engine burn, so the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine to get back on it. On April 15 they swung around the far side of the Moon. At their farthest point they were about 248,000 miles from Earth, which is still the farthest any humans have travelled. Haise and Swigert took photographs of the cratered surface below. Lovell, who had orbited the Moon on Apollo 8, knew he would not get another chance to land there.

About two hours after passing behind the Moon, the crew fired the descent engine again. This burn sped up their return by roughly ten hours and moved the splashdown point to the Pacific Ocean, where the Navy's recovery ships were waiting.

Then a slower danger appeared. Every breath the astronauts exhaled added carbon dioxide to the cabin. The lunar module cleaned the air with canisters of lithium hydroxide, but it carried only enough for two men over two days. The command module had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. As carbon dioxide levels rose toward dangerous levels, engineers on the ground worked out a fix using only items the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard covers torn from the flight manuals, a length of hose, and plenty of duct tape. Mission Control read the instructions up step by step, and the crew built the improvised adapter, which the astronauts called the "mailbox." It worked, and the carbon dioxide readings began to drop.

Living conditions on board were miserable. To save power, almost everything was switched off, including the heaters. The temperature inside fell to about 38 degrees Fahrenheit, and water condensed on the walls and windows. Drinking water was rationed to keep the lunar module's equipment cooled. The men barely slept. Haise developed a urinary tract infection and ran a fever. By the end of the flight the three had lost a combined 31 pounds.

Navigation was its own headache. The crew normally checked their position by sighting stars through a telescope, but the cloud of debris drifting with the spacecraft made stars impossible to pick out. For one critical course correction they steered by hand, keeping the edge of Earth's shadow lined up in a window while Swigert timed the engine firing. The burn put them on target.

Back in Houston, engineers including John Aaron and Ken Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, worked out how to restart the cold, dormant command module using its limited batteries. They spent long hours in simulators testing a startup procedure that had never been attempted in flight. Nobody knew whether Odyssey's electronics would survive the freezing temperatures and moisture.

On April 17, about four and a half hours before reaching Earth, the crew released the damaged service module. As it drifted away they got their first look at it. An entire side panel had been blown out. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. The crew then climbed into Odyssey, powered it up, and found that it worked. About an hour before reentry they let go of Aquarius, the lifeboat that had kept them alive. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," the ground radioed.

Reentry always caused a radio blackout of a few minutes while superheated air surrounded the capsule. This time the silence went on noticeably longer than expected, and Mission Control and a worldwide television audience waited. Finally Swigert's voice came through. Minutes later three main parachutes opened, and Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa. Helicopters carried the crew to the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima.

An investigation later traced the explosion to a chain of small errors made years earlier. Oxygen tank 2 had been dropped slightly at the factory, and after a test before launch, technicians used its internal heaters to boil off the leftover oxygen. A thermostatic switch in the tank had been designed for the spacecraft's 28-volt power and had never been upgraded to handle the 65 volts used on the ground. The switch welded shut, the heaters ran for hours, and temperatures inside the tank rose high enough to burn away the insulation on the wiring. When Swigert switched on the fans in space, a spark from the damaged wires started a fire that ruptured the tank. NASA redesigned the tanks and added an extra oxygen supply and a backup battery before Apollo flew again.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, but people often call it NASA's "successful failure." It showed that a crew and a ground team could improvise their way out of a disaster no one had planned for, using a lunar module never designed for the job, a few rolls of tape, and a great deal of careful arithmetic.
