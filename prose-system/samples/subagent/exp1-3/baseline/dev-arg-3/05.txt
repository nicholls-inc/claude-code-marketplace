**Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years the argument comes round again. Children type, tablets are cheap, and the cursive alphabet pinned above the whiteboard looks like a relic of inkwells and blotting paper. Several American states dropped cursive from their standards after 2010, and Finland moved its schools toward keyboards in 2016. In England, teachers still spend scarce hours on looped ascenders and exit strokes, and it is fair to ask why. If a nine-year-old will do most of her adult writing on a screen, what is the point of teaching her to join her letters?

I think the point survives the question. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and teach it deliberately rather than leaving it to chance. The case does not rest on nostalgia, tradition or the look of a tidy page. It rests on three practical claims. Handwriting is still a daily working tool for children. Fluent joined writing serves that tool better than print alone. And the alternatives on offer are weaker than their advocates admit.

**Handwriting is not obsolete in the classroom**

Begin with where children actually write. In most primary classrooms, the great majority of independent writing is still done by hand: stories, science notes, maths workings, spellings, comprehension answers. The device-in-every-lap school is a minority picture, and even where tablets are plentiful, young children rarely have the keyboard skills to type faster or more accurately than they write. Touch-typing takes sustained practice, and most primary curricula do not provide it.

Written exams make the point sharper. Public examinations in the UK, and in most countries, are still handwritten. A pupil who writes slowly or illegibly carries a penalty into every one of them. Examiners cannot mark what they cannot read, and studies of marking have repeatedly found that neat scripts tend to receive more generous marks than untidy ones with identical content. That is an unfair bias, but it is a real one, and children cannot opt out of it. Handwriting fluency is therefore a matter of access, and schools that neglect it hand an avoidable disadvantage to the pupils who can least afford it.

**What joining actually does**

The sceptic will concede that children must write by hand but ask why the writing must be joined. Print-script, the separate letters taught in reception, is perfectly legible. Why not stop there?

The honest answer is that the evidence for cursive over print is more modest than its fans claim, and I will not pretend otherwise. There is no strong proof that joined writing makes children cleverer, and the grander claims about brain development outrun the research. But the practical case is solid. Joined writing is faster. When letters flow into one another, the pen leaves the page less often, and each lift costs time and interrupts the rhythm of the hand. Studies of handwriting speed in the upper primary years generally find that children who join fluently write at least as quickly as those who print, and often faster, with fewer reversals and letter-formation errors. Once a child has to write for an hour in an exam, that speed is a real advantage.

Joining also helps with spelling, in a way that is easy to overlook. A joined word is written as a single motor pattern. The hand learns the movement of "-ing", "-tion" or "-ough" as a unit, and that muscle memory reinforces the visual memory of the spelling. Teachers of children with dyslexia have long used cursive for this reason. Because every letter begins on the baseline and moves forward, joined writing also reduces the b/d and p/q reversals that trouble many young writers. It makes whole-word shapes more distinctive, which is exactly what a struggling speller needs.

Finally, joined writing is where most adult handwriting ends up anyway. People who are never taught to join tend to invent their own half-joined hybrid in adolescence, often an ugly one, and often illegible at speed. The choice schools face is not between cursive and some neutral alternative. It is between a taught, consistent style and a set of improvised ones.

**The research on hand and mind**

There is another strand of evidence, and I will treat it with some care. Several studies, notably work by Anne Mangen and by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer, suggest that handwriting engages memory and comprehension differently from typing. Students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those who typed, apparently because the slower pace forced them to summarise rather than transcribe. Neuroimaging work with young children has found that writing letters by hand activates reading-related brain regions more strongly than typing or tracing them.

These findings are suggestive rather than decisive. Some replications of the note-taking result have been weaker than the original, and most of the imaging studies concern handwriting in general, not joined handwriting in particular. I am not claiming that cursive specifically unlocks the brain. The claim is narrower. The research that exists gives us no reason to think that moving children away from handwriting is harmless, and some reason to think it carries costs. When the evidence is mixed and the downside is a loss of a foundational skill, the sensible default is to keep teaching it.

**Answering the objections**

The strongest objection is about time. The primary day is crowded. Every minute spent on loops and joins is a minute not spent on reading, number or science. This is a serious point, but it overstates the cost. Good handwriting teaching is brief and frequent: ten minutes a few times a week, built into existing spelling and phonics work, since joins naturally follow the letter patterns children are already learning. And the time is partly repaid. A child who writes fluently spends less working memory on the mechanics of forming letters and more on what she wants to say. Research on the writing process has long shown that struggling with transcription depresses the quality and length of children's composition. Automatic handwriting frees attention for ideas.

The second objection is that keyboards are the future, and children should spend their time learning the tool they will use. I agree that typing should be taught, properly and early enough to matter. But this is a false choice. The two skills do not compete in any serious way, and a school that teaches both is better placed than one that teaches either alone. Adults move between pen and keyboard all day, and the pen has not vanished. People still sign documents, fill in forms, annotate pages, write shopping lists, scribble down a phone number, and take notes in meetings where opening a laptop would be rude. A literate person who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand is hampered in small ways every day.

The third objection concerns equity, and it cuts the other way from how it is usually argued. Children with motor difficulties or dysgraphia sometimes find cursive harder than print, and rigid insistence on one style can humiliate them. That is a reason for flexibility in teaching, and schools should certainly allow an alternative for pupils who genuinely cannot manage joins. It is not a reason to withdraw the teaching from everyone. Most children benefit from it, and the ones who struggle need more support, not an absence of instruction. Dropping handwriting teaching would not remove the inequality. Children from homes with books, pens and patient adults would still learn to write well, and the rest would be left to pick it up alone.

**What good teaching looks like**

If we keep joined-up handwriting, we should teach it better than we often do. That means starting with correct pencil grip and posture, and securing print letter formation first, since children join what they can already form. It means choosing a single, simple style with entry and exit strokes, not a fancy looped script that demands more of small hands than the task justifies. It means regular short practice rather than a blitz in Year 3 that is then forgotten. And it means treating legibility and fluency as the aims, not elegance. Nobody needs a nine-year-old to produce copperplate. We need her to write something a stranger can read at a reasonable speed without strain.

It also means being straightforward with children about why they are doing it. A class told "this is how it is done" will treat handwriting as an arbitrary ritual. A class told "this makes your writing faster and easier to read, and it will help in your exams" has a reason to care.

**The conclusion**

The argument for dropping joined-up handwriting depends on a picture of the near future in which keyboards have replaced pens entirely. That picture is not yet true, and it may never be. In the meantime, children write by hand every day at school, they are examined by hand, and they will go on using handwriting in small but real ways for the rest of their lives. Joined writing is quicker, supports spelling, and spares children from inventing their own illegible compromise. The research on handwriting's wider benefits is incomplete, but it points in the same direction, and nothing in it argues for abandoning the skill.

Schools face real pressure on time and real questions about what to prioritise. Handwriting deserves a place among those priorities, taught briefly, consistently and without fuss. The cost is small and the benefit is a child who can put her thoughts on paper quickly and clearly with whatever tool is to hand. That is still worth ten minutes a day.
