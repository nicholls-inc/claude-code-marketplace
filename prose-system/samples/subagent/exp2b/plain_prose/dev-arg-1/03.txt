Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules, and they should do it soon.

Start with what a parking minimum actually is. It is a rule, written decades ago by traffic engineers working from thin data, that says every new apartment, restaurant, clinic or shop must provide a set number of off-street spaces. The numbers often came from surveys of suburban sites with ample free parking, then hardened into law as if they were physical constants. A city might demand two spaces per apartment, one for every hundred square feet of restaurant floor, or a space for every few seats in a church. Nobody voted on the idea that a bakery needs a small parking lot. It simply became the default, and the default quietly shapes almost everything that gets built.

The first problem is cost, and it lands on people who never asked to pay it. A surface space costs a few thousand dollars to build and consumes land that could hold something useful. A space in a structure runs to tens of thousands, and underground spaces cost more still. Those costs do not vanish. Developers pass them into rents and prices, which means a household with no car subsidizes the car storage of its neighbors. Researchers who have studied this find that mandated parking raises the price of housing and reduces how much gets built, because some projects only pencil out if they can shed the spaces. In a housing shortage, forcing every new home to carry an expensive accessory is hard to defend.

The second problem is what parking minimums do to the shape of a city. Land is finite, and every space needs roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles and ramps. Stack that requirement across a downtown and you get gaps between buildings, blank walls, and storefronts set back behind seas of asphalt. Walking becomes unpleasant and then impractical, which pushes more people into cars, which seems to justify the parking. The rule produces the very condition it claims to answer. Small businesses suffer in particular. A narrow building on a historic main street could never meet modern requirements, so converting it to a cafe or a studio may be legally impossible, and the building sits empty or gets demolished for a parking lot.

Defenders raise a fair objection: if cities stop requiring parking, will neighborhoods fill with cars circling for spaces? The worry deserves a serious answer, and the answer is that removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build spaces where customers and tenants want them, because a building with no parking in a car-dependent area is hard to lease. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision informed by the actual market rather than a number from a manual. Buffalo abolished its minimums citywide in 2017, and the result was not a collapse of the street grid. Developers kept building parking where it made sense and skipped it where it did not. Minneapolis, Anchorage and a growing list of other places have followed, and the sky has stayed where it was. Even in cities that kept some rules, studies of buildings constructed under them regularly find that the required lots sit half empty on the busiest day of the year.

Where street parking really is scarce, the better tool is pricing, not mandates. Charging a sensible rate for curb space, and returning some of the revenue to the neighborhood, keeps a few spots open at all times and gives residents a stake in the arrangement. Residential permit schemes can protect existing blocks while still letting new buildings go up without a space attached. These tools address the actual problem, which is competition for a limited public resource, instead of forcing every private owner to build for the worst imaginable day.

There is also a plain argument about freedom and information. Property owners and lenders know their own projects better than a planning office does. A developer building near a rail station, a senior housing provider, and a company running a delivery kitchen each have very different needs, and a single formula cannot serve them all. Letting them decide leads to more variety: some buildings with generous parking, some with little, and many with shared arrangements where a church lot serves an office on weekdays. That diversity is more resilient than uniform rules, and it lets a city adapt as habits change, whether through ride sharing, electric bikes, remote work, or whatever comes next.

Finally, consider the climate and fiscal case. Cities carrying acres of low-value pavement collect little tax per acre, yet they must maintain the roads and pipes that serve it. Compact, mixed development generates more revenue on the same footprint and lets more people reach daily needs without a car. That is good for budgets and for emissions, and it asks no one to give up their vehicle. It only stops forcing everyone else to pay for it.

Parking minimums were an understandable mistake made with the best intentions and the worst data. Repealing them does not abolish parking; it returns the decision to the people who bear the cost and know the context. Cities that want affordable homes, living streets and healthy finances should strike the requirements from their codes and let the market, and the curb price, do the rest.
