Imagine that you and a stranger want to agree on a secret number, but the only way you can talk is by shouting across a crowded room where everyone can hear every word. For most of history this was thought to be impossible, because sharing a secret seemed to require meeting first in private, perhaps to swap a codebook or a key. In the 1970s, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, building on ideas from Ralph Merkle, showed that two people can build a shared secret entirely in public, and the idea underpins almost everything we do securely online today. The trick is not to send the secret at all, but to send pieces from which only the two of you can assemble it.

The easiest way to see the idea is with paint. Suppose you and the stranger agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow, and everyone in the room hears it. You each secretly pick a private colour of your own, such as red for you and blue for them, and mix your private colour into a big tub of the yellow. You then swap your mixed tubs across the room. Anyone watching can see the two tubs, but unmixing paint is practically impossible, so they cannot work out which private colours went in. Now you add your own private colour to the tub you received. You end up with yellow, plus the stranger's blue, plus your red, and the stranger ends up with yellow, plus your red, plus their blue. The order of mixing does not matter, so you both hold exactly the same final shade. The onlooker has only yellow, yellow-and-red and yellow-and-blue, and cannot get to the final shade without a private colour.

Mathematics has a version of paint mixing, and it relies on something called a one-way operation, which is easy to do but extremely hard to undo. The one we need comes from clock arithmetic. On a twelve-hour clock, if you add hours beyond twelve you wrap around to the start, so ten o'clock plus five hours is three o'clock. Mathematicians use the same wrapping idea with other sizes of clock, and they call the remainder after wrapping around the result "modulo" the clock size. Now consider repeated multiplication on such a clock. If you start with a number, multiply it by itself a chosen number of times, and keep only the remainder after wrapping, the result is easy to calculate. But if I show you only the final remainder and ask how many multiplications I did, you can find out only by trying every possibility. With a small clock that is trivial, but with a clock of several hundred digits it would take longer than the age of the universe.

Let us do a small example with a clock of twenty-three hours, so every answer is a whole number from zero to twenty-two. You and the stranger publicly agree on the clock size, twenty-three, and on a starting number, five. These two numbers are the yellow paint, and anyone may know them. You secretly choose the number six, and the stranger secretly chooses fifteen. You multiply five by itself six times, which gives fifteen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, and then wrap this around the twenty-three-hour clock. The remainder is eight, and you call this your public number. The stranger multiplies five by itself fifteen times, wraps the result around the same clock, and gets nineteen. You shout your eight across the room and they shout back their nineteen, and everyone hears both.

Now comes the clever step, which is the mixing of the second private colour. You take the stranger's nineteen, multiply it by itself six times, which is your secret number, and wrap it around the clock, and you get two. The stranger takes your eight, multiplies it by itself fifteen times, which is their secret number, and wraps it around the clock, and they also get two. This is no coincidence. Both calculations amount to multiplying five by itself six times fifteen, which is ninety times, and wrapping, and the order in which you do the multiplying does not change the answer. You each reached the same number by a different route, and the number two is your shared secret, which neither of you ever had to say aloud.

The question is why the listener cannot do the same. They know the clock size, the starting number five, and the two shouted numbers, eight and nineteen. To find the secret, they would need one of your private numbers, and the only way to get one is to work backwards from the public number. With a clock of twenty-three hours, they could try every possibility in seconds, which is why this example is only a toy. Real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits, or closely related tricks with curves, and then the number of possibilities is so vast that no computer on Earth could search through them. The calculation you do going forwards stays quick, because there are clever shortcuts for repeated multiplication, but the backwards search has no known shortcut. The security rests on that gap in difficulty, rather than on any hidden secret in the method, and the method itself is published openly for anyone to inspect.

Once you share a secret number, you can use it as the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method, and the two of you can then talk privately for as long as you like. A related idea, called public-key encryption, handles the case where you want people to send messages to you without any back-and-forth first. Here you create two linked numbers, one you publish for everyone and one you keep forever to yourself. The public one works like an open padlock that anyone can snap shut around a message, but only your private key can open it again. The best-known version of this, named RSA after its inventors, uses the fact that multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, while taking the product and finding the two primes again is brutally hard. The public key contains the product, and the private key contains the knowledge of the primes.

There is one important weakness, which is that all of this protects you against people listening, but not against someone pretending to be the stranger. If a trickster stands between you, they can do one exchange with you and another with the real stranger, and quietly read everything passing through. To prevent this, the public numbers are tied to a verified identity through certificates, which are digital statements vouched for by organisations that your computer already trusts. When your browser shows a padlock beside a website's name, it has done both jobs, first checking that the certificate really belongs to the site, and then agreeing a fresh shared secret with it in the open. In this way two parties who have never met can talk privately in full view of everyone, which once seemed impossible, and which now happens billions of times a day.
