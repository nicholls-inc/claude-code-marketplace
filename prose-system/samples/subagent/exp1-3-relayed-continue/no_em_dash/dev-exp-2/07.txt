A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to official clocks every so often so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. It exists because there are two ways of keeping time, and they do not quite agree.

The first is astronomical. For most of history a day was defined by the Earth's rotation: one full spin, with the Sun returning to the same spot in the sky. The second is atomic. Since 1967 the length of a second has been fixed by the vibrations of caesium atoms, which are steady to a fraction of a billionth of a second per day. Clocks built on this standard, combined into a worldwide timescale called Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), are far more regular than the planet.

The Earth is not a perfect timekeeper. Tides, the movement of the oceans and atmosphere, and the slow churning of the molten core all nudge its spin. Over the long run tidal friction is slowing it, so days are getting a little longer. The shortfall is tiny, a couple of thousandths of a second per day, but it accumulates. To keep atomic time from drifting away from the Sun, international authorities began inserting leap seconds in 1972. When one is added, the last minute of a chosen day, usually on 30 June or 31 December, runs to 61 seconds. In all, 27 have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016.

The trouble is that computers hate them. Most software assumes a minute has 60 seconds and that time only moves forward in neat steps. A repeated or extra second can confuse systems that log events in order, trade shares, route phone calls or synchronise power grids. Past leap seconds have been blamed for crashes and outages at major websites and airlines. Large technology firms now handle them by "smearing" the extra second across a day, but each company does it differently, which creates its own inconsistencies. Every insertion is a small gamble, and the risk grows as more of the world depends on precisely timed networks.

There is also a surprise in the data. In recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the prospect of a first-ever "negative" leap second, where a second is removed. Engineers say that would be even more dangerous for software, since skipping a moment is harder to handle than repeating one.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to stop the practice by or before 2035. The plan is to let the gap between atomic and Earth time grow, to perhaps a minute, and then deal with it in one go, possibly decades or even a century from now, in a way not yet decided. For ordinary people, nothing will change: the Sun will still rise on schedule, give or take a few seconds.
