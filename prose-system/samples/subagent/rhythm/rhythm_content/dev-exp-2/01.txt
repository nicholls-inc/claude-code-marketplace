The Earth does not spin at a perfectly steady rate, and a leap second is the occasional correction that keeps the clocks we live by in step with the planet beneath them. Since 1972, the world has run on Coordinated Universal Time, which is kept by atomic clocks that count out seconds with extraordinary regularity, losing perhaps a second over hundreds of millions of years. The Earth, by contrast, is a rather unreliable timekeeper, because tides, the movement of molten rock in its core, the melting of ice and the shifting of the atmosphere all nudge the length of a day by tiny and unpredictable amounts, and over the long run its rotation has generally been slowing. Left alone, the atomic clocks and the sun would drift apart, so that noon on the clock would eventually no longer coincide with the sun standing at its highest. To prevent this, international timekeepers have inserted an extra second at the end of June or December whenever the gap threatened to exceed 0.9 of a second, and they have done so 27 times, most recently at the end of 2016.

The system worked well enough when the only people who cared were astronomers and a few engineers, but it has become a nuisance in a world run by computers. A leap second means that a minute briefly contains 61 seconds, a possibility that a great deal of software was never designed to handle, and the results have included crashed servers, frozen websites and outages at major internet companies, because different systems handled the extra second in different and incompatible ways, some repeating a second, some stretching it out across the day and some simply failing. Financial markets, telecommunications networks, satellite navigation and power grids all depend on timestamps that agree to a tiny fraction of a second, and the risk of error grows with every system that must be taught to deal with a rare, irregular event that cannot be predicted more than a few months ahead.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that governs the international system of units, voted to abandon leap seconds by 2035, leaving the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation free to grow for at least a century. The decision was also made easier by a surprise, since the Earth has recently been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the odd prospect of a negative leap second in which a second would be removed, something that has never been tried and that engineers dread even more than the usual kind. Scientists say that the accumulated difference, which might reach a minute or so, can be dealt with later by some new and gentler arrangement, perhaps a larger correction made once in many decades, and for ordinary people the change will be invisible, since nobody will notice that the sun and the clock have begun, very slowly, to part company.
