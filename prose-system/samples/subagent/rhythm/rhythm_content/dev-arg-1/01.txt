Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon, because the rules force every new building to subsidize car storage whether or not anyone who will live or work there owns a car. A minimum parking requirement is a line in the zoning code saying that a restaurant must provide so many spaces per hundred square feet, or an apartment building so many per unit, and the numbers were mostly invented in the mid-twentieth century by planners who borrowed them from traffic engineering manuals, relied on surveys of suburban sites with free parking, and had little evidence that the figures matched what any particular building needed. Donald Shoup, the UCLA professor whose book The High Cost of Free Parking laid out the case, found that the ratios in many cities traced back to the peak demand observed at a handful of sites, which means a developer is told to build for the busiest hour of the busiest day at a place with no price on parking, and then to build that much everywhere, forever.

The consequences show up in the cost of housing, in the shape of our streets, and in the businesses that never open. A single structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground space in an expensive city can cost far more, and since a developer cannot recover that money except through rents and sale prices, tenants who own no car pay for spaces they never use. Parking mandates also eat land and floor area that could hold apartments or shops, so a small lot that could have fit twelve homes fits eight, or the project is abandoned because the parking math will not close. Old buildings suffer from the same logic, since a corner bakery that wants to replace a vacant laundromat may be told it needs a dozen spaces the lot cannot supply, and the storefront stays empty. Cities that wonder why their main streets look like gap-toothed seams of surface lots might look first at their own code.

The usual defence is that without mandates, new buildings will dump their cars onto the surrounding streets and neighbors will fight over curb space. That worry deserves a serious answer, and the answer is that removing a minimum is different from banning parking. Developers who believe tenants want spaces will keep building them, because a building with no parking in a place where people drive is hard to rent, and lenders know it. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision informed by the market instead of a number handed down by a committee, and in practice many projects still include plenty of parking after reform. Buffalo eliminated its citywide requirements in 2017 and Minneapolis followed in 2021, and neither saw the spillover catastrophe that opponents predicted, while developers in those cities have been able to propose smaller, cheaper and more varied projects. Auckland, which scrapped its minimums in 2016, saw new housing include a wide range of parking ratios depending on location, which is what you would expect if the market were finally allowed to speak.

The spillover problem, where it exists, has a better fix than a mandate, which is to price the curb. Streets are public property and the cheapest way to manage them is to charge something close to what the space is worth at the busiest times, using residential permits or metered rates, with the revenue returned to the neighborhood in the form of sidewalk repairs or lighting. When curb parking is free and scarce, every new building looks like a threat, but when it is priced to stay roughly one space in seven open, the threat dissolves and a new resident's car is simply another customer for a managed resource. Shoup has long argued that the combination of pricing and reform is where cities get the real gains, and I think he is right, though it is a mistake to wait for the second step before taking the first.

A fair critic will point out that some places are so car dependent that dropping minimums changes little, and that is partly true, since in a sprawling suburb with no transit most developers will keep building parking anyway. But that is an argument for repeal being harmless in those places, and it hardly supports keeping a rule that does real damage in the neighborhoods where people could live without a car if the law stopped insisting on one. There is also the fairness argument, which cuts in a direction the defenders of mandates rarely admit: households without cars tend to be poorer, younger or older than the average, and they are the ones who pay extra for the spaces they do not use, while the households who do drive pay a price that hides the real cost of storing their vehicles.

Climate and public finance point the same way. Abundant free parking is an invitation to drive for every trip, and it spreads destinations apart until walking is impractical, which locks in emissions for decades. It also costs cities money, because low-density parking-heavy development generates less tax revenue per acre than compact blocks while requiring the same pipes and roads. Cities that care about any of these goals have been undermining them with one hand while advertising them with the other.

Repeal is not a radical step. It asks a city to stop pretending that it knows how many spaces a laundromat, a clinic or a fourplex needs, and to let the people who will pay for those spaces decide. Where the market wants parking, it will appear, and where it does not, the land can hold homes. Councils that take this step will find the fears mostly unfounded and the benefits arriving quickly, and the ones that wait will keep paying, in rents and in empty lots, for a number that nobody can defend.
