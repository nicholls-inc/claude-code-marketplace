The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 rose from Florida on a column of fire, bound for the Moon. It was the third mission meant to land people there, and by then the public had grown a little bored with the idea. Television networks had skipped the crew's broadcasts. Nobody guessed that within three days the whole world would be watching again.

The crew was Jim Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert. Lovell was the veteran, on his fourth flight, and he was to command the landing. Haise was the lunar module pilot. Swigert was a late substitute. Only days before launch, doctors discovered that the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles, and Swigert took his seat with almost no time to spare. The spacecraft had two halves joined together. The command module, named Odyssey, was the cone-shaped cabin where the men would live and where they would return to Earth. Behind it sat the service module, a cylinder packed with fuel, water and the oxygen tanks that fed the electrical system. Attached to the front was the lunar module, named Aquarius, a spindly craft built to carry two men to the surface.

The first two days passed without trouble. About fifty-five hours into the flight, the ship was nearly two hundred thousand miles from home. Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the supercold liquid oxygen in the service module's tanks, a routine chore that keeps the contents from settling. He flipped the switch. Moments later there was a bang, and the cabin shuddered.

Lovell and Haise first suspected a meteoroid strike. Swigert's report to the ground has become famous. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," he said, and Lovell repeated it for the controllers. In Houston, screens showed readings that made no sense. Pressure in one oxygen tank had vanished, and the second was draining fast. Electrical power was dropping. Out of the window, Lovell saw gas streaming into space.

The cause lay years in the past. Before launch, a tank had been damaged during a ground test, and a later workaround had scorched the insulation on a wire inside it. When Swigert switched on the stirring fans, a spark leaped through the oxygen and the tank burst. The blast tore away an entire panel of the service module and crippled the neighbouring tank. Without oxygen the fuel cells that made electricity and water began to die, and the command module was suddenly a lifeboat with a few hours of battery left.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams worked through the logic quickly. The Moon was no longer the goal. The goal was to get three men home alive. The answer was to treat Aquarius as the lifeboat. The little lander had its own oxygen, power and water, all meant to keep two men alive for about two days on the lunar surface. Now it would have to sustain three men for roughly four. The crew shut down Odyssey to save what battery it had for the return through the atmosphere, and they crowded into the lunar module.

The ship was on a path that would swing it around the far side of the Moon and, if nothing were done, bring it back toward Earth, but not close enough to the right place. Controllers needed to speed up the return and aim it at the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships waited. A few hours after the explosion, Lovell fired Aquarius's descent engine, an engine designed for landing and not for this. The burn worked. Days later, as the ship rounded the Moon, they fired it again, and the crew passed farther from Earth than any humans had ever travelled, a record that still stands.

The hardest part was living through the voyage. Aquarius shed heat, and the temperature inside sank toward freezing. Water condensed on the walls. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. To spare electricity and water, the men slept little and drank very little, a few ounces a day. They also had to avoid shutting off the systems that kept the cabin livable, because restarting them was never guaranteed.

Then came a quieter danger. Every breath adds carbon dioxide to the cabin air, and canisters of lithium hydroxide scrub it out. The lunar module carried enough for two men, and the supply was running out. Odyssey held plenty of spare canisters, but they were square and the lander's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the same odds and ends the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard, a hose and a roll of duct tape, and built an adapter on a table in Houston. They read the instructions to the crew, step by step. The crew built the device in orbit and fitted it, and the gauge readings began to fall.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the astronaut who had missed the flight, worked in a simulator with engineers to write a new procedure. The command module normally took hours to wake up with plenty of power, and now it had to be restarted on a tiny budget, in the right order, with almost no margin. They found a sequence that fit.

Navigation added another problem. The guidance system in Aquarius had been shut down to save power, so the crew could not rely on the usual stars and computers. At one point they steered by the line between night and day on the Earth's surface, holding it steady in the window while a manual burn ran for a few seconds. The ground checked the numbers, and the corrections held.

On April 17, the crew prepared for the end. They fired the service module away first, and as it drifted off Lovell saw how much of its side was gone. A whole panel was missing, and equipment hung out. It was the first clear picture of the damage. Next they released Aquarius, which had saved their lives, and it fell behind to burn up in the atmosphere. Alone in Odyssey, the men waited.

Reentry brings a communications blackout, as the heat of the hot gas around a capsule blocks radio. It normally lasts around three minutes. This time it stretched past that, and the silence in Mission Control grew. Controllers watched the clocks and said very little. Some feared the heat shield had cracked in the explosion. Then Swigert's voice came through the static. The capsule had survived.

Parachutes opened, and the cone drifted down into the South Pacific near Samoa, a few miles from the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima. The crew had been in space almost six days. They were tired, cold and thin, and Haise was ill, but all three walked or were helped across the deck. Millions of people had followed the story by radio and television, and the response was relief, and also a kind of astonishment that it had worked.

An investigation blamed the damaged wiring and the tank design, and engineers changed the tanks, the wiring and the way power was stored for later flights. NASA also changed how it prepared for emergencies. Lovell never walked on the Moon, but his book about the flight and a later film made the mission famous. A phrase often linked to Kranz, "Failure is not an option," came from that film, not from his real words. What the real record shows is people who kept thinking when the plan fell apart. A ship built for one purpose was used for another, and three men came home.
