**Stop Making Cities Build Parking Nobody Asked For**

Picture a small café that wants to open in an empty storefront on a walkable main street. The owner has a lease and a menu ready, and customers live within a few blocks. Then the city tells her she can't open until she provides eight off-street parking spaces. There is no room for them, so the storefront stays empty.

Variations of this happen in cities across the country every week. They happen because of minimum parking requirements, the zoning rules that make every new building include a set number of parking spaces, whether or not anyone needs them. Cities should repeal these rules. Parking would not disappear. Developers and businesses would decide how much to build based on actual demand, not on a formula written decades ago.

**The numbers were never real**

Most parking minimums were not built on careful study. Planners often took them from neighboring towns or from industry manuals that counted cars at suburban sites during peak hours. The urban planning scholar Donald Shoup, whose book *The High Cost of Free Parking* did more than anything else to put this issue on the agenda, spent years showing how arbitrary the ratios are. Codes have required a given number of spaces per thousand square feet of floor area, per bowling lane, or per seat in a church. A café in a dense neighborhood where most customers walk ends up with the same requirement as one beside a highway exit.

When a rule rests on guesswork, it gets things wrong in both directions. In practice, the errors pile up on the side of too much parking. Many studies of residential and commercial parking have found large shares of required spaces sitting empty even at peak times. That empty asphalt is land that could hold homes, shops, or trees.

**Parking is expensive, and everyone pays**

A structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground space often costs more. Developers do not absorb that cost. It shows up in rents, in home prices, and in the price of a sandwich. Because the cost is bundled into the building, people who do not own a car still pay for a space they never use. That group skews toward lower-income residents, older people, and young people.

Parking requirements also decide which projects get built at all. On a small or oddly shaped lot, fitting the required spaces may be physically impossible or may consume so much of the budget that the project no longer pencils out. Small apartment buildings, conversions of old buildings, and modest infill housing, the types of housing many cities say they want more of, are the projects most likely to be killed by parking mandates. During a housing shortage, a rule that quietly blocks homes deserves hard scrutiny.

**Cities that tried it are doing fine**

This is not a theoretical experiment. Buffalo, New York, dropped citywide parking minimums in 2017. Minneapolis removed them in 2021. Austin, Texas, followed in 2023, becoming one of the largest American cities to do so, and California passed a state law in 2022 barring most parking minimums near major transit stops. Many other cities have eliminated requirements in downtowns or along transit corridors.

These places did not descend into parking chaos. Developers kept building parking where the market called for it, because lenders and tenants in car-dependent locations still want it. In walkable and transit-served areas they built less, and they used the savings for more units or ground-floor space. Removing the mandate does not ban parking. It lets the people who know the site decide.

**Answering the objections**

The most common worry is spillover: if a new building lacks enough spaces, residents will take up street parking that neighbors count on. That concern is reasonable, but parking minimums are a crude and costly way to address it. Cities have better tools. They can price curb parking, set up residential permit districts, and enforce time limits. Those measures handle the street directly, instead of forcing every new building to overbuild.

A second objection is that some neighborhoods truly depend on cars. That is true in some places, and in those places developers will keep building parking because buyers and tenants will demand it. Repeal does not stop that. It only stops the city from forcing that answer onto every block, including the ones where it is wrong.

**A modest, overdue reform**

Repealing parking minimums is one of the rare policy changes that costs the public almost nothing. It needs no new spending and no new agency. It simply deletes a mandate that raises housing costs, wastes land, and puts small businesses at a disadvantage, and it lets supply follow demand.

Cities are free to keep regulating how parking looks, where driveways go, and how the curb is managed. They should stop requiring developers to build spaces that nobody may use. That café owner should be able to open her doors. Let her customers walk in.
