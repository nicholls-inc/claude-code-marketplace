Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. I hold this view because the requirements ask planning authorities to settle a question that builders, lenders and tenants are better placed to settle, and because the places that have dropped them have seen little go wrong.

A minimum parking requirement is a rule that tells a developer how many spaces to build with each new building. A common American formula asks for two spaces per dwelling, or one space for every 300 square feet of shop floor. Few of these numbers come from measurement of local demand. Donald Shoup, the planning professor at UCLA, showed in The High Cost of Free Parking in 2005 that many were drawn from manuals of the Institute of Transportation Engineers, which in turn relied on surveys of suburban sites where parking was plentiful and free. Those figures describe peak demand for a space that costs the driver nothing. They do not describe how many spaces a building needs.

The cost of the rule is easy to state. A space in a surface lot takes up about 300 square feet once the aisles are counted. A space in a parking structure commonly costs between $25,000 and $50,000 to build in the United States, and an underground space costs more. A developer who must provide them adds that cost to the rent or the sale price. Tenants who own no car pay for the space anyway. Where land is expensive, a developer may drop homes from the plan or give up a small site altogether, because the required parking will not fit. A rule meant to protect neighbours from crowded streets therefore also reduces the number of homes and shops that get built.

There is a second cost, which falls on the shape of the place. When every building must carry its own lot, buildings are set back from the street and spread apart, and walking between them becomes longer and less pleasant. That makes driving more necessary, which raises the demand for parking. The requirement helps to create the condition it is meant to answer.

The usual objection is that without a requirement, new buildings will have no parking and cars will fill the surrounding streets. The experience of cities that have acted does not support this. Buffalo, New York, removed its citywide minimums in 2017, and most new projects there still include parking, in amounts chosen by the developer. Auckland removed its minimums in 2016, and New Zealand required all of its cities to do the same in 2020. England abolished its national minimum standards in 2011, and builders there continue to provide parking where buyers want it. Developers have no wish to build homes that cannot be sold, and lenders are wary of projects that look short of spaces. The market already supplies parking in suburbs and in places where most households own cars. Removing the requirement changes who decides, and does not decide whether parking exists.

Removing the minimum also does not oblige a city to ignore parking. A city that is worried about spillover onto residential streets has better tools than a fixed ratio in the building code. It can price kerb space, issue residential permits and set prices so that a few spaces are free at any hour. Shoup has argued that charging for kerb parking at a level that keeps about 85 per cent of spaces occupied leaves streets usable and raises revenue that can be returned to the neighbourhood. These measures deal with the street, where the problem appears, and leave the building to be designed for the people who will use it.

Some will say that poorer households need cars and that fewer spaces will hurt them. This is a fair concern. Yet the requirement raises the price of every unit, including those that low-income renters occupy, and low-income households own fewer cars than average. Bundled parking is a subsidy to drivers that is paid for by everyone who rents the flat. Letting tenants choose whether to pay for a space is likely to help those with the least money.

There is also the matter of existing buildings. Old downtown blocks in many cities could not be built today, because their lots are too small to meet the current code. An owner who wants to convert an empty shop into flats or a restaurant often has to ask for a variance, which costs time and money and leaves the fate of the project to a hearing board. Removing the minimum would end that step.

I do not claim that every city will see a surge of building the day after the vote. Land prices, limits on height and the cost of construction matter more in many places. The case is a smaller one. A rule that has weak grounding in local evidence, that adds thousands of dollars to each home and that cities can replace with better tools ought to go. Cities should repeal their minimum parking requirements and let the amount of parking be decided by those who pay for it.
