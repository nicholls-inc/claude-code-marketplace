Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, or at least stop treating those estimates as promises. The case rests on a simple observation: the people who ask for estimates and the people who give them almost never believe the numbers, yet both sides keep acting as though the numbers were real.

Consider what an estimate actually is. It is a guess about the future made at the moment when you know the least about the problem. The hard parts of a software task, the odd legacy behavior, the missing API field, the dependency that turns out to be broken, reveal themselves only after you start building. By then the estimate has already been written down, repeated in a planning meeting, and turned into a date on someone's roadmap. Decades of experience, and a fair amount of research, suggest that we are consistently optimistic and that the error is large enough to swamp any precision we pretend to have.

The usual defense is that managers need estimates to plan, coordinate with other teams, and make promises to customers. That need is genuine, but the estimate is a poor way to meet it. A number produced under pressure and padded for safety tells a manager very little, and it quietly changes behavior in damaging ways. Engineers pad, so the work expands to fill the padding. Or managers trim, so engineers cut corners on testing to hit a figure they never believed. Either way, the conversation shifts from what we should build to whether we are on schedule, and that is a worse conversation to have.

There is also the cost of the ritual itself. Teams spend hours each sprint debating whether a ticket is a three or a five, and those hours produce no software. The debate occasionally surfaces useful disagreement about scope, but you can get that benefit by asking a better question, namely whether the work can be broken into pieces small enough that nobody argues about their size.

What should replace estimation is a steady flow of small changes and honest measurement of what the team actually ships. If you slice work into increments that take a day or two, then counting finished items gives you a forecast based on real history rather than hope. A manager who can say that the team typically completes eight to twelve items a week, and that the backlog holds sixty, has a far more defensible answer than any sum of story points. When the answer changes, the data shows it early, before a deadline is missed rather than after.

For the harder cases, where a date truly matters, the right move is to negotiate scope rather than time. Decide what must ship, what can slip, and review progress weekly against working software. That is a conversation about tradeoffs, which is the conversation managers should want to be having anyway.

None of this means teams should abandon planning or refuse to discuss size. It means we should stop pretending that a guess made on day one is a commitment, and stop rewarding people for producing confident numbers about things nobody can yet know. Put the effort into smaller slices, faster feedback, and plain talk about uncertainty, and you will get better predictability than estimation ever delivered.
