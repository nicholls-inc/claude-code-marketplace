The Earth does not spin at a perfectly steady rate, and a leap second is the occasional correction that keeps our clocks in step with it. Since 1972 the world has run on Coordinated Universal Time, which is kept by atomic clocks that count out seconds with extraordinary regularity, while the planet itself, the original basis of timekeeping, wobbles and drifts, slowed by tides and nudged by the movements of its molten core and by shifts in ice and ocean. When the gap between atomic time and the time implied by the Earth's rotation threatened to grow beyond a second, officials at the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service announced that one extra second would be inserted at the end of a June or December, so that a minute that day ran to sixty-one seconds and the clock read 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Twenty-seven such seconds were added between 1972 and the end of 2016, and the practice was long regarded as a harmless piece of housekeeping that kept noon roughly aligned with the sun.

The trouble is that computers are not good at being told that a minute has sixty-one seconds. Most software assumes every minute has sixty, and the extra second has repeatedly caused systems to freeze, mis-order records or crash, as happened to several large websites and airline booking systems after leap seconds in 2012 and 2015. Engineers at the big technology companies developed workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across a whole day in tiny increments, but each company did it differently, which creates its own risks of disagreement between machines that need to agree on the time to within a few thousandths of a second, as in financial trading, power grids, telecommunications and satellite navigation.

A further complication arrived in recent years, when the Earth began spinning slightly faster than expected, raising the prospect of a first negative leap second in which a second would be removed, something that no software has been properly tested to handle and that might be more dangerous than adding one.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of the second, voted to stop applying leap seconds by 2035, allowing the difference between atomic and astronomical time to grow well beyond a second for the time being. The expectation is that a different fix, perhaps a correction of a full minute made only once in many decades or even centuries, will be agreed on later, at which point most people will notice nothing at all.
