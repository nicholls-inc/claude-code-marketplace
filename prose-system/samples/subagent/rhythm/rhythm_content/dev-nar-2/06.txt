Apollo 13 lifted off from Cape Kennedy on April 11, 1970, carrying James Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert toward what was meant to be the third landing of humans on the Moon. Swigert had joined the crew only days earlier, when flight surgeons grounded the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, after he was exposed to German measles, and the launch was otherwise so smooth that the first two days passed in an atmosphere of routine, with the crew's television broadcast on the evening of April 13 drawing little interest from the networks. The spacecraft was a stack of three parts: the conical command module, named Odyssey, where the crew lived and where they would return to Earth; the cylindrical service module behind it, which held the main engine, the fuel cells that made electricity and water, and the tanks of oxygen and hydrogen that fed them; and the spidery lunar module, Aquarius, which was to carry Lovell and Haise to the surface of the Fra Mauro highlands.

About fifty-five hours into the flight, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine job that mixes the supercold contents so that the gauges read correctly, and Swigert threw the switches that turned on the fans inside oxygen tank number two. At 9:08 that evening, Houston time, a bang shook the spacecraft, the lights flickered, and a master alarm sounded. Swigert reported that they had had a problem, and Lovell repeated it for the ground, in the phrase that later became famous in a slightly altered form. The cause lay in a history that began years before the flight. The tank had been dropped a couple of inches during handling at the factory in 1968, and when ground crews later had trouble draining it, they ran its heaters on a higher voltage than the thermostat switches were built for, which fused the switches shut and damaged the Teflon insulation on the wires inside. When Swigert turned on the fans, a spark in that bare wiring ignited the insulation in a tank full of liquid oxygen, the pressure rose until the tank ruptured, and the blast tore off a panel of the service module and crippled the second oxygen tank as well.

The crew did not know any of this at first. They saw only that the fuel cells were dying, that oxygen pressure was dropping, and that out of a window Lovell could see gas venting into space, a sight that told him more than any gauge. Within a couple of hours it was clear that the command module would soon lose all its power and that the landing was off, and the whole effort of the next four days became a matter of getting three men home alive. The lunar module, built to keep two men alive for about two days on the surface, became a lifeboat that had to support three men for roughly four. Odyssey was shut down almost entirely to save its small reserve of battery power for the final descent, and the crew crowded into Aquarius, where they powered down everything they could, leaving only what was needed to keep the oxygen flowing and the electronics cooled.

The trajectory was the next problem, because the spacecraft had been aimed on a path that would have missed the Earth entirely had no further burn been made after the Moon was passed. Flight controllers in Houston, led by Gene Kranz and his team in white vests, along with the engineers of the contractors, chose to use the lunar module's descent engine, which had never been intended for such a task, to put the craft back on a course that would bring it to Earth. The first burn came a few hours after the explosion, and the second came about two hours after the craft swung around the far side of the Moon, passing some two hundred and fifty kilometres above the surface; it sped up the return by about ten hours and moved the splashdown into the Pacific, where recovery ships waited. At the farthest point of its swing, the spacecraft was nearly four hundred thousand kilometres from Earth, farther than any humans had been before or have been since.

Life on board became a long ordeal of cold and thirst. With the power off, the temperature in the cabin dropped to a few degrees above freezing, moisture condensed on the walls, and the men, who had to ration water to a few ounces a day each, slept little and badly in the lunar module's cramped space. Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection, and all three lost weight and became dehydrated. A deeper danger came from the air itself, since carbon dioxide exhaled by three men was building up in a cabin whose lithium hydroxide canisters, the filters that scrub it out, were nearly spent, and the spare canisters from the command module were square while the receptacles in the lunar module were round. Engineers on the ground, working from only the materials known to be aboard, devised an adapter from a plastic bag, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, hoses from a spacesuit and a great deal of duct tape, and read the instructions up to the crew, who built it and watched the carbon dioxide reading fall back to safe levels.

The last great task was bringing Odyssey back to life, because no one had ever turned on a command module from a cold, nearly dead state, and there was no written procedure for it. Mattingly, who had stayed on the ground, worked with engineers in the simulator through the last days to build one that squeezed the entry sequence into the limited amps available, and it was read up to the crew a few hours before the entry. Along the way the crew had to correct their course by hand, aligning the spacecraft using the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, since the guidance platform had been turned off and could not be trusted to be realigned. Shortly before the end, they jettisoned the service module and saw for the first time how much of its side had been blown away, and then, an hour or so before entry, they released Aquarius, which had saved them, and let it burn up in the atmosphere behind them.

The entry on April 17 brought a final period of suspense, since the communications blackout caused by the heat of re-entry normally lasts about three minutes, and this time it stretched on past four, then five, and nearly six minutes, as controllers watched and the world listened to silence on the radio. Then Swigert's voice came through, and soon afterwards the three parachutes were seen on television blossoming over the South Pacific, and Odyssey splashed down near Samoa within sight of the recovery ship Iwo Jima, a little under six days after launch. A review board led by Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank and its mishandled history, and the redesign that followed removed the fans from the oxygen tanks and added a third tank and other changes, while the crew, though denied the Moon, became symbols of a kind of improvisation that the program's engineers called their finest hour.
