Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. The case for dropping it sounds modern and sensible, since children will spend their adult lives typing on phones and keyboards, and the hours spent looping letters together could be given to coding or reading. But the argument rests on a narrow picture of what handwriting is for. Cursive is not a decorative leftover from the age of quill pens. It is a practical skill that serves fluency, memory, inclusion and thought, and the evidence for abandoning it is much weaker than its critics assume.

Start with the simplest point, which is that people still write by hand, and they will go on doing so. Children take exams with a pen, fill in forms, leave notes, label diagrams and jot down ideas in meetings, and most of those tasks reward speed and legibility. A child who prints each letter separately lifts the pencil dozens of times in every sentence, and that adds up to a slow and tiring way of writing. Joined-up writing lets the hand move in a continuous line, so words come out as single gestures rather than strings of separate marks. Teachers who mark long timed essays will tell you that a fluent, joined hand is often the difference between a pupil who finishes and one who runs out of time. Typing does not replace this, because schools cannot put a laptop in front of every child for every task, and many examinations still depend on paper.

The second argument concerns what happens in the brain when a child learns to write by hand. Research in developmental psychology has repeatedly found that forming letters by hand helps children recognise them. In studies that compared young children who practised letters by writing with those who typed or traced them, the hand writers tended to show stronger letter recognition and more activity in the parts of the brain associated with reading. The reason is plausible enough. Writing a letter requires the child to produce its shape from memory, and that act of production fixes the shape more firmly than pressing a key ever could. All keys feel the same under the finger, whereas every letter has a different movement. Cursive adds a further demand, because the child must learn how letters flow into one another, and that raises the level of motor planning involved. It would be too strong to claim that cursive is proven to be better than print for every purpose, and honest advocates should concede that the research is mixed on that narrower question. But the broader finding, that handwriting supports early literacy in ways typing does not, gives schools good reason to keep a rich handwriting curriculum rather than thin it out.

Joined-up writing also has a specific advantage for spelling. When a child writes a word in one continuous movement, the word becomes a single motor pattern, almost like a small piece of choreography. Many teachers report that children who write cursive from an early age find it easier to remember how common letter strings fit together, such as the endings of words or the awkward double letters. Print can produce the same effect, but cursive makes it more natural because the hand is already carrying the sequence forward. This matters especially for children who find spelling hard. For those who reverse letters, the difficulty is easier to fix in cursive, because the joins make it awkward to form a b or a d the wrong way round. A well-taught joined hand can therefore act as a quiet support for pupils who would otherwise struggle, which is a good reason to teach it to everyone rather than reserving it for the few.

That point leads naturally to inclusion, where the case for cursive is stronger than many people expect. Children with dyslexia and related difficulties are often helped by multisensory teaching in which the hand, eye and ear work together, and cursive fits that approach well. Occupational therapists frequently recommend it for children with motor difficulties, since the continuous flow reduces the number of stops and starts that make writing laborious. Critics sometimes answer that these children could simply be given a keyboard, and for some of them a keyboard is the right tool. But a keyboard is an addition to handwriting, not a substitute for the foundation that early handwriting builds. A school that teaches joined-up writing well and also offers typing gives its pupils both skills, while a school that drops cursive removes one of its most useful supports before it has been tried.

There is also the question of what children are being denied if the skill disappears altogether. Adults who cannot read cursive are cut off from a great deal of the written record. Letters from grandparents, historical documents, old family recipes, handwritten comments from a teacher and the annotations in an old book are all written in joined-up script. A child who has never learned it will find these things as opaque as a foreign alphabet. This is not a sentimental point. It is a point about literacy in its fullest sense, which includes the ability to read the handwriting of other people. If we want children to be able to read widely, and to feel connected to the past, we should teach them the script that most people over a certain age use every day.

Opponents will say that curriculum time is limited and that every minute spent on handwriting is a minute taken from something else. This is a fair objection, but the numbers do not support it. Handwriting practice in a typical primary school takes a small share of the week, often in short sessions of ten or fifteen minutes. That time is not wasted even on the critics' own terms, since it also trains fine motor control, concentration and care, qualities that carry over into drawing, music and craft. The real choice is not between handwriting and coding, as some of the rhetoric suggests. Schools can teach both, and most of the countries that are held up as models of educational success manage to do so. Finland, which is often cited in this debate as having removed compulsory cursive, still requires handwriting in some form, and the Finnish decision came with a strong emphasis on typing that most British and American schools could not yet match.

A further objection is that cursive is hard to teach well and that many children end up with a messy hybrid in any case. That is true, and it is an argument for better teaching rather than for abandonment. Most adults do not write in the strict style of a school copybook, and nobody should expect them to. They develop a personal hand that mixes joined and unjoined letters, and research suggests that the fastest and most legible writers often do exactly this. But a hybrid style is something a person arrives at from a starting point, and the starting point matters. A child who has been taught a clear, consistent joined script has a base from which to develop a fast and readable personal hand. A child who has been taught nothing but print will tend to stay slow, and a child who has been taught nothing at all will often invent a cramped and illegible scrawl. The purpose of teaching cursive is to give children a good model, not to enforce a perfect one.

Finally, there is something worth defending in the experience of learning to write well by hand. Writing by hand is slower than typing, and that slowness has value. It encourages a child to think before committing words to paper, to attend to the shape of a sentence, and to take pride in producing something clear and handsome. Many people who write for a living still draft by hand at certain stages because the pace suits their thinking. Children do not need to become calligraphers, but they do benefit from learning that care in the making of a thing is worth the effort. Handwriting is one of the few places in a school day where a child is asked to make something with the hands and judge the result by eye, and a curriculum that cares about the whole child should not be quick to give that up.

None of this means that schools should cling to old methods or punish children for untidy work. Typing should be taught properly and early, and digital tools should be used wherever they help. The claim is a more modest one. Joined-up handwriting is a useful, learnable and in many ways beneficial skill, and the case against it rests more on fashion than on evidence. It supports speed, early literacy and spelling, it helps children who find writing difficult, and it opens the door to reading the handwriting of others. It costs little time and offers real returns. Primary schools should keep teaching it, teach it well, and trust that a child who can both write fluently and type competently is better equipped than one who can only do the second.
