Houston, We've Had a Problem

On April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 13 from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center. Three men rode in the spacecraft. Jim Lovell, a veteran of two Gemini flights, commanded the mission. Fred Haise was making his first trip to space. Jack Swigert, a command module pilot, had been added only days before launch. He took the seat of Ken Mattingly, who had been pulled from the crew after being exposed to German measles. The goal was a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands, a region Apollo 12 had not reached.

The first two days went smoothly. The command module, named Odyssey, was joined to the lunar module, Aquarius, which was tucked beneath it. Early on the evening of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the flight and nearly 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew prepared for a routine task. Swigert switched on a fan inside one of the service module's two liquid oxygen tanks, a stirring procedure meant to keep the supercold oxygen evenly mixed. The tank had been damaged earlier, and wiring inside it had been overheated by a thermostat, leaving the insulation brittle. When the fan ran, a spark ignited the insulation. The oxygen inside burned violently, and the blast rocked the spacecraft.

Lovell later described it as a sharp jolt, then a series of alarms. Lights flickered, and the crew saw their electrical systems failing. Swigert's radio call to Mission Control, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," started one of the most famous exchanges in spaceflight. Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas streaming from the side of the service module. The oxygen tank had emptied, and the second tank was failing too. Without oxygen, the fuel cells that made electricity and drinking water could no longer run. Two of the three were dead. The mission was no longer a landing. It was a fight to get home alive.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz gathered his team and formed a group that became known as the Tiger Team. The engineers had to answer hard questions quickly. Was the command module still usable? How much power and oxygen remained? Could the spacecraft be steered back to Earth? The team settled on a plan. The lunar module would become a lifeboat. Its engines, life support and batteries were designed to keep two men alive on the Moon for about two days. Now it had to carry three men for roughly four days.

The crew moved into Aquarius and shut down Odyssey to save its remaining power. The move brought new problems. The lunar module was cramped and cold. Temperatures inside dropped close to freezing, and moisture dripped from the walls. Water was rationed sharply. Breathing became the most urgent issue. Exhaled carbon dioxide had to be removed by canisters filled with lithium hydroxide, and the lunar module's canisters were round, while the spare canisters aboard the command module were square. Odyssey's canisters would not fit. Engineers in Houston were given the same parts the crew had on board and asked to build an adapter. They improvised one from plastic bags, cardboard, duct tape and a spare suit hose. Once the design was ready, the crew assembled it while the team watched on television, and the adapter worked.

The mission now depended on physics. The crew could not turn back by simply firing an engine. Instead, they would use the Moon's gravity. The flight plan's free-return trajectory sent the spacecraft around the far side of the Moon and back toward Earth without requiring a large engine burn. On April 14, while swinging around the Moon, Apollo 13 set a record that still stands for the greatest distance humans have traveled from Earth: 248,655 miles. The next day, the spacecraft passed behind the Moon, out of radio contact for about half an hour. When it reappeared, the crew was about 158 miles above the surface. Mission Control then used the lunar module's descent engine for a short correction burn to make sure the path home was accurate.

Keeping the spacecraft's temperature steady also took work. Without enough power for normal heaters, the crew slowly rotated the spacecraft so that no side stayed in the Sun too long. This "barbecue roll" kept the ship from freezing or overheating. The crew also tracked their position with navigation sightings, while engineers on the ground watched every system and rehearsed the restart of Odyssey. Ken Mattingly, the crew member who had been replaced, helped by testing the restart procedures in the simulator. The restart was risky. The command module had been dark and cold for days, and its batteries had to be saved for the final part of the trip.

On April 17, the crew prepared to re-enter Earth's atmosphere. Before leaving the lunar module, they jettisoned it, and then they separated the service module. From the command module, they saw the damaged service module with a gash in its side. The command module's heat shield was the only thing protecting them from temperatures near the Sun's surface during re-entry. Odyssey was powered up and steered through the most important burn of the flight. The crew waited through the blackout that comes from ionized air surrounding a spacecraft during re-entry. Then the parachutes opened.

Apollo 13 splashed down in the South Pacific at 1:07 p.m. Eastern Time on April 17, 1970, close to the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The crew was safe. They had flown 142 hours and 54 minutes, and they had survived one of the most serious emergencies in the history of crewed spaceflight.

The mission did not land on the Moon, but it became one of the most respected stories of the space program. Its lessons changed how NASA designed spacecraft and trained flight controllers. The lunar module's use as a lifeboat was studied in detail, and procedures were rewritten to protect against similar failures. Apollo 14 flew to Fra Mauro in early 1971, completing the landing that Apollo 13 had been planned to achieve.

Years later, Lovell reflected on the mission with some humility. He and his crew were not heroes in the sense of planning to be rescued. They were ordinary men doing their jobs under extraordinary pressure, supported by thousands of people on the ground who refused to accept that the situation was hopeless. The phrase "Houston, we've had a problem" remains the mission's most famous line, but the story behind it is about teamwork, improvisation and patience. Apollo 13 is remembered not because everything went right, but because a crew and a control room found a way to bring everyone home when much of the spacecraft had already failed.
