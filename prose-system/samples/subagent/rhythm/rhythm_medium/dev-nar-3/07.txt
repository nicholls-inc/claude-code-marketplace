On a mild January afternoon in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with the ordinary noise of a working neighborhood. Dockworkers, teamsters, and children crowded the streets near the harbor, and many of them paused to eat lunch in the winter sun. Looming over them at Commercial Street stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, which held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a vital ingredient in the munitions that the country had been manufacturing during the Great War.

The tank had never been trustworthy. It had been built quickly in 1915, with thin walls and little testing, and nobody had bothered to fill it with water first to check for weakness. When it was loaded, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping molasses in cans, and the company responded by painting the tank brown so that the stains would be harder to see. Workers heard groans and creaks from the structure, but supervisors dismissed their worries. A fresh delivery of warm molasses had been pumped in only a day earlier, and the sudden rise in temperature, combined with the pressure of the full load, was about to prove fatal.

At about twelve thirty on January 15, the tank ruptured with a sound that witnesses compared to machine gun fire. Rivets shot out like bullets, and the steel wall tore open. A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen feet high in places, surged across the neighborhood at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It was heavy and dense, and it crushed everything in its path. The elevated railway was pushed off its supports, buildings were knocked from their foundations, and wagons and horses were swept away. A firehouse was torn apart, and some of the firefighters inside were trapped in the wreckage.

The cold air quickly thickened the syrup, which turned a rescue into a desperate struggle. People who had survived the initial blow found themselves stuck in a substance that clung to their limbs and pulled them under whenever they moved. Rescuers from the Navy, the Red Cross, and the police waded into the mess to reach the injured, and they worked through the night. Twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty more were hurt. Horses that could not be freed were destroyed, and the harbor ran brown for months.

The aftermath became one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, hoping to avoid responsibility, but an auditor appointed by the court heard years of testimony and concluded that the structure had simply been too weak. Purity Distilling was ordered to pay damages, and the families of the victims received settlements. The case also helped push lawmakers toward stricter rules requiring engineers to approve and sign off on construction plans. Residents said for decades afterward that on hot summer days the neighborhood still smelled faintly of molasses.
