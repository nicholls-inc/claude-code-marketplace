A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything; it moves heat that already exists in the outdoor air into your house, and the reason this works on a cold day is that "cold" is only relative. Air at minus five Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy, since absolute zero is more than 270 degrees lower, and the machine's job is to collect some of it and deliver it indoors, much as a refrigerator pulls heat out of its cabinet and dumps it into your kitchen, only run in the other direction and pointed at the whole house.

The mechanism is a sealed loop of refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at a very low temperature, far below freezing at normal pressure. Outside, a fan blows air across a coil containing the cold refrigerant, which is kept colder than the air by controlling its pressure. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, heat flows into it naturally and it evaporates into a gas, even though the air feels bitter to you. A compressor then squeezes that gas, and compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor hotter than your radiators or underfloor circuit need to be, typically somewhere around 40 to 55 degrees for a domestic system. That hot gas passes through a second coil indoors, where it gives up its heat to the water in your heating circuit or to the air in your rooms and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops the pressure, which chills the refrigerant again, and the cycle repeats. Heat never flows from cold to hot on its own, which is why the compressor, driven by electricity, is essential, but it is moving heat rather than creating it, and that distinction is the source of the economics.

The measure you will see quoted is the coefficient of performance, the ratio of heat delivered to electricity consumed. A gas boiler cannot exceed about 90 to 95 percent efficiency, so each unit of gas yields slightly less than one unit of heat, whereas a heat pump commonly delivers three units of heat or more for each unit of electricity, because two of those units were collected free from the outside air. That ratio falls as the weather gets colder, because the compressor must lift the heat across a larger temperature gap, so a pump that manages four on a mild day might manage two and a half at minus ten. Modern units using inverter-driven compressors and refrigerants suited to low temperatures keep working well below freezing, and models designed for Nordic climates are rated down to minus 25 or lower, though their output and efficiency do drop. The outdoor coil can also frost up in damp, near-freezing conditions, and the unit periodically runs a short defrost cycle to clear it, which costs a little energy and is normal.

For your decision, several practical points matter more than the physics. Heat pumps work best delivering gentle, steady warmth at low flow temperatures, so a well-insulated house with generous radiators or underfloor heating suits them best, while a draughty house with small, old radiators designed for water at 70 degrees or more may need insulation work or some larger radiators to reach the same comfort efficiently. You will probably also need a hot water cylinder, which many homes with combi boilers do not have, and space for it can be a real constraint. The running cost comparison depends on your local electricity and gas prices: at a ratio of three units of heat per unit of electricity, the pump wins if electricity costs less than about three times as much as gas per kilowatt-hour, and where electricity is relatively dear that margin narrows or disappears, although time-of-use tariffs and solar panels can improve it. Installation is expensive, so check what grants or subsidies apply to you, and ask installers for a heat loss calculation room by room, not a rule of thumb, because correct sizing is what separates a quiet, economical system from a disappointing one. A good installer will also tell you honestly if your house needs fabric improvements first, and that advice is worth following.
