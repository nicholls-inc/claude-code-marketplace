The Pump on Broad Street

On the last night of August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded working district of London. It struck with a speed that terrified even a city used to epidemics. People who were well at breakfast were dead by nightfall, drained by violent diarrhoea and vomiting. Within ten days more than five hundred people living within a few minutes' walk of Broad Street had died. Families who could leave did, and whole streets fell quiet.

Most doctors of the day believed they knew the cause. Cholera, like other epidemic diseases, was thought to spread through "miasma": foul air rising from rotting waste, sewers and overcrowded graveyards. The theory seemed sensible, because the poorest and smelliest districts suffered most.

John Snow, a physician who lived a short walk from the outbreak, thought the theory was wrong. Snow was the son of a labourer from York and had made his name as a pioneer of anaesthesia; he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. Five years earlier he had published an essay arguing that cholera was carried not by air but by something swallowed, most often in water contaminated by the waste of earlier victims. His reasoning was simple. Cholera attacks the gut, not the lungs, so the poison probably entered through the mouth. Few of his colleagues were persuaded.

The Soho outbreak, almost on his doorstep, gave him a chance to test the idea. He suspected at once the public pump on Broad Street, a popular well whose water was thought better than most. When he looked at a sample, though, he saw little wrong with it. He needed another kind of evidence.

So he began counting. From the office that registered deaths he obtained a list of 83 people who had died of cholera in the district in the first days of the outbreak. Then he walked the streets, knocking on doors and asking the bereaved where the dead had got their drinking water. Nearly all the deaths, he found, were in houses for which the Broad Street pump was the nearest source of water. Of the ten victims who had lived closer to a different pump, five came from families who preferred the Broad Street water and always sent for it, and three were children who went to school near the pump.

The exceptions turned out to be as revealing as the pattern. A workhouse stood around the corner, housing more than five hundred of the poor, yet only five of its inmates died. It had its own well. At a brewery on Broad Street itself, none of the seventy or so workers died. The men were given an allowance of beer, the owner told Snow, and the brewery too had a well of its own.

The strangest case came from Hampstead, a village miles away on the hills north of London, where there was no cholera at all. A widow there had died of it, and so had her niece, who had been visiting. Snow learned from the widow's son that she had once lived on Broad Street and liked the taste of its water so much that a cart brought her a large bottle of it every day. The water had been drawn on 31 August. She drank it and died two days later. Bad Soho air could not have reached Hampstead, but a bottle of Soho water had.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow took his findings to the local officials responsible for the parish. They were doubtful, but they had nothing better to try, and the next day they had the handle removed from the pump so that no one could draw water from it.

The story is often told as though that single act ended the epidemic. Snow knew better and said so. Deaths were already falling by then, largely because so many residents had fled. Removing the handle may have prevented a second wave, but it did not settle the argument.

Further evidence came from a man who had set out to disagree with him. Henry Whitehead, a local curate who knew the neighbourhood's families well, doubted the pump theory and began his own inquiries. They changed his mind. He traced the first case to a baby who had fallen ill at 40 Broad Street, the house beside the pump, just before the outbreak. Her mother had rinsed the soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspool at the front of the house. When the ground was dug up, the cesspool's brick lining was found to be decayed and leaking, less than three feet from the well.

Snow later plotted the deaths on a street map, marking each with a small black bar. The bars piled up thickly around the pump and thinned with distance from it. That map became one of the most famous images in medicine.

Official opinion still rejected his conclusion, and the pump handle was soon replaced. Snow died in 1858, at forty-five, before his theory was widely accepted. Acceptance came over the following decades, as London rebuilt its sewers and scientists identified the cholera germ under the microscope. Snow never saw the germ. He found its route by asking people where they got their water and counting what they told him, and that method is one reason he is remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the study of how disease moves through populations.
