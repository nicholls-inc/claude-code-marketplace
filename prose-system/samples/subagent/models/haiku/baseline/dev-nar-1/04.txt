The Pump Handle on Broad Street

In the last days of August 1854, a disease struck the streets of Soho, in central London, with a speed that frightened everyone who saw it. Cholera caused violent vomiting and a rapid loss of body fluid. Healthy adults could be dead within a day. Within a few weeks, more than 500 people living around Broad Street had died. Most doctors and officials believed the illness spread through bad air, an idea called miasma theory. John Snow, a physician who had already made his name by giving anaesthetic to surgical patients and to Queen Victoria during childbirth, believed something else. He thought cholera travelled in the water people drank.

Snow had argued this since 1849, when he published a pamphlet suggesting that the disease entered the body by mouth. Few colleagues were persuaded. He needed evidence, and the Soho outbreak gave him the chance to collect it.

He started by walking the district. He spoke to residents, shopkeepers and parish officials, and he gathered details of each death: the address, the date the illness began, and where the person usually got their water. He then marked each fatal case on a street map with a small bar placed at the house where it occurred. The bars bunched together thickly around one spot, a public water pump on Broad Street, now Broadwick Street.

A cluster on a map was suggestive, but it did not prove anything, so Snow kept testing it. Some people who lived very close to the pump never caught the disease. He wanted to know why. He learned that a brewery nearby had its own well, and its workers drank beer rather than water. A workhouse a short distance away, which had its own supply, recorded very few deaths. Snow then made what he considered his strongest comparison. Two water companies supplied houses in the same part of south London, with their pipes running side by side. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company drew its water from the Thames below the point where sewage flowed into it. The Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream, away from the city's waste. Households supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall died of cholera at several times the rate of those supplied by Lambeth. Nature, in effect, had run a controlled experiment, and the water was the only consistent difference.

Snow next had to persuade the authorities. He argued to the local parish board that the Broad Street pump was the source. Many officials were unconvinced, but on 8 September the handle was removed from the pump at his urging, and residents were told to fetch water from elsewhere. The timing complicates the story. The outbreak was already declining by then, and many of the sickest had already died or fled. Snow admitted this uncertainty in his writing, and that candour is part of why his account is still trusted.

Snow's case grew stronger later. The Reverend Henry Whitehead, a local clergyman who had initially doubted Snow's theory, investigated the neighbourhood in greater depth. He found that a cesspit, a pit for human waste, lay only a few feet from the pump's well, and that it had been leaking into the water supply. He also traced an infant's soiled nappy, reportedly rinsed in that cesspit, to the first death of the outbreak. Whitehead's findings helped turn Snow's suspicion into a documented chain of events.

Snow's ideas still took years to win full acceptance. His 1855 book, On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, expanded his argument, but many doctors continued to blame the air. The cause was not confirmed until 1883, when Robert Koch identified the bacterium that causes cholera. Snow had been right without knowing what the organism was. He had shown that careful counting, mapping and comparison could reveal the source of a disease. London's sewer system, built in the following decade under Joseph Bazalgette, eventually removed waste from the water supply, and cholera stopped being a recurring threat to the city.

The Broad Street map remains one of the best-known pieces of public health evidence. It shows how a question can be answered by looking closely at ordinary facts, such as which house had which pump and who drank from it.
