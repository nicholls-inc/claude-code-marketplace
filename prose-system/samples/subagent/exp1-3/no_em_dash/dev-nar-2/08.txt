The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of three earlier flights and was making his second trip to lunar space. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was a rookie. The command module pilot, Jack Swigert, was a late substitute. Ken Mattingly had been grounded days earlier after being exposed to German measles, and Swigert had trained for the role as a backup. Americans had landed on the Moon twice already, and the public had begun to treat the journey as routine. Television networks did not even bother to carry the crew's broadcast live.

The crew had named their command module Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. For two days nothing went wrong. The spacecraft coasted outward, about 200,000 miles from home, and the men settled into the ordinary work of a long flight.

At about nine in the evening Houston time on April 13, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module. The tanks held supercold liquids, and a quick stir kept the contents from settling and made the gauges read accurately. Swigert flipped the switch. A few moments later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights lit up. Lovell, looking at the instruments, spoke the line that became famous in a slightly altered form: "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause lay years in the past. Oxygen tank number two had been damaged during ground handling before it was ever installed. Later, a test at the launch site left heaters running far hotter than intended, and the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank was scorched. Nobody knew. When Swigert's switch sent current through those bare wires, a spark ignited the insulation in the pure oxygen. Pressure surged, and the tank ruptured, blowing off a panel of the service module. The second oxygen tank soon lost its contents as well. The fuel cells that made electricity and water from oxygen began to fail, and Odyssey was dying.

Aboard the ship, the crew understood quickly that a lunar landing was out of the question. The question now was whether they could survive at all. Odyssey's batteries would be needed for the final return to Earth and could not be drained in the meantime. The only other source of power and air was Aquarius, a craft built to hold two men for about two days on the lunar surface. It would have to sustain three men for four.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams in Houston worked through the night. The ship was on a path that would swing it around the Moon and, if nothing were done, send it back toward Earth, but not on a course that would bring it safely down. The engineers decided to fire Aquarius's descent engine to correct this. Lovell and Haise moved into the lander, and Swigert shut down the command module piece by piece, a careful shutdown that would later need to be reversed.

The ship passed behind the Moon, and the astronauts saw the far side with their own eyes while knowing they were not going to land. Lovell later said they had little time for sightseeing. About two hours after their closest approach, they fired the descent engine again, a longer burn that sped their return and moved the landing site to the Pacific Ocean.

Then came the long days of cold and thirst. To save power, almost everything was turned off, and the cabin temperature fell to about 38 degrees Fahrenheit. Water was rationed to a few ounces per man per day, and the crew became dehydrated. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. Condensation dripped on the walls. Sleep was nearly impossible, and the crew huddled in their flight suits. Lovell lost about 14 pounds.

A second crisis came from the air itself. Exhaled carbon dioxide was building to dangerous levels. Aquarius had canisters of lithium hydroxide to absorb it, but they were built for two men and were filling up. Odyssey had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lander's system took round ones. On the ground, engineers gathered the items the crew had on board, such as plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, suit hoses, and duct tape, and worked out a way to fit a square canister into a round hole. They read the instructions to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the adapter, nicknamed the mailbox, and the carbon dioxide readings began to fall.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the man who had been left behind, spent long hours in a simulator in Houston. The engineers faced a problem no one had planned for: restarting a spacecraft that had been powered down, using only the small amount of battery power available, and doing it in a sequence that could not be tested safely on the real vehicle. Mattingly worked with them to write that procedure, trying it again and again until it held up.

Navigation was another worry. The ship had drifted slightly, and a final correction burn was needed to keep the entry angle within narrow limits. Too steep and the capsule would burn up. Too shallow and it would skip off the atmosphere into space. With the guidance system shut down to save power, the crew steered by hand, lining up the ship using the sunlit edge of the Earth as a reference. The burn worked.

Before entry, the crew separated the service module, which had been left attached until the end. As it drifted away, they saw for the first time how bad the damage was. An entire panel was gone, and the side of the module was torn open. Lovell reported it to Houston in shaken tones. Then they said goodbye to Aquarius, which had saved their lives, and released it. Odyssey, alone, was powered back up on its small battery reserves.

On April 17, the capsule met the atmosphere. Radio silence is normal during reentry because of the superheated plasma around the spacecraft, and it usually lasts about three minutes. This time the silence stretched on. Controllers in Houston stared at the screens. Then, after about four minutes, Swigert's voice came through, and the room erupted. Three parachutes opened, and the capsule dropped into the South Pacific near Samoa shortly after noon, only a few miles from the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted nearly 143 hours.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, yet it came to be seen as one of NASA's finest hours. A review board traced the accident to the faulty tank and its wiring, and the design was changed for later flights. Lovell and his crew had gone to the Moon, in a sense, only to find that the journey back was the harder part, and that the people on the ground and the men in the cabin could solve it together.
