How Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

The problem

Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met. They want to talk privately, but the only way to reach each other is a line that anyone can listen to. A snooper, whom we will call Eve, hears every word they say. It sounds impossible for Alice and Bob to end up with a secret that Eve does not know. If Eve hears everything, surely she knows everything.

For most of history, that was true. Secret codes needed a shared key, a secret that both sides already held. You would meet in person, swap the key, and only then talk safely. That works for spies and generals, but it fails for a shopper who wants to send a card number to a website they have never visited before. In the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around the problem. The idea is called public-key cryptography, and it rests on a simple fact: some jobs are easy to do and very hard to undo.

Easy to do, hard to undo

Mix a pot of yellow paint with a pot of blue paint and you get green. Now try to un-mix the green. You cannot get the yellow and blue back out. Mixing is easy, and unmixing is practically impossible.

Here is the paint trick, which captures the whole idea. Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow. Eve hears this, and that is fine. Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red, and mixes it with the yellow. She sends the mixture to Bob. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, say blue, mixes it with the yellow, and sends that mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures travel past, but she cannot unmix them to find the private colours.

Now Alice takes the mixture she received from Bob, which is yellow plus blue, and adds her private red. Bob takes the mixture he received from Alice, which is yellow plus red, and adds his private blue. Both end up with yellow, red and blue together, the same final colour. Eve has only the yellow and the two partial mixtures. To get the final colour she would need one of the private colours, and those never left the owners' hands.

Paint is only a picture. Real computers need something that behaves the same way, and school maths supplies it.

Clock arithmetic

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, because the clock wraps around after 12. Mathematicians call this working modulo 12. You can do the same with any wrap-around number. We will use 23, so that after reaching 22 the count goes back to 0. The result of dividing by 23 and keeping only the remainder is the answer we want.

The easy job is repeated multiplication. Take a base number, say 5, and multiply it by itself a chosen number of times, wrapping around at 23 whenever the total gets too big. For example, 5 multiplied by itself 6 times is 15625. Dividing 15625 by 23 gives 679 with a remainder of 8. So the answer is 8. A computer does this in a blink, even with enormous numbers.

The hard job is going backwards. Suppose someone tells you that 5 multiplied by itself some number of times gives 8 on the 23 clock. How many times was it? With numbers this small, you could try 1, 2, 3 and so on until you hit it. But if the wrap-around number has hundreds of digits, the list of things to try is so long that all the computers on Earth could not finish before the sun burns out. Going forward is quick. Going backward is hopeless. That gap is the whole secret.

The exchange, with numbers

Alice and Bob announce two public numbers: the wrap-around number 23 and the base 5. Eve hears both.

Alice privately picks 6 and tells no one. She works out 5 multiplied by itself 6 times on the 23 clock, which is 8, as we saw. She sends 8 to Bob.

Bob privately picks 15. He works out 5 multiplied by itself 15 times on the 23 clock, which comes to 19. He sends 19 to Alice.

Eve now knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. She does not know 6 or 15.

Now Alice takes Bob's number, 19, and multiplies it by itself 6 times, her private number, wrapping at 23. The squares go 19 times 19 is 361, which leaves 16. Working through the rest gives the final answer 2.

Bob takes Alice's number, 8, and multiplies it by itself 15 times, his private number, wrapping at 23. After the same sort of working, he also gets 2.

Both have arrived at 2, and neither ever sent it. The reason they match is a rule you know from school: multiplying a number by itself 6 times and then 15 times is the same as multiplying it by itself 15 times and then 6 times. The order does not matter. Alice's route and Bob's route are two ways of multiplying 5 by itself 6 times 15 times, which is 90 times in total. They take different roads to the same place.

Why Eve is stuck

To get the 2, Eve would need either 6 or 15. To find 6, she would have to look at the number 8 and work out how many times 5 was multiplied to produce it. That is exactly the hard backward job. With tiny numbers like ours she could guess her way through in seconds. Real systems use wrap-around numbers hundreds of digits long, so guessing becomes impossible in practice.

The secret 2 is then used as a key for an ordinary fast code, the kind where both sides scramble messages with the same shared number. The slow, clever maths is used only once, to set up the key. After that, the cheap shared-key code does the daily work.

The padlock version

There is a second, closely related idea that explains the word public in public-key. Picture Bob making a thousand identical open padlocks and posting them to anyone who asks. He keeps the only key. Alice takes one open padlock, puts her message in a box, snaps the padlock shut, and sends the box. Anyone can get a padlock, and anyone can see the box in transit, but only Bob's key opens it.

In real life the padlock is a public number and the key is a private number, and the two are linked by a hard backward job. The best known version multiplies two enormous prime numbers together. Multiplying them is easy. Taking the result and finding the two original primes is the hard part, much like unmixing paint. Bob publishes the product, which acts as the padlock, and keeps the primes, which act as the key.

What is really being trusted

Nothing here is magic, and it is worth being honest about the limits. The security does not come from hiding the method. The method is published in textbooks, and that openness is a strength, because thousands of experts have tried to break it. The security comes from the gap between easy and hard, and nobody has proved that the gap can never be closed. It has simply held up for decades.

There is also a practical wrinkle. Eve could do something sneakier than listening. She could cut the line and pretend to be Bob to Alice and pretend to be Alice to Bob, passing messages between them while making a separate secret with each. The maths alone cannot stop this. Real systems add a way to prove who you are, using certificates vouched for by trusted organisations, so that Alice can be confident the number she received really came from Bob.

The takeaway

Two strangers can agree a secret in public because they share only numbers that are useless without a private ingredient. Each person mixes something secret into something public, they swap the mixtures, and each adds their own secret again. The order of mixing does not change the result, so both land on the same answer, while the listener, who cannot unmix, is left with nothing. Every time a padlock appears in your browser, that same idea is working quietly behind it.
