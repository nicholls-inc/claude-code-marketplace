A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists outside and delivers it indoors, and because even cold air holds a great deal of thermal energy, the fact that it is colder outside than inside is not an obstacle. Heat is simply the motion of molecules, and that motion persists far below freezing; air at minus five Celsius still has plenty of it, since the true zero point is down around minus 273. What matters is that heat flows naturally from warm things to cold things, so to pull it from cold outdoor air into a warm house you need a machine that works against that natural direction, much as a pump lifts water uphill.

The machine does this with a refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at very low temperatures, well below anything you would find in a British winter. In the outdoor unit the refrigerant circulates through a coil at a pressure low enough that its boiling point sits below the outside air temperature, so as air is blown across the coil the refrigerant absorbs heat and evaporates. A compressor then squeezes this vapour, and compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, in the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor hotter than your radiators need to be, typically somewhere between 50 and 80 degrees. That hot vapour passes through a second coil indoors, where it gives up its heat to the water in your heating circuit and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops the pressure again, the liquid turns cold, and the cycle repeats continuously.

The reason this is attractive rather than merely clever is efficiency. The electricity you pay for runs the compressor and fans but does not create the heat itself, so for each unit of electricity the system delivers roughly three to four units of heat over a typical winter, a ratio called the coefficient of performance, or seasonal COP when averaged across the year. A gas boiler, by comparison, converts at best about 90 to 95 percent of the fuel's energy into heat and can never exceed one unit of heat per unit of fuel. The ratio falls as the weather gets colder, because the compressor has to lift the heat across a bigger temperature gap, so on a very cold night you might see a COP nearer two, but it remains above one, and that is why the technology works at all.

For your decision, the practical questions are less about physics than about your house and your bills. Heat pumps deliver water at lower temperatures than a boiler, so they work best with large radiators, underfloor heating, or a well-insulated home, and an older house with small radiators and poor insulation may need upgrades to be comfortable, which adds cost and should be priced before you commit. The installation itself is more expensive than a boiler swap, though grants such as the Boiler Upgrade Scheme in England and Wales have reduced the upfront figure by several thousand pounds, so check what is currently available. Running costs depend on the gap between electricity and gas prices; because electricity usually costs about three to four times as much per kilowatt hour as gas, a heat pump with a COP of three only roughly breaks even on fuel cost, and it comes out ahead if you can use a cheaper time-of-use tariff or generate your own solar power. You will also need space outside for the unit, and usually an indoor hot water cylinder.

If your home is reasonably insulated, you have room for the equipment, and you plan to stay for several years, a heat pump is likely to cut your carbon emissions substantially and could cut your bills too. If the house is draughty and the radiators are small, the sensible first step is to insulate and survey the system, and then ask an installer for a proper heat loss calculation before you decide.
