On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Dockworkers were eating lunch, children were playing near the waterfront, and firemen were relaxing at a nearby station house. Looming over the neighborhood stood a steel tank about fifty feet tall and nearly 90 feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held more than two million gallons of crude molasses, which was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, and, with Prohibition about to take effect, into the raw material for munitions as well.

The tank had never been trusted by the people who lived beside it. It had been built quickly in 1915, with little oversight and thin steel plates, and the company's managers seemed more interested in speed and profit than in safety. Nobody had run a proper test by filling it with water to check for leaks, and the tank leaked so badly that molasses oozed down its sides. Rather than repair it, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. Neighborhood children sometimes collected the sweet syrup from the seams in tin cups.

Just after 12:30 that afternoon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a deep rumbling, followed by a sound like machine-gun fire as the rivets shot out of the steel. Then the tank burst, and a wave of molasses perhaps twenty-five feet high at its crest rushed out into the streets at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. The wave was heavy, about 26 million pounds of it, and it moved with enough force to knock buildings off their foundations. It buckled a section of the elevated railway, and it swept up wagons, horses, and people as if they weighed nothing.

Those caught in the flood faced a terrible kind of danger. The molasses was cold enough to thicken quickly, and as it did, it became almost impossible to move through. Rescuers waded in up to their knees and struggled to reach survivors who were pinned in wreckage or trapped beneath the syrup. Sailors from a docked ship, the USS Nantucket, rushed to help, along with soldiers, police officers, and Red Cross workers. In the end, twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Among the dead were children, a city worker, and several men who were simply doing their jobs.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews used saltwater from fire boats and covered the streets in sand to break up the sticky mess, and the harbor ran brown until summer. Residents said the smell lingered for years, and some claimed that the area still smelled of molasses on hot days.

The legal aftermath proved to be as important as the disaster. The company insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank, but after a long civil trial, a court-appointed auditor found that the tank had been structurally unsound and the company negligent. Victims' families eventually received settlements, in a case that became one of the first major class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The disaster also pushed the state to demand stricter building codes and engineering oversight, so that architects and engineers had to certify their plans before construction began.
