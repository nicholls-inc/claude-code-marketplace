The Great Molasses Flood

On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unusually mild. The temperature had climbed into the low forties, and people walked the streets without heavy coats. On Commercial Street, beside the harbor, stood a steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It was about fifty feet tall and held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The sugary syrup was bound for distilleries, where it would be turned into alcohol. The United States needed that alcohol for explosives during the First World War.

The tank had a poor record. It had been built quickly and never properly tested. Its seams leaked, and workers had complained for months. The company reportedly painted the tank brown to hide the seepage, and nobody ordered it emptied or repaired.

Shortly after noon, the tank gave way. Witnesses heard a noise like machine-gun fire as the rivets popped. A wall of molasses, later estimated at twenty-five feet high and moving perhaps thirty-five miles an hour, burst down the street. It knocked buildings off their foundations, twisted the iron supports of the elevated railway, and flipped wagons and carts. Horses were trapped and drowned. People who had been walking to lunch or working nearby were swept off their feet and carried into the sticky tide.

Molasses is dense and clings to everything, so escape was very hard. Rescuers waded through the goo, sometimes up to their waists, and pulled out the injured. Sailors from the nearby navy yard and workers from the docks joined local residents in the effort. Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were hurt. Some of the dead were never found until the mess was cleared.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews used sand, salt water, shovels and fire hoses to scrape the streets and sidewalks. Molasses soaked into the harbor, turned the water brown, and killed fish. Even after the sand and seawater had done their work, the district smelled of syrup for years, especially on warm summer days.

In the aftermath, the company blamed anarchists and suggested that a bomb had caused the collapse. Investigators did not find evidence for that claim. Lawsuits followed, and the case dragged on for years. In 1925, a judge ruled that the company had been negligent in building and maintaining the tank. The owners paid out settlements over the following years.

The disaster changed how Boston thought about industrial safety, and it became a lasting local legend. Neighbors told stories about the day the molasses came down the street. The flood was a reminder that a company's shortcuts could hurt the people living in its shadow.
