The Pump on Broad Street

In the last days of August 1854, people in Soho began to die with terrifying speed. Soho was then one of the most crowded corners of London, a tangle of narrow streets where families lived a room apiece above workshops, slaughterhouses and cowsheds, and where the cellars beneath many houses held brimming cesspools. On the night of 31 August, cholera struck. Its victims were seized by violent diarrhoea and vomiting, their bodies drained of fluid so quickly that a person healthy at breakfast could be dead by nightfall. Within three days, 127 people living on or near Broad Street had died. Within ten days the toll passed 500. Those who could flee did, and within a week most of the neighbourhood's residents had gone, leaving shuttered shops and silent streets.

Almost everyone at the time believed they knew the cause. Cholera, like most epidemic disease, was blamed on "miasma": foul air rising from rotting matter, sewers and filth. The theory seemed like common sense. Poor districts stank, and poor districts suffered. Germs as a cause of disease were decades from being accepted.

John Snow thought otherwise. A physician of forty-one, the son of a labourer from York, he had made his name as a pioneer of anaesthesia, and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth the year before. He was a quiet, methodical man, and he had been puzzling over cholera for years. If the disease came from bad air, he reasoned, it ought to attack the lungs. Instead it attacked the gut. That suggested something swallowed, not breathed. In 1849 he had published an essay arguing that cholera spread through water contaminated by the waste of its victims. Few were persuaded.

Snow lived only a short walk from Broad Street. When the outbreak began he went straight to the district, and his suspicion fell at once on the public pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street. The pump was popular; its water was cool and thought to taste better than that of others nearby. Snow took samples and examined them, but saw little that settled the matter. The water looked clear enough. He would have to find his proof another way.

So he counted the dead. He obtained from the General Register Office a list of the cholera deaths in the area, with addresses, and then walked from house to house asking the bereaved where the dead had got their drinking water. The pattern emerged quickly. Nearly all the deaths had occurred in homes for which the Broad Street pump was the nearest source. Of the deaths in houses closer to another pump, several families told him they had fetched water from Broad Street anyway because they preferred it, or that their children drank from it on the way to school.

The exceptions proved as telling as the rule. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street, hemmed in by houses where people were dying, held more than 500 inmates, yet only a handful fell ill. The workhouse had its own well. At the brewery on Broad Street itself, none of the seventy or so workers died. The men were given an allowance of beer, and the brewery too had a well of its own; they never touched the pump.

Then there was the strangest case of all. A widow living in Hampstead, miles from Soho and in healthy air, had died of cholera, along with a niece visiting her. Snow found that the widow had once lived on Broad Street and liked its water so much that she had a large bottle carted out to her every day. The bottle delivered on 31 August had killed her.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow put his findings before the local parish board. The officials were not convinced by his theory, but they were desperate, and the next day they had the handle taken off the pump. The outbreak was already waning by then, since so many residents had fled, and Snow himself was careful to say so. But the well could spread no more disease.

Snow later drew a map of the district, marking each death as a small black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars stacked up thickly around the pump and thinned away with distance. It remains one of the most famous images in the history of medicine.

The source of the contamination was found the following year by an unlikely ally. Henry Whitehead, a local curate who had set out to disprove Snow, traced the first case to a baby at 40 Broad Street. Her mother had rinsed the child's soiled nappies into a cesspool that lay barely three feet from the well. Its brickwork was leaking.

The authorities soon replaced the pump handle, and miasma theory lingered for years. Snow died in 1858, at forty-five, before his view was accepted. But his method of tracking a disease by patiently counting who fell ill, where, and what they had in common, became the foundation of modern epidemiology.
