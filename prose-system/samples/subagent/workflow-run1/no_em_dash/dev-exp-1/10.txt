A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists outside and brings it indoors. That is why it can warm a house even when the air outside is colder than the air inside.

**Cold air still holds heat**

Temperature measures how fast molecules are moving, and nothing is truly without heat until about minus 273 degrees Celsius. Air at minus 5 degrees Celsius still contains a great deal of thermal energy. The trick is to collect it and deliver it somewhere useful.

**The refrigerant cycle**

Heat naturally flows from warm to cold, never the other way. A heat pump gets around this by using a fluid called a refrigerant and a compressor, much like a fridge running in reverse. Here is the loop:

1. Outside, the refrigerant circulates through a coil. It is kept so cold, often well below the outdoor temperature, that heat flows from the outside air into it. The refrigerant boils into a gas as it absorbs that heat.
2. The compressor squeezes this gas. Compressing a gas makes it hot, in the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. The gas may now reach 50 to 70 degrees Celsius, well above room temperature.
3. Inside the house, this hot gas passes through another coil, which could feed radiators, underfloor heating or a hot water cylinder. Because the gas is hotter than the water or air it meets, heat flows into your home and the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid.
4. An expansion valve then drops the liquid's pressure, which chills it sharply, and the cycle starts again.

The compressor is the only part that really consumes energy, and it runs on electricity. Most of the heat you get is simply carried in from outside.

**Why this beats a boiler on efficiency**

A modern gas boiler converts roughly 85 to 95 percent of its fuel into heat. A heat pump typically delivers three to four units of heat for every one unit of electricity it uses. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance, or COP. A COP of 3 means 300 percent efficiency in plain terms, which sounds impossible until you remember that most of that heat was relocated, not created.

**What happens in a deep freeze**

Efficiency falls as the gap between outside and inside temperatures grows, because the pump must work harder. On a mild day, the COP might be 4. At minus 10 degrees Celsius, it might drop to around 2. Even so, that is still more efficient than a boiler. Modern cold-climate models keep working at temperatures of minus 20 degrees Celsius or lower, though some include an electric backup element for extreme spells.

**What to weigh before replacing your boiler**

- **Insulation.** Heat pumps deliver gentler, steadier warmth at lower flow temperatures. A well-insulated house gets the best results. A draughty one may need upgrades first.
- **Emitters.** Existing radiators may be too small for low-temperature heating. You may need larger radiators or underfloor heating in some rooms.
- **Upfront cost.** Installation usually costs more than a new boiler, though grants and subsidies in many regions narrow the gap. Check what is available where you live.
- **Running costs.** These depend on the price of electricity relative to gas. Where electricity is expensive, the efficiency advantage shrinks, so look at a time-of-use tariff if you can.
- **Space and noise.** The outdoor unit needs clear airflow and sits outside, producing a low hum similar to a fridge.
- **Carbon.** As the grid gets cleaner, a heat pump's emissions fall with it, while a gas boiler stays tied to fossil fuel.

**The bottom line**

A heat pump can heat your home in winter because it moves heat rather than making it, using a compressor to lift the energy from cold outdoor air to a useful temperature. If your home is reasonably insulated and your boiler is near the end of its life, it is worth getting a proper survey. A good installer will size the system to your house and tell you honestly whether it suits you.
