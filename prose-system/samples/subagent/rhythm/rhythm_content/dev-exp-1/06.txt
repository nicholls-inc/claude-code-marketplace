A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything; it moves heat that already exists in the outdoor air into your house, and it can do this even when the air outside feels bitterly cold, because "cold" is only relative. Air at minus five degrees Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy, since the true zero point for heat is far lower, at roughly minus 273 degrees. The machine's job is to collect some of that energy and deliver it indoors, which is why it is often compared to a refrigerator running in reverse, drawing heat out of one space and releasing it into another.

The trick that lets heat travel from a colder place to a warmer one is a sealed loop containing a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures. Outside, a fan pulls air across a coil of this refrigerant, which is kept even colder than the air, so heat flows into it naturally and it evaporates into a gas. That gas then passes through a compressor, which squeezes it, and squeezing a gas makes it hot, in the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. The refrigerant leaves the compressor much hotter than your radiators or underfloor pipes need to be, so inside the house it passes through another coil where it gives up its heat to your heating water and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops its pressure, chilling it again, and the cycle repeats. The compressor, driven by electricity, is the only part doing real work, and the work is spent on moving heat and raising its temperature rather than on creating it.

This is why heat pumps are described as more than one hundred percent efficient, which sounds impossible until you see that they are not converting electricity into heat but using it to shift much more heat than the electricity itself contains. A good modern unit might deliver three units of heat for every one unit of electricity it consumes in mild weather, a ratio called the coefficient of performance. A gas boiler, even a new condensing model, can never exceed about ninety to ninety-five percent, because it can only release the energy locked in the fuel.

The cost to you is that performance falls as the gap between outside and inside temperatures grows. On a freezing night the same unit might manage a ratio nearer two than three, since the compressor has to lift the heat across a larger temperature difference, and some older or cheaper models struggle badly below minus ten or so. Cold-climate models designed for Nordic conditions keep working well below that, and in Scandinavia, where heat pumps are very common, they heat homes through winters far harsher than most of Britain or the northern United States ever sees. Outdoor coils can also frost up, so the unit periodically runs a short defrost cycle, which you may notice as a puff of steam and a brief pause in heating.

For your decision, the key questions concern your house and your tariff rather than the climate outside. Heat pumps produce water at lower temperatures than a boiler, typically thirty-five to fifty-five degrees rather than seventy or more, so they work best in a well-insulated home with radiators large enough to emit adequate heat at those temperatures, or with underfloor heating. Many homeowners need some insulation upgrades or a few bigger radiators, and a proper heat-loss survey by an installer should tell you how much. Running costs depend on the price ratio between electricity and gas: where electricity costs three times as much per kilowatt-hour as gas, a unit with a seasonal efficiency of three roughly breaks even on fuel, and anything better saves money, so a time-of-use tariff or solar panels can tip the balance. Installation costs more than a boiler, though grants in many countries cover a substantial part, and a heat pump also provides no-cost cooling in summer if it is a reversible air-to-air or suitable air-to-water model.

If your home is reasonably insulated and your boiler is near the end of its life, a heat pump is usually a sound replacement; if the house is draughty and uninsulated, fix that first.
