A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to the world's official clocks to keep them in step with the turning of the Earth.

To see why it exists, consider that there are two ways of measuring time. The older way is astronomical. A day is the time the Earth takes to spin once, and the sun's position in the sky sets the hour. The newer way is atomic. Since the 1950s, clocks that count the vibrations of atoms, usually caesium, have kept time with astonishing steadiness. They would drift by less than a second over hundreds of millions of years.

The trouble is that the Earth is a poor timekeeper by comparison. Its spin is slowed by the pull of the Moon's tides, and it is nudged by earthquakes, shifting ice, ocean currents and the churning of its molten core. Days are not all the same length, and over the long run they are getting slightly longer. Atomic time therefore slowly pulls ahead of the sky.

In 1972, the international community agreed a compromise called Coordinated Universal Time. It runs on atomic seconds, but whenever the gap with the Earth's rotation threatened to exceed 0.9 of a second, an extra second was inserted, usually at the end of June or December. On those days the last minute lasted 61 seconds. Twenty-seven have been added so far, the most recent at the end of 2016.

For decades this seemed a tidy solution. In practice it has been a headache. Modern life runs on computers, and computers dislike a minute with 61 seconds. Software often assumes that every minute has 60, and a surprise extra tick has caused outages and crashes at major websites and in financial systems, aviation and telecoms networks. Engineers have devised workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across a whole day, but different companies use different methods, which creates its own inconsistencies. The leap second also cannot be predicted far ahead, because it depends on the unruly behaviour of the planet. It is announced only a few months in advance.

There is a further twist. In recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the odd prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second would be removed. Nobody has ever tried this, and many experts fear that software would cope with it even less well.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. The gap between atomic and astronomical time will be allowed to grow for now, and the rule will be revisited later. The scientists involved expect it to reach about a minute, which will be far too small for anyone to notice in daily life. Sunrise will not visibly move. The change is aimed mainly at the machines that quietly keep modern society running.
