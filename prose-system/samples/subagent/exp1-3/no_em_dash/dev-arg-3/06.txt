Keep the Loops: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years a headline announces the death of handwriting. Keyboards are everywhere, the argument goes, so why should seven-year-olds spend precious classroom hours looping their letters together? Several American states dropped cursive from their curricula in the 2010s, and plenty of teachers in other countries quietly let it slide. The case for dropping it sounds modern and practical. It is also wrong. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and teach it properly, because it serves children's thinking, their fluency, their access to the written record, and their sense of themselves as writers. The skill is smaller than it looks, but the benefits are larger than the critics admit.

Start with what handwriting actually is. It is not merely a way of getting words onto a page. For a young child, it is one of the first complex fine-motor skills they master, and it is bound up with learning to read and spell. Researchers who have compared children learning letters by hand with those learning them by typing or tracing have repeatedly found that handwriting produces stronger letter recognition. Karin James, working at Indiana University, scanned the brains of pre-literate children and found that writing letters freehand activated the reading networks of the brain far more than typing them or tracing them did. Drawing a letter from memory forces the child to build a mental model of its shape. Pressing a key does not. If the goal of primary education is to produce confident readers and writers, handwriting is part of the machinery, not a decorative extra.

Now the obvious reply: that argument supports handwriting, not joined-up handwriting. Children could print, and stop there. This is the strongest objection, and it deserves a direct answer. The answer is that joined writing offers real advantages over print that go beyond tradition.

The first is speed and flow. Printed letters require the pen to lift after every character. Each lift is a small interruption, and a child writing a story in print makes hundreds of them. A joined script lets the hand move in a continuous rhythm, with whole words produced as a single motion. Once fluent, children can write faster and for longer without tiring. That matters because handwriting that is slow and laborious competes with thinking for the same limited mental resources. A child who must concentrate on forming each letter has less attention left for what they want to say. Studies of young writers, such as the long-running work of Virginia Berninger and colleagues in the United States, have found that automatic handwriting is linked to the quality and length of children's compositions. When the hand is on autopilot, the mind is free. Joined writing, taught well, is the route to that automaticity for most children.

The second advantage is spelling. Because a joined word is a single unbroken movement, the hand learns the word as a whole pattern, much as a pianist learns a phrase rather than a string of isolated notes. Teachers who use joined scripts from an early stage often report that common letter strings such as "ing", "tion" and "ough" become embedded in muscle memory. This is hard to prove with a tidy experiment, and I will not overclaim it. But the logic is sound, and it fits the broader evidence that spelling and movement reinforce each other.

The third advantage is legibility, which will surprise anyone who remembers their own scrawled cursive. Poorly taught joined writing is a mess. But well taught joined writing is often more legible than rushed print, because the connections between letters impose spacing and consistency. Printed letters in a hurry tend to float apart, change size, and lose their baseline. The problem with many adults' handwriting is not that it is joined; it is that nobody ever taught them how. That is an argument for better teaching, not for abandoning the skill.

Then there is the question of the written record. A great deal of the world is handwritten and joined: family letters, census returns, wartime diaries, recipe cards, the marginal notes in a grandparent's books. A generation that cannot read cursive is cut off from these documents unless someone transcribes them. This is a real cost, and it is already visible. Archivists in several countries report that young volunteers and even history students struggle with handwritten sources. Learning to read joined writing and learning to write it are closely linked; the child who can form the shapes finds them far easier to decipher. Teaching handwriting to every child keeps a door open to the past that would otherwise close.

The critics have a response: how many people actually handwrite anything in adult life? Few, perhaps, but not none, and the minority that remains is not trivial. People write cheques less, certainly, but they still sign documents, fill in forms, take notes in meetings, write birthday cards, leave messages for family, and sit exams. In many countries, exams are still handwritten, and a student who writes quickly and legibly has a measurable edge over one who does not. Research on note-taking, including the much-discussed work by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer, suggests that handwritten notes can promote deeper processing than typed ones, because the slower pace forces students to summarise rather than transcribe. The replication record on that particular study is mixed, so I would not rest the case on it. But the general point stands: handwriting is a tool that still does work, and children who lack it are poorer for it.

There is also a fairness argument that critics overlook. Children from homes with few devices, or without quiet space and parental support, depend on school to give them every basic tool. If handwriting instruction is withdrawn, the children most likely to lose out are the ones who will not pick it up elsewhere. Wealthier families will go on buying workbooks and hiring tutors, and fluent handwriting will become another quiet marker of advantage. Public education exists in part to prevent exactly that kind of gap.

I should address the strongest version of the opposing case honestly. Time in a primary timetable is finite, and every minute on loops and joins is a minute not spent on reading, mathematics, science, or learning to type. Keyboard skills are genuinely important, and children do need them. Some children, particularly those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties, find joined writing extremely hard, and forcing them through it can damage their confidence. These are serious points. But they do not defeat the case for teaching joined writing; they shape how to do it. Handwriting practice need not eat hours; short, frequent sessions of ten or fifteen minutes are more effective than long ones, and the skill can be woven into spelling and composition rather than set apart. Typing can be taught alongside it, since the two are not rivals. And for children who truly struggle, schools can offer adaptations, such as simplified joins, a different pen grip, or earlier access to a keyboard, without scrapping the entire approach for everyone. The existence of exceptions argues for flexibility, not abolition.

It is also worth saying what I am not arguing. I am not arguing for a nostalgic return to rows of identical copperplate, rulers across knuckles, and marks deducted for a wobbly capital G. The aim is a fluent, legible, personal hand, not a perfect one. Many adults end up with a style that mixes print and joins, and that is fine. What matters is that children are given the foundation, so that they can develop something that works for them. A child who has learned the joins can choose to drop them; a child who never learned them cannot choose to pick them up.

Finally, there is something harder to measure but worth naming. Handwriting is personal in a way typing is not. A handwritten note carries its author with it, in the pressure, the slant, the hurry or care visible on the page. Children take quiet pride in developing a handwriting of their own, and the first time a child is told that their writing is neat enough for the wall display, or the first time they write a card in their own hand to a grandparent, they are learning that writing is a form of communication between people. That sense of authorship is part of education too.

So the verdict is clear. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. It supports early literacy, frees mental capacity for composition, builds spelling through movement, keeps historical documents readable, and guards against a quiet inequality. The best objections are about time and about children with motor difficulties, and both are answered by teaching it efficiently and flexibly rather than dropping it. A skill that costs a few short sessions a week and repays children for the rest of their lives is not an outdated luxury. It is one of the better bargains in the curriculum, and we should not give it up because a keyboard happens to be nearby.
