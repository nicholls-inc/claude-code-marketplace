A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current, constant voltage, usually shortened to CC-CV. It has two phases, and the second explains why the last stretch is slow.

During charging, an external supply drives current through the cell. Lithium ions leave the cathode, which is typically a layered metal oxide, travel through the electrolyte, and insert into the graphite anode. This process is called intercalation. Electrons take the external circuit to balance the charge. The cell's terminal voltage is the sum of two terms. The first is the open-circuit voltage, which depends on how much lithium sits in each electrode. For a common chemistry it rises from about 3.0 V when empty to about 4.2 V when full. The second term is the current times the cell's internal resistance, which comes from ionic transport, charge transfer at the electrode surfaces, and contact resistance. This second term is why the terminal voltage during charging is always higher than the open-circuit voltage.

In the constant current phase, the charger holds a fixed current, for example 0.5 C, meaning half the rated capacity per hour. The terminal voltage climbs steadily as the electrodes fill. Most of the capacity, often 70 to 80 percent, goes in during this phase. It ends when the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, typically 4.2 V.

In the constant voltage phase, the charger holds the voltage at that limit and lets the current fall on its own. The limit is a safety boundary. Above it the cathode degrades, the electrolyte oxidizes, and metallic lithium can plate onto the anode, which creates a short circuit hazard. So the charger cannot push harder to finish faster.

The current tapers because of the relationship in the voltage equation. As the cell fills, its open-circuit voltage approaches the held limit. The gap between the two shrinks, and that gap is what drives current through the internal resistance. Current is roughly the gap divided by resistance, so it decays toward zero, approximately exponentially. Resistance also rises as the anode surface saturates, which adds to the effect. Inside the graphite, ions must diffuse inward from the surface. Near full charge the surface concentration is already high, so the concentration gradient driving diffusion is small, and the remaining sites deep in the particles fill slowly.

The charger ends the cycle when the current falls to a cutoff, commonly 0.05 C to 0.1 C. Waiting longer adds little capacity and extra time at high voltage accelerates aging. This tradeoff is why fast chargers deliver perhaps 80 percent quickly and then slow markedly.
