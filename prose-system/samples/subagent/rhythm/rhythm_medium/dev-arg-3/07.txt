Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case against it has become fashionable, and it is not foolish, but it rests on a narrow view of what a child is doing when she learns to write by hand. The strongest version of that case says that typing is now the dominant way adults put words on a page, that print letters are perfectly legible, and that the hours spent on looping connections would be better spent on reading, grammar or coding. I think this argument underestimates what cursive offers, overstates what is lost by teaching it, and quietly assumes that a skill is worthless unless it is used daily in office life. A good primary curriculum should still teach children to join their letters, and it should do so deliberately and early.

Begin with the practical objection, because it is the one that sounds most reasonable. Most adults spend far more time on keyboards and phone screens than with a pen, so why wait until children are seven or eight to teach a skill they will use only occasionally? The answer is that occasional use is not the same as no use. Adults still write shopping lists, exam answers, birthday cards, margin notes, forms and signatures, and a surprising number of professions depend on quick handwritten notes taken in places where a laptop is awkward or rude. A child who cannot write fluently by hand will struggle in exams, where handwriting remains the main way knowledge is tested in most countries, and where slow or illegible script can cost marks that have nothing to do with understanding. Fluency matters in exactly those moments, and it is built by instruction and practice rather than by osmosis.

The second point concerns speed, which is where joined writing earns its keep. Printed letters require the pen to leave the page after every stroke, so each word becomes a series of small starts and stops. Joined writing lets the hand travel across the page with fewer interruptions, and children who have learned it well tend to write faster without sacrificing legibility. That advantage looks small on a single line, but it compounds over a school day. A pupil who writes quickly can keep pace with her own thoughts, whereas a pupil who labours over each letter will drop ideas before they reach the paper. Research on handwriting fluency has repeatedly linked automatic letter formation to the quality and length of children's written composition, because a mind freed from the mechanics of forming letters has more attention left for the content of the sentence. If we want children to write well, we should make the physical act as effortless as we can, and a consistent joined style is one of the most dependable ways to do that.

A third argument is cognitive. A growing body of work, including studies using brain imaging and others comparing note-taking methods, suggests that writing by hand engages the brain differently from typing. When a child forms a letter with a pen, she must recall its shape, plan the movement and coordinate eye and hand, and this seems to strengthen her memory for the letter and for the idea she is recording. Typing, by contrast, asks only that she find the right key, and every letter takes the same small motion regardless of what it is. I would not claim that the science is settled, and some of the headline findings have been oversold in the press. Still, the broad direction of the evidence is consistent enough that a cautious school should hesitate before removing a practice that appears to support learning. Joined-up writing adds something more specific, because cursive demands continuous motor planning across whole words rather than single characters, and that rhythm of movement plausibly helps children to see words as wholes rather than as strings of unrelated marks. It is also worth noticing that spelling often improves when children write in a flowing hand, since the pattern of a word gets stored in the fingers as well as in the eye.

Critics sometimes reply that print can be taught to the same standard and that nothing is gained by making the leap to cursive. There is some truth in this, since a neat printed hand is entirely acceptable and many adults write that way happily. But the honest comparison is not between joined writing and perfect print, it is between joined writing and what children actually produce when they are left to develop their own style. Without explicit teaching, many children drift into an idiosyncratic mixture of printed and half-joined forms that is slow, uneven and hard for others to read. Teachers see this in secondary school every year, when pupils who never learned a consistent method are expected to write for hours in exams. Teaching one clear joined style gives every child the same foundation and the same chance to build speed on top of it. It also removes a quiet source of inequality, because children who do not get handwriting practice at home are precisely the ones who will not pick the skill up unaided.

There is also a point about children with learning differences that is too often ignored. For many pupils with dyslexia or dyspraxia, a joined style can actually be easier than print, because the continuous movement reduces the risk of reversing letters such as b and d and gives the hand a steady rhythm to follow. Occupational therapists and specialist teachers have long recommended cursive for exactly this reason, and some dyslexia organisations advise it from the start. Letters that are joined cannot be easily mistaken for one another in the way that isolated print letters can, and the spaces between words are clearer when the letters within each word are connected. A policy of dropping joined writing altogether would therefore take away a useful tool from the very children who need the most support, while offering no obvious benefit in return.

Beyond the practical and cognitive arguments, there is a cultural one that I do not think should be dismissed as sentimental. Handwriting is how people have signed their names, written letters to those they love and recorded their private thoughts for centuries. A child who cannot read cursive cannot read her grandmother's birthday card, an old family recipe or a historical document in an archive, and she is cut off from a large part of the written record of her own society. Teaching children to read and write joined script is a modest way of keeping that door open. A signature is also a small piece of personal identity, and a fluent, individual hand is something many children take real pride in. Pride of that kind should not be lightly traded away, especially at an age when children are forming their sense of what they are capable of mastering.

The best objection that remains is about time. The school day is crowded, and every minute spent practising loops and joins is a minute not spent on something else. This is a fair concern, but it is easier to answer than it looks. Handwriting practice does not need to be long, and short, regular sessions of ten or fifteen minutes are more effective than occasional marathons. Those sessions can be tied to spelling, vocabulary and the content of other lessons, so the practice doubles as reinforcement rather than competing with it. The time cost is also front-loaded, because once a child has a fluent hand, every later piece of writing becomes faster. A school that skimps on handwriting in the early years often pays for it later, with pupils who write slowly across the whole curriculum. If the curriculum is overloaded, the remedy is to trim what is least valuable, and the ability to put one's thoughts on paper quickly and legibly is not a candidate for the chop.

It is worth being clear about what I am not arguing. I do not think children should be drilled for hours with copybooks and penalised for imperfect loops, and I do not think keyboard skills should be neglected. Children need both, and a sensible school will teach typing alongside handwriting, probably from the later primary years when small hands are ready for it. Nor do I think a single approved style must be imposed on every adult for life, since many people modify their handwriting as they grow older and find a personal compromise between joined and printed forms. The point is that a child should be taught a joined hand first, so that she has a solid base from which to develop her own style, rather than being left to invent a method from scratch. Good teaching gives children a foundation and then lets them make it their own.

Finally, there is a danger in the way this debate is usually framed, as a contest between the past and the future. Teaching cursive is not a nostalgic gesture towards a vanished world of ink wells and blotting paper. It is a decision about how best to help young children become fluent, confident writers in a world that will demand both screens and pens of them. The evidence points, with reasonable consistency, to faster composition, better exam performance, stronger support for children with dyslexia and plausible benefits to memory and spelling. Against that stands the argument that most adults type most of the time, which is true but does not show that handwriting is dispensable. A skill can be useful without being universal, and it can be worth teaching even if it is not the main tool a child will use as an adult. Primary schools should therefore keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should do so with confidence rather than apology.
