On a mild January day in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with the ordinary noise of a working neighborhood. It was January 15, a Wednesday, and the temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit after several bitterly cold days. Dock workers, teamsters, and children were out in the winter sun. Looming over the waterfront on Commercial Street stood a huge steel tank, 50 feet tall and 90 feet wide, owned by the United States Industrial Alcohol Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, which was used in munitions and, soon, in bootleg liquor, as Prohibition was only days from taking effect. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply in 1915. Its walls were thin, and it was never properly tested. Neighbors had long noticed it groaning and leaking, and some had collected the brown syrup that seeped down its sides. The company's response was to paint the tank brown, so the stains would not show.

Around 12:30 in the afternoon, a fresh shipment had recently been pumped in, and the warmer air had thinned the contents and increased the pressure. Witnesses described a deep rumble, then a sound like machine-gun fire as rivets burst from the steel. The tank split open.

A wave of molasses roughly 25 feet high rushed out at around 35 miles per hour. It carried more than 2.3 million gallons with it. The surge tore the elevated railway track from its supports, lifted a firehouse off its foundation, and crushed freight cars and wooden homes as if they were toys. A boy later said it felt like the whole street had turned to brown water that would not let go.

The cold made everything worse. As the molasses cooled, it thickened, and it became like glue around those it had trapped. Rescuers waded in to their knees, and many victims could not be pulled free. Police, sailors from a nearby ship, Red Cross workers, and Boston Cadets rushed in. Nurses set up a makeshift hospital. Boston Harbor ran brown for weeks.

The disaster killed 21 people and injured about 150 more. Among the dead were children, a city worker, and several men who had been eating lunch near the firehouse. Searchers spent days looking for bodies, and the last was not found until months later. The smell of molasses lingered in the neighborhood for decades, and on hot summer days locals said they could still catch a whiff.

The lawsuit that followed was one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts. The company claimed anarchists had sabotaged the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court spent years hearing hundreds of witnesses and rejected that claim. He found that the tank had been poorly built, and in 1925 the company agreed to pay damages, about $628,000 in total. Families of the dead received roughly $7,000 each.

The flood changed how buildings are built. It helped lead to stricter rules requiring engineers to review and sign off on structural plans, and it pushed construction toward real testing and safety standards. A plaque near the site now tells the story of a day when a sweet, ordinary substance became a deadly force on a quiet winter afternoon.
