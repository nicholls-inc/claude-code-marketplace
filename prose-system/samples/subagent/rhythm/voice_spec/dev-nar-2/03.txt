Apollo 13 left Kennedy Space Center on 11 April 1970, carrying James Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert. It was the third mission planned to land men on the Moon. Lovell was the commander and had flown to the Moon once before, on Apollo 8. Haise was to be the lunar module pilot. Swigert had joined the crew only days earlier, replacing Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles by another astronaut and was thought likely to fall ill during the flight. The planned landing site was the Fra Mauro highlands. The command and service module was named Odyssey and the lunar module Aquarius.

The launch had one irregularity. The centre engine of the second stage shut down about two minutes early, because of vibration in the engine's fuel feed, and the other engines burned longer to make up the difference. The spacecraft reached its planned orbit, and for the next two days the flight was routine. The crew fired the engine that sent them toward the Moon, separated the command module from the rocket stage, turned it around and docked with the lunar module. On the evening of 13 April they broadcast a television programme from the cabin, which the major American networks did not carry.

The accident began with a fault that had been in place for years. The service module held two tanks of liquid oxygen, which supplied the crew's air and the fuel cells that generated electricity and water. Each tank contained a fan to stir the contents, and a heater to keep the oxygen at the right pressure. The second tank had been dropped by about two inches while it was being removed from another spacecraft in 1965, which probably damaged an internal fitting. Before the launch of Apollo 13, the tank was filled for a test and then had to be emptied, and the ground crew used its heater to boil the oxygen off. The heater was designed for a 28-volt supply, but the ground equipment supplied 65 volts, and the thermostat switches that should have limited the heat had fused shut. The heater ran for hours and burned away much of the Teflon insulation on the wires inside the tank. Nobody recognised this at the time, and the tank was cleared for flight.

At about 55 hours and 54 minutes into the mission, controllers in Houston asked the crew to stir the tanks. Swigert switched on the fans in the second tank. The bare wires sparked in the pure oxygen, and the tank burst about 90 seconds later. The crew heard a bang and felt a shudder. Swigert reported that they had had a problem, and Lovell repeated the words. The spacecraft was then about 200,000 miles from Earth. The blast tore away a panel on the side of the service module and damaged the first oxygen tank, which began to leak. Within hours both tanks were empty, two of the three fuel cells had stopped, and the command module was left with only its batteries and the oxygen in a small reserve tank.

The flight directors in Houston, among them Gene Kranz and Glynn Lunney, decided within a few hours that the landing was off. The aim became getting the crew home, and the lunar module offered the only practical shelter. It had its own batteries, oxygen and water, but had been built to support two men for about two days on the surface. It would now have to support three men for about four. Haise and Lovell moved into it and powered it up, while Swigert shut down the command module to save what remained of its batteries.

The spacecraft was on a path that would have missed the Earth, so the first task was to correct it. Controllers chose not to turn around at once, because the damaged service module engine could not be trusted. The crew instead used the lunar module's descent engine, first to steer back onto a path that returned to Earth, and later, after the spacecraft had gone around the far side of the Moon, to shorten the trip. That second burn, at about 79 hours, ran for just over four minutes and moved the expected splashdown from the Indian Ocean to the Pacific. On the way past the Moon the spacecraft came within about 250 kilometres of the surface and, at its greatest distance, was roughly 400,000 kilometres from Earth, further than any people had been before.

The crew then faced several shortages at once. The lunar module's batteries had to last until reentry, so the crew switched off nearly everything and ran the craft on a small fraction of its normal power. Water was rationed to about six ounces per man each day to keep the cooling system going, and the crew ate little. Without heat, the cabin temperature fell to around 3 degrees Celsius, and the men were cold and slept badly. Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection. The three men lost about 31 pounds between them over the course of the flight.

The most urgent problem was carbon dioxide. The lunar module had canisters of lithium hydroxide to absorb the gas, but they were sized for two men for two days, and the levels in the cabin rose toward a dangerous point. The command module had spare canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's system took round ones. Engineers on the ground built an adapter from items known to be on board: a cardboard cover from a flight manual, a plastic bag, a sock, a hose from a spacesuit and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew, who built the device, fitted it, and saw the carbon dioxide level fall.

The remaining problem was how to restart the command module for reentry. It had never been designed to be powered up after being shut down for days, and its batteries had to be conserved for the final hours. Mattingly, who had stayed on the ground, worked with engineers in a simulator to write a new start-up procedure that kept within the power available. A further course correction was made at about 105 hours, by hand and with the descent engine, because the spacecraft's guidance equipment had been switched off. Lovell lined up the burn by keeping the Earth's day-night boundary in a window.

On 17 April, about four hours before landing, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module. They saw for the first time that a whole panel was missing and that equipment inside was exposed. Later they moved back into Odyssey, started it using the new procedure and released Aquarius, which burned up over the Pacific. The command module entered the atmosphere with a communications blackout that lasted longer than expected, about 87 seconds beyond the planned period, and controllers waited without any word until Swigert's voice came through. Odyssey landed under parachutes in the South Pacific near Samoa at 12:07 pm Houston time, after 142 hours and 54 minutes. The crew were picked up by helicopter and taken to the USS Iwo Jima.

A review board led by Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank and its heater. The tanks were redesigned, the fans were removed, and a third oxygen tank was added to the service module. Apollo 14 flew in January 1971, and the programme went on to land twelve men on the Moon before it ended in 1972.
