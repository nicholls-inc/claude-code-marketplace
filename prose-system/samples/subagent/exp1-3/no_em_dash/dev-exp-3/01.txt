## The puzzle

Suppose you and I have never met. We want to share a secret, such as a password, so that we can send private messages. The only way to talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Everything I say, you hear, and so does every eavesdropper.

It sounds impossible. If the secret is spoken aloud, it is no longer secret. For most of history, people believed the only answer was to meet first in private and agree on a code. Armies and banks sent couriers carrying codebooks. That does not work for the modern internet, where you might need to share a secret with a shop you have never heard of, in a fraction of a second.

In the 1970s, researchers found a way around the problem. It rests on a simple idea: some jobs are easy to do and very hard to undo. Public-key cryptography is built on that gap.

## Easy one way, hard the other way

Think about mixing paint. Mixing yellow and blue to get green is easy. Looking at a pot of green and working out exactly which yellow and which blue went in is very hard, and you can never separate the colours again.

Here is how two people could use that to share a secret colour. First, Alice and Bob publicly agree on a starting colour, say yellow. Everyone can see it. Then each picks a private colour that they never reveal. Alice picks red and Bob picks blue.

Alice mixes her private red into the shared yellow and sends the result, an orange, across the open line. Bob mixes his private blue into the shared yellow and sends his result, a green. An eavesdropper sees yellow, orange and green. Now each adds their own private colour to what they received. Alice adds her red to Bob's green. Bob adds his blue to Alice's orange. Both end up with the same muddy mix of yellow, red and blue. The eavesdropper cannot make that colour. They would need to unmix orange or green to find a private colour, and that is the hard part.

Paint is only a picture. Computers use numbers, and the numbers need an operation that is easy forwards and hard backwards. School maths has one.

## Clock arithmetic

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You divide by 12 and keep the remainder. Mathematicians call this working "modulo 12". We can use any size of clock. A clock with 23 positions is "modulo 23". In it, 25 becomes 2, because 25 is 23 plus 2.

Now take powers. 5 to the power 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. On a 23 clock, 125 is 5 lots of 23 (which is 115) plus 10, so the answer is 10.

Going forwards is easy. Pick a number, raise it to a power, take the remainder. Going backwards is the problem. If I tell you that 5 raised to some power lands on 8 on a 23 clock, can you find the power? With a small clock you can just try every power in turn. Once the clock has hundreds of digits, the remainders jump around in a pattern that looks random, and there is no known quick way to work backwards. ## The exchange with real numbers

Let us run it with small numbers. In real use the numbers are enormously bigger, but the steps are identical.

Both have arrived at 2, and neither ever said it aloud. The secret is 2.

## Why they always get the same answer

This works because of a rule you know from school: (5 to the power 6) to the power 15 is the same as 5 to the power 90, and also the same as (5 to the power 15) to the power 6. Multiplying the powers in either order gives 6 times 15 either way. Alice effectively computes 5 to the power 90, and so does Bob, though each reached it by a different route. The clock remainder does not change this rule.

An eavesdropper knows 5, 23, 8 and 19. To find the secret they need Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. That means working backwards from "5 to some power gives 8 on the 23 clock". With a clock this small, anyone can crack it in seconds by trying all the powers. That is why real systems use clocks with more than six hundred digits. At that size, working backwards is out of reach for every computer on Earth.

## Public keys and private keys

The word "key" now makes sense. Each person holds a pair. Alice's private key is her secret number, 6. Her public key is the number she calculates from it, 8. She can post the public key anywhere, even print it in a newspaper. It reveals nothing useful because of the one-way gap. Her private key never leaves her device.

Other schemes, such as RSA, use the same pair idea in a different way. Instead of agreeing on a shared secret, anyone can use Alice's public key to lock a message that only her private key can open. RSA relies on a different easy-versus-hard problem: multiplying two big prime numbers together is easy, while splitting the result back into those primes is very hard. The principle is the same, with one half of the pair open to the world and the other half guarded.

## What happens next

Once both sides hold the same secret number, they use it as the key for a fast, ordinary scrambling method, where both people use the same key to lock and unlock messages. Public-key tricks are slow, so they are used only for this opening handshake. Your phone does this every time you visit a secure website, in a few thousandths of a second, usually without you noticing.

## One honest limit

The method protects against someone who only listens. It does not by itself protect against someone who sits in the middle, pretending to be Bob when talking to Alice and pretending to be Alice when talking to Bob. Each victim would then agree a secret with the impostor. To stop this, people need a way to be sure a public key really belongs to who it claims. Websites handle this with certificates, which are public keys vouched for by trusted organisations. The vouching itself uses a related public-key trick, where a private key signs something and anyone can check the signature with the public key.

## The takeaway

Two strangers can agree on a secret in public because some calculations are easy to do and practically impossible to undo. Each person mixes a private number into a shared public one, swaps the results, and mixes in their private number once more.
