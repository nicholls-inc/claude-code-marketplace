**Let the Market Park the Car: Why Cities Should Scrap Parking Minimums**

Somewhere in your city's zoning code is a table that tells builders how many parking spaces they must provide. A restaurant needs so many per 100 square feet of dining room. An apartment building needs one or two per unit. A church needs one for every few seats. Most residents have never read it, but they live with it every day. It shapes how much housing gets built, what that housing costs, how far apart the shops are and how much of downtown is asphalt. It is time to delete it.

Minimum parking requirements began with a reasonable worry. In the mid-twentieth century, car ownership was soaring, and planners feared that new buildings would push drivers to fight over curb space on surrounding streets. Their answer was to make every new building supply its own parking. Nobody tested the numbers behind those tables, though. As the UCLA economist Donald Shoup showed in *The High Cost of Free Parking*, many of them were copied from one city's code to the next, or taken from studies of suburban sites at their busiest hour, when parking was free. They describe peak demand for parking that costs nothing. That is a strange thing to write into law.

The costs are large and mostly hidden. A space in a structured garage can easily cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground space costs more. That money does not vanish. It turns up in rents, in the price of a sandwich and in projects that are never built because the parking makes the numbers fail. On a small urban lot, a requirement of one space per apartment can decide whether a building holds twelve homes or six, or whether it can be built at all. In a housing shortage, a rule that quietly cuts supply is hard to defend.

Minimums also make people who do not drive pay for people who do. A household without a car still pays for the garage space bundled into its rent. A shopper who arrives on foot or by bus pays the same grocery prices as one who parks out front. Lower-income households are the least likely to own cars, so the cost falls hardest on them. Drivers get a subsidy, and nobody votes on it, because it never appears in any budget.

Then there is what the rules do to the shape of a city. Required parking lots push buildings apart, leave dead gaps along the street and make walking slower and less pleasant. When every destination has to come with a parking lot, the only practical way to reach it is by car, and that drives up demand for the parking the code then requires. Many of the older neighborhoods people love most, with shops opening onto the sidewalk and apartments above, would be illegal to build under modern minimums.

Abolishing minimums is not the same as banning parking. This point causes more confusion than any other in the debate. Without a mandate, developers can still build parking, and they will wherever tenants, lenders and customers want it. A suburban big-box store will still have a big lot. The only change is who decides how much. A business owner who risks their own money on the answer is better placed to judge local demand than a number written into a code decades ago.

Cities that have tried this have not collapsed into gridlock. Buffalo removed its citywide minimums in 2017. Minneapolis followed, then San Jose. In 2023 Austin became the largest American city to drop them entirely, and California has barred most minimums near major transit. Early research from these places points the same way: developers tend to build less parking than the old rules demanded, but rarely none, and more small and infill projects that the old rules had blocked start to become possible. Housing gets built on lots that used to be stuck.

The strongest objection is spillover. Residents worry that new buildings without garages will fill every curb space on their block. That worry deserves an answer, and the answer is to manage the street, not to mandate the lot. Residential permit zones, meters priced to keep a few spaces open on each block and time limits all work. These tools deal with the actual scarce resource, the curb, rather than forcing every new building to overbuild in case of a problem that may never arrive. Accessibility is a fair concern too, and cities can keep specific requirements for accessible spaces without keeping the whole system of minimums.

Ending minimums will not solve the housing crisis by itself, and it will not make anyone give up a car they need. It removes a costly, arbitrary rule that makes cities more expensive and less livable, and it lets each project be built to the parking its users actually want. That is a modest reform with a large payoff. Cities should stop requiring parking and let people decide how much they need.
