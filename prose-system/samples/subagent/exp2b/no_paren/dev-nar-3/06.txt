On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about forty degrees, a remarkable thaw after days of bitter cold. Dockworkers ate lunch outside, children played in the streets, and the neighborhood, crowded with Italian immigrants and working families, went about its business beneath a massive steel tank that loomed over Commercial Street.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It stood about fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sweet cargo had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a business that had grown urgent as the First World War demanded munitions. Prohibition was only days from becoming law, and the company was in a hurry to turn the molasses into liquor before the ban took hold.

The tank had been built quickly and cheaply. Its walls were too thin, and nobody had carried out proper engineering calculations or tests before it was filled. From the day it opened, it groaned and leaked. Neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cans. The company, rather than reinforce the structure, painted the tank brown so the leaks would be harder to see.

Just after noon, a sound like a machine gun rattled through the neighborhood. Witnesses later described it as the popping of rivets as they sheared away from the steel. Then the tank split open. A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged out at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a force that knocked buildings from their foundations and crushed wagons, horses, and people in its path.

The elevated railway nearby buckled as the wave struck a support column. A firehouse was shoved off its foundation, and several firemen were trapped in the wreckage. The flood swept across the district, filling cellars and streets with a thick, sticky mass. Because molasses is far more viscous than water, and especially in the cold air that returned that evening, victims found themselves unable to swim or even free their limbs. The more they struggled, the more entangled they became.

Rescuers rushed in, including soldiers and sailors from nearby posts, Red Cross workers, and local police. They waded through waist-deep goo to search for survivors, and many were found only after hours of digging. In the end, twenty-one people died and about one hundred fifty were injured. Some of the dead were children.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed saltwater from fireboats and covered the streets in sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown well into the summer, and residents claimed the area smelled sweet on hot days for decades.

A lengthy lawsuit followed, among the first class action cases in Massachusetts. The company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, but an auditor concluded that the structure had simply failed. The company paid roughly six hundred thousand dollars in damages. The disaster helped push states toward stricter building codes and requirements that engineers sign off on large structures.
