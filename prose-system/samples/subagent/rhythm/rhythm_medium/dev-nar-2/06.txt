On the evening of April 13, 1970, Apollo 13 was about fifty-five hours into its flight, nearly two hundred thousand miles from home, when the astronauts aboard were asked to perform a routine chore. Commander James Lovell, command module pilot Jack Swigert, and lunar module pilot Fred Haise had launched two days earlier on what was meant to be NASA's third landing on the Moon. The mission had already survived a few small troubles, including an engine on the Saturn V's second stage that shut down early, but nothing had seemed serious. Mission Control in Houston now asked the crew to stir the supercritical oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a standard step that kept the cryogenic contents from stratifying and helped the gauges read accurately.

Swigert flipped the switch for the fans in oxygen tank number two. Moments later the crew heard a sharp bang and felt a shudder run through the spacecraft. Swigert reported a problem to the ground, and Lovell repeated it with the words that became famous in slightly altered form, saying that Houston, we've had a problem. At first nobody understood how bad it was. Warning lights flickered, the main electrical buses dropped in voltage, and the thrusters that held the craft's orientation began firing on their own. Looking out a window, Lovell saw gas venting into space, and he understood that something had gone very wrong with the service module.

The cause lay in a chain of errors that began years before the flight. Oxygen tank number two had been built with a heater controlled by a thermostat, and the thermostat had been designed for a lower voltage than the ground equipment used at the Kennedy Space Center. During a pre-launch test, the tank had been difficult to empty, and technicians had used the heaters to boil off the oxygen, running them for hours at the higher voltage. That damaged the wiring insulation inside the tank, leaving bare wire exposed. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark jumped across the damaged wires, and the pure oxygen ignited. The fire raised the pressure so quickly that the tank ruptured, blowing off a panel of the service module and crippling the neighboring tank as well.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers watched their consoles show a collapse that made little sense. Within about two hours, the second oxygen tank was empty and the first was draining. That mattered enormously, because the oxygen fed the fuel cells that generated electricity and water for the command module, as well as the air the astronauts breathed. Without it, the command module, called Odyssey, would soon be dead. The crew's only hope of surviving was to use the lunar module, Aquarius, as a lifeboat, even though it had been built to carry two men for two days, not three men for four.

The decision to abandon the main spacecraft was made quickly, and the crew moved into Aquarius while Swigert and his colleagues powered down Odyssey to save its small reserve of battery power for reentry. Mission Control then faced a second question, which was how to bring the ship home. The Moon landing was obviously over, but the spacecraft was on a trajectory that would swing it around the Moon and not bring it back to Earth in any useful way. The service module's main engine, which was the normal tool for such a change, could not be trusted after the explosion. Engineers chose instead to use the descent engine of the lunar module, burning it about two hours after passing the Moon's far side to speed the return and aim for a Pacific landing.

The days that followed were a grind of cold, thirst, and improvisation. Aquarius was run at minimal power, and the temperature inside dropped to near freezing, with condensation beading on the walls. The crew slept little and drank very little water, since the supply had to be rationed to keep the electronics cool. Haise developed a painful kidney infection from dehydration and fever. The astronauts were exhausted, and the mood, by some accounts, occasionally grew tense, though they kept working.

A more urgent threat was the air. The lunar module's scrubbers used lithium hydroxide canisters to remove carbon dioxide, and these were designed for two men. As three people breathed, carbon dioxide climbed toward dangerous levels. The command module carried spare canisters, but they were square, while the lunar module's receptacles were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the materials the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from flight manuals, spacesuit hoses, and duct tape, and devised a way to fit a square canister into a round hole. They read the instructions to the astronauts step by step, and the crew built the adapter. The carbon dioxide level fell, and the immediate danger passed.

The remaining problems were nearly as demanding. A small error in the course meant that a correction burn was needed, and it had to be done manually, because the alignment systems were off to save power. Lovell used the Earth itself as a reference, keeping its terminator line in the window to orient the ship. Meanwhile, the ground team worked out the sequence for powering up Odyssey again, a procedure never meant to be done in this way, after days of being cold and dormant. They wrote a new checklist in a few days, tested it in simulators, and read it up to the crew. Engineers worried about whether the electronics would survive condensation and cold, but there was no alternative.

Before reentry, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module, and for the first time they could see what had happened. A whole panel was missing, and the side of the module was scorched and torn. Lovell described it to Houston, and photographs were taken. Then they left Aquarius, which had saved their lives, and returned to Odyssey, which held only a small reserve of power. The lunar module was released shortly before reentry and burned up in the atmosphere.

On April 17, the command module plunged into the atmosphere. Controllers had to wait through the usual radio blackout, which stretched longer than expected as the ship's path was slightly shallower. The silence at Mission Control was tense, and then, after a few minutes, Swigert's voice came back. Parachutes opened, and Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The astronauts were pale and thin, but they were alive and able to walk, after almost six days in space.

An investigation led by Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank and its heater design, and NASA redesigned the oxygen system, adding a third tank and removing the stirring fans. Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, but it came to be regarded as one of the agency's finest hours, a case in which a team of people on the ground and in space turned a disaster into a rescue. The mission is often described as a successful failure, because everything about the plan failed and everything about the people held together.
