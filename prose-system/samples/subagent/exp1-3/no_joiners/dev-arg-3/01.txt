The Case for Keeping the Joined Up Hand

Every few years a headteacher or a columnist announces that handwriting is finished. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, so children should spend their precious hours on coding or critical thinking rather than looping their letters together. It is a tidy argument and it feels modern. It is also wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined up handwriting, and they should teach it properly, with time set aside, clear expectations and a sensible sequence. The skill is not a nostalgic ornament. It serves the child's thinking, speed, confidence and independence in ways that no tablet has yet matched.

Begin with what handwriting actually is. It is not a matter of making pretty marks. It is a motor skill that sits at the junction of language, memory and movement. When a six year old forms the letters of a word, the hand has to recall the shape of each letter, plan the sequence, and carry it out with fine control. Researchers who have scanned the brains of children and adults have found that writing by hand activates a wider network than typing does, including regions linked to reading and to memory. In one well known line of studies, young children who practised letters by hand recognised them more readily afterwards than children who typed the same letters. Typing makes every letter the same press of a key. Handwriting makes every letter a different movement, and that difference seems to help the brain learn what each one is.

Joined up writing adds something further. Printing a word means lifting the pen after every letter, so the child builds the word as a string of separate events. A joined hand lets the word flow as a single gesture. The unit of movement becomes the word, or at least a long stretch of it, rather than the letter. This matters because spelling lives in patterns. A child who feels the movement of ing or tion or ough in the hand has one more way to hold those patterns in memory. Teachers who use joined script from an early stage often report that it supports spelling, because the child is rehearsing whole sequences instead of guessing at letters one by one. The evidence here is less settled than the evidence for handwriting in general, and honest advocates should say so. But the direction of the findings, and the experience of a great many classrooms, point the same way.

Speed is the second reason. Critics say typing is faster, and for a trained adult it is. But primary children are not trained typists. Most of them peck at keys, hunting for each letter, and their typing speed is far below their handwriting speed until well into secondary school. A child who prints slowly, letter by letter, with constant lifting of the pen, is often slower than a child with a fluent joined hand. Fluency frees the mind. When forming letters is automatic, a child can spend attention on what to say rather than how to draw it. When it is laborious, the sentence is forgotten before the last word is down. We have all seen the child who has wonderful ideas in conversation and then produces two cramped lines on the page. Often the bottleneck is the hand. Teaching a fast, legible, joined style removes that bottleneck.

Third, there is the matter of exams and real life. Whatever we hope for the future, children in England and in many other places still sit written examinations by hand. They take notes in lectures and fill in forms. They write birthday cards, shopping lists and condolence letters. A pupil who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand is handicapped in every one of these settings, and the handicap falls hardest on children from homes with fewer books, fewer devices and fewer adults with time to coach them. A school that drops handwriting instruction does not abolish the need for handwriting. It simply leaves the skill to be picked up at home, and homes differ. Those who arrive at school already holding a pencil well will do fine. Those who do not will fall behind in a way that is quiet, cumulative and unfair.

The most common objection is that the time could be better spent. This is where the argument needs the most care, because the time is real. A primary timetable is crowded, and every minute for handwriting is a minute taken from something else. But consider how little time is involved. Short, regular, well taught sessions, perhaps ten or fifteen minutes a few times a week, are enough to build a good hand over several years. That is a tiny share of the school week. And the skill it builds is used across every other subject. Nobody suggests we stop teaching children to read aloud on the grounds that they might one day use audio books. We recognise that a basic skill pays back its cost many times over. Handwriting belongs in the same category.

A related objection says that joined writing is old fashioned and that printed letters are good enough. There is a respectable version of this view. Some countries, Finland most famously, have moved away from compulsory cursive, and their children have not collapsed into illiteracy. Fair enough. But look more closely at what those systems do. They still teach handwriting, usually a simple printed or lightly connected style, and they still treat legibility and fluency as goals. The real choice is not between joined writing and a keyboard. It is between a taught, fluent hand and an untaught, laboured one. If a school chooses a simple semi joined italic style over looping traditional cursive, I have no quarrel. The point is that children should leave primary school able to write fast and clearly, and a connected hand is the most reliable route to that end.

There is also a case from inclusion that deserves more attention than it gets. Children with dyslexia are often said to benefit from joined writing, because the continuous movement reduces reversals of letters such as b and d and gives the hand a rhythm to follow. Not every child will find it easy, and children with motor difficulties need support and sometimes alternatives, including a keyboard. But that is an argument for flexibility within a handwriting curriculum, not for dropping the curriculum. We do not abandon swimming lessons because some children need armbands. We teach the skill and adapt for the child. Keyboards should be there for those who truly need them, and they should be taught as well. A child should leave primary school with both skills, not with neither.

Some will say that touch typing deserves the time instead. I agree that typing should be taught, and taught well, probably from the later primary years. The two skills are not rivals. Each has a job. Typing is excellent for long drafts, for editing and for sharing. Handwriting is excellent for quick notes, for thinking on paper, for exams and for the early years when the brain is building its picture of letters and words. A curriculum that offers both is richer than one that offers either alone. The mistake is to assume that because one tool has arrived the other must go. Calculators did not make mental arithmetic pointless. Cameras did not make drawing useless. Each new tool has pushed us to ask what the older skill is still good for, and in the case of handwriting the answer is plenty.

I should say something too about the plain human side of the matter. A handwritten letter carries the person who wrote it. Children who learn a joined hand develop a personal style, a signature, a way of putting themselves on the page. There is quiet pride in a page of neat writing, and I have watched children beam when they earn their pen licence after months of effort. In a world where so much of what children see is generated, templated and scrolled past, the ability to make a mark that is entirely their own is worth protecting. It builds patience and care. It teaches that some things come only through practice, which is a lesson that a screen, with its instant results, rarely offers.

None of this means schools should return to the days of rapped knuckles and endless copying of lines. Good handwriting teaching is short, kind and purposeful. It begins with posture, grip and pattern work in the early years. It introduces letter families in a logical order. It brings in joins early enough that bad habits do not set, perhaps once letters are secure, and it gives children chances to use their handwriting for real purposes rather than empty drills. It also accepts that the goal is legible, comfortable writing, not copperplate perfection. A child whose hand is clear and quick has succeeded, whatever the slant of the letters.

What should happen next is simple. Schools should keep joined up handwriting on the timetable and protect a few minutes for it each week. Training for teachers should include how to teach it, since many are given no instruction at all. Inspectors and policy makers should judge it by whether children write fluently and legibly, not by whether they loop their tails in an approved fashion. And parents should be told plainly why the school does it, so that practice at home is a shared effort.

The fashion for abandoning handwriting rests on a half truth. It is true that we live surrounded by screens. It does not follow that children no longer need to write by hand. The evidence from brain research is encouraging, the practical benefits in speed and examinations are clear, and the equity argument is strong. For a few minutes a day across a few years, schools can hand every child a skill that serves them for life. That is a bargain worth keeping.
