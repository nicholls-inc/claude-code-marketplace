On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Dockworkers, children, and city employees went about their business beside a enormous steel tank that rose fifty feet above Commercial Street. The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a use that mattered a great deal in the closing months of the Great War and just before Prohibition took effect.

The tank had been built quickly and cheaply in 1915. Its walls were thinner than they should have been, and the company had not bothered to hire a qualified engineer to check the design. When workers first filled it, the tank groaned and leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping molasses in pails. Instead of investing in repairs, the company painted the tank brown to hide the seeping stains. It never ran a proper test, and warnings from residents were brushed aside.

A little after twelve thirty that afternoon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a low rumble, a sound like a machine gun as the rivets popped, and then a sudden collapse of the walls. A wave of molasses roughly twenty-five feet high at its crest surged out in every direction at around thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a force that few could have imagined from something so thick and sticky. The wave crushed buildings, tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports, and swept wagons, horses, and people along in a brown tide. The firehouse near the tank was knocked off its foundation, and at least one firefighter was trapped beneath the wreckage.

The cold of the New England winter made matters worse in the hours that followed. As the warm molasses cooled, it thickened, and those caught in it found themselves held fast like insects in amber. Rescuers waded in waist deep, and the sailors from a nearby ship, the USS Nantucket, joined police, Red Cross workers, and army personnel in a desperate effort to reach survivors. The search continued for days. In the end, twenty-one people died and about one hundred and fifty were injured. Among the dead were children who had been playing near the tank, along with workers from the neighborhood.

The cleanup was an ordeal in its own right. Crews used salt water from fire boats to wash the syrup from the streets, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer. Residents claimed that the area smelled of molasses for decades afterward, especially on hot days.

The legal aftermath proved just as significant. Hundreds of residents sued the company, which insisted the tank had been destroyed by anarchist sabotage. After a lengthy trial, an auditor appointed by the court concluded that the structure had been poorly built and that the company was to blame. The firm paid out settlements to families of the victims. The disaster also helped push Massachusetts and other states toward stricter building codes, requiring that engineers review and sign off on plans for large structures. What began as a bizarre curiosity became a lasting lesson about corporate negligence and public safety.
