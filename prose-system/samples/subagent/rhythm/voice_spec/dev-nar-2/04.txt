Apollo 13 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida on 11 April 1970. It was the third mission planned to land men on the Moon. The crew were James Lovell, the commander, Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, and John Swigert, the command module pilot. Swigert was a late replacement. Ken Mattingly had been assigned to the flight, but doctors found that he had no immunity to German measles after he was exposed to the disease by another astronaut, and Swigert took his place three days before launch. The planned landing site was the Fra Mauro highlands. The command module was called Odyssey and the lunar module was called Aquarius.

The first two days were uneventful. On the evening of 13 April, 55 hours and 55 minutes into the flight and about 330,000 kilometres from Earth, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module. This was a routine step that kept the contents from settling. Swigert switched on the fans in the tanks. About two minutes later there was a loud bang and the spacecraft shook. Swigert said, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," and Lovell repeated it a few seconds afterwards.

Flight controllers could see that pressure in oxygen tank 2 had dropped to nothing and that electrical power was falling. The tank had ruptured, and the force of it had damaged the plumbing of tank 1 and blown a panel off the side of the service module. Two of the three fuel cells, which made electricity and drinking water from oxygen and hydrogen, lost their oxygen supply. Tank 1 leaked its contents into space over the next three hours or so. The command module had only a limited battery charge, which was meant for the final descent, and it could not support the crew for the days that the journey home would take. Within a few hours Lovell, Haise and Swigert had moved into Aquarius and used it as a lifeboat, and the landing was abandoned.

The lunar module had been built to keep two men alive for about two days, mostly on the Moon. It now had to keep three men alive for about four. The ground team and crew shut down nearly everything that used power, including the guidance computer and most of the heaters. Water was the scarcest supply. Each man was limited to about 200 millilitres a day, roughly a fifth of the normal amount, and Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection that he did not report until later. The cabin cooled until its temperature was close to 3 degrees Celsius, and the men slept badly in the cold.

The route home also had to be settled. The spacecraft had earlier been moved off a path that would carry it back to Earth without any further engine burns, so that it could reach Fra Mauro. About five hours after the explosion the crew used the descent engine of the lunar module to put it back on a path that would swing round the Moon and return. The service module engine was not trusted after the damage. Two hours after passing the Moon, the crew fired the descent engine again, this time for a little over four minutes, to speed the return by about ten hours and to bring the spacecraft down in the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships were waiting. At its closest the spacecraft passed about 254 kilometres above the far side of the Moon. At its greatest distance, about 400,000 kilometres from Earth, it was farther from the planet than any crewed vehicle has been before or since.

A second danger came from the air. Carbon dioxide from the crew's breathing is removed by canisters of lithium hydroxide. The lunar module's canisters were close to full, but the command module held spare canisters of a different shape, which did not fit the lunar module's fittings. Engineers on the ground built an adapter out of items known to be aboard, among them a plastic bag, the cardboard cover of a flight manual, a hose from a spacesuit and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, the crew assembled the device, and the carbon dioxide level fell to a safe figure.

Navigation was another problem. With the guidance platform switched off to save power, the crew could not rely on the computer to hold the spacecraft's attitude. For a course correction of about 14 seconds in the final day, they lined up on the Earth's day and night boundary and held the attitude by hand. Meanwhile, engineers and astronauts on the ground, Mattingly among them, wrote and tested in simulators a procedure for starting Odyssey from cold. It had to be done on a small reserve of battery power, in an order that would not drain it before the parachutes opened, and it had not been tried before.

About four hours before reaching the atmosphere, the crew separated the service module. Lovell reported that an entire panel was missing and that the side of the module was badly damaged, which gave the ground its first direct look at what had happened. Swigert and Haise photographed it. The crew then moved back into Odyssey, powered it up with the new procedure and released Aquarius, which burned up in the atmosphere. The radio blackout that comes with re-entry lasted longer than expected, and controllers waited with no word from the crew. Then Swigert's voice came through, and the parachutes were seen from the recovery aircraft. Odyssey splashed down near Samoa on 17 April at 18:07 UTC, after 142 hours and 54 minutes in flight. The crew were taken aboard the USS Iwo Jima. All three were tired, and Haise was ill, but none had been seriously injured.

A review board chaired by Edgar Cortright investigated the accident and reported in June 1970. The fault began on the ground. Tank 2 had been dropped by a few centimetres in 1968 while it was being removed from another spacecraft, and this probably damaged the pipe used to empty it. During a pre-launch test in March 1970 the tank could not be drained in the normal way, so technicians ran its internal heaters for several hours on a 65 volt ground supply. The thermostat switches that should have shut the heaters off had been designed for 28 volts and fused shut. The heat damaged the Teflon insulation on wires inside the tank, and nobody knew. In flight, when the fans were switched on, the bare wires sparked. The insulation caught fire in the high-pressure oxygen, and the tank burst.

NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks, removed the thermostat switches, added a third tank and put a spare battery in the service module. Apollo 14 landed at Fra Mauro in February 1971. Lovell, who had flown four space missions, never flew again. Many people regard the return of Apollo 13 as one of the best pieces of improvisation in the history of spaceflight. That is a judgement, and the record behind it is the one set out here, of a damaged ship, three men with very little power and water, and a ground team that found a way to bring them home.
