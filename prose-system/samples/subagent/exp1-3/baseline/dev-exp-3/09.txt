**How two strangers can agree on a secret in public**

**The problem**

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret, such as a password or a number, so they can send private messages afterwards. The only way to talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Everything they say will be heard by an eavesdropper, whom we'll call Eve.

For thousands of years this seemed impossible. Secret codes needed a shared key, such as a codebook or a agreed shift of letters. To share a key, you had to meet first, or send it by some trusted route. If the key travelled over the open line, Eve would have it too.

In the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around this. The idea is called public-key cryptography. It rests on a simple fact: some calculations are easy to do and almost impossible to undo.

**One-way calculations**

Multiplying two numbers is easy. 61 times 53 is 3,233, and you can do it in seconds. Now go backwards. Which two prime numbers multiply to give 3,233? You'd have to try dividing by 2, 3, 5, 7 and so on until something fits. For small numbers that is tolerable. For numbers with 600 digits, even the fastest computers in the world would need longer than the age of the universe.

The method below uses a different one-way calculation, based on remainders. You already know remainders from division: 17 divided by 5 is 3 remainder 2. Mathematicians write this as "17 mod 5 = 2". Think of a clock. After 12 it wraps back to 1, so 14 o'clock is 2 o'clock. Arithmetic "mod 12" is just clock arithmetic. We can do the same with a clock that has any number of hours.

Here is the one-way part. Take a number g and raise it to a power, then take the remainder. For example, 5 to the power 6 is 15,625. Divide by 23: 23 goes into 15,625 exactly 679 times, which is 15,617, leaving a remainder of 8. So "5 to the 6 mod 23 is 8".

Going forwards is quick. But if someone shows you only the answer, 8, and asks "what power of 5 gave this?", you have no shortcut. The remainders jump around unpredictably. You must try 5 to the 1, 5 to the 2, 5 to the 3, and so on, checking each one. With a small clock like 23 that is easy. With a clock of several hundred digits, it is hopeless.

**The trick, step by step**

Alice and Bob start by agreeing on two numbers. These are not secret. They can shout them across the line. We'll use 23 (the size of the clock) and 5 (the base number). Eve hears both.

Next, each of them picks a private number and tells nobody.

- Alice picks 6.
- Bob picks 15.

Each now does the one-way calculation with their private number and sends only the result.

- Alice works out 5 to the 6 mod 23, which is 8. She sends "8" to Bob.
- Bob works out 5 to the 15 mod 23, which is 19. He sends "19" to Alice.

Eve hears 8 and 19. She still does not know 6 or 15.

Now comes the clever part. Each person takes the number they received and raises it to their own private power.

- Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private 6: 19 to the 6 mod 23.
- Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private 15: 8 to the 15 mod 23.

Check the arithmetic. Since 19 is 4 less than 23, it behaves like negative 4 on this clock. Negative 4 to the 6 is 4,096. Dividing by 23 gives 178 with 4,094 used, leaving remainder 2. So Alice gets 2.

For Bob, 8 is 2 cubed, so 8 to the 15 is 2 to the 45. On this clock, 2 to the 11 happens to equal 1 (2,048 leaves remainder 1 when divided by 23, since 2,047 is 23 times 89). So 2 to the 44 is also 1, and 2 to the 45 is 2. Bob gets 2 as well.

They have arrived at the same number, 2, and neither ever sent it. That is their shared secret.

**Why did they match?**

This is the heart of it. Alice's number, 8, is 5 raised to her power 6. Bob then raises that to his power 15. So Bob is effectively computing 5 raised to (6 times 15). Bob's number, 19, is 5 raised to 15, and Alice raises that to 6. So Alice is computing 5 raised to (15 times 6).

Multiplication does not care about order: 6 times 15 equals 15 times 6. Both of them land on 5 raised to 90, remainder-ed by 23. The route differs, but the destination is the same. Each person contributed one private ingredient, and the maths combines them identically from either side.

**Why Eve is stuck**

What does Eve know? She knows the clock size 23, the base 5, Alice's 8 and Bob's 19. To find the secret she needs either 6 or 15. To get 6, she must work out which power of 5 gives remainder 8. With a clock as small as 23, she'll find it in moments by trial and error. She could even just try every possibility.

That is why real systems use enormous clocks, with numbers of 600 digits or more. At that size, the trial-and-error route needs more steps than there are atoms in the known universe. Meanwhile, Alice and Bob's own calculations stay fast, because computers have a clever shortcut for raising to big powers: they repeatedly square the number and take remainders as they go, so the numbers never grow out of hand.

So the design uses a lopsided difficulty. Legitimate users do a quick calculation. Attackers face an impossible one.

**From shared secrets to public keys**

What we've just seen is called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, after the two researchers who published it in 1976. It lets two people agree on a secret number. They then use that number as the key for an ordinary fast code to scramble their real messages.

A close relative is the "public key" idea in the name. Here, you make two linked numbers. One is public, and you can print it on a billboard. Anyone can use it to lock a message to you. The other is private, and only you hold it. Only the private one can unlock what the public one locked. It is like a padlock that anyone can snap shut, but only you hold the key to open. The best-known version, RSA, relies on the multiplying-primes problem described earlier. You publish the product of two huge primes. Others use it to lock messages. Only you know the two primes, which makes unlocking easy for you and infeasible for anyone else.

**Where you meet this**

Every time your browser shows a padlock beside a web address, something like this has just happened. Your device and the website, which have never met, have swapped public numbers and agreed on a secret in a fraction of a second. Every message after that is scrambled with the secret. Anyone watching the connection in a cafe, or at your internet provider, sees only numbers that mean nothing to them.

**One honest caveat**

The security does not come from a proof that Eve cannot succeed. It comes from the fact that nobody has found a fast method, despite decades of effort by very clever people. If someone found a quick way to undo these one-way calculations, the system would break. That is why researchers keep testing it, and why the numbers used keep getting bigger over time.

The lesson is that secrecy does not need a secret meeting. It needs a calculation that is easy to do and hard to undo, and a bit of arithmetic that gives the same answer from both directions.
