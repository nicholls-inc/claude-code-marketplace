The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

When Apollo 13 lifted off from Florida on April 11, 1970, much of the country had stopped paying close attention. Two crews had already walked on the Moon, and the television networks no longer carried every hour of a lunar flight. The crew was Jim Lovell, the commander and a veteran of three earlier flights; Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot; and Jack Swigert, the command module pilot. Swigert had joined the crew only three days before launch. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles, and doctors would not risk him falling ill in space. The plan was for Lovell and Haise to land in the hilly Fra Mauro region of the Moon while Swigert stayed in orbit.

The spacecraft had two parts the crew could live in. The command module, named Odyssey, was the cone-shaped capsule that would carry them home and through the atmosphere. Behind it sat the service module, a large cylinder holding the main engine, the fuel cells that made electricity, and tanks of oxygen that fed both the fuel cells and the crew. The lunar module, Aquarius, was attached to the nose of the command module, ready to separate and descend to the surface.

The first two days went smoothly. On the evening of April 13, nearly 56 hours into the flight and more than 300,000 kilometres from Earth, the crew finished a television broadcast that none of the networks aired live. A few minutes later, Mission Control asked Swigert to run a routine chore: switch on the fans that stirred the supercooled oxygen in the service module's tanks so the gauges would read correctly.

Seconds after he flipped the switch, the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights came on. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it a moment later: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first neither the crew nor the controllers understood how bad it was. The readings made so little sense that some in Mission Control suspected faulty instruments. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming away from the ship. "We are venting something out into space," he reported. It was oxygen. One tank was empty and the second was leaking. Two of the three fuel cells had died, and the third was failing. Without oxygen, the command module would soon have no power and the crew nothing to breathe.

The cause came out only later. During tests on the ground weeks before launch, a heater inside oxygen tank number two had been run on a higher voltage than its safety switch was built to handle. The switch fused shut, the heater ran unchecked, and temperatures inside the tank climbed high enough to burn away the insulation on the wiring. Nobody noticed. When Swigert turned on the fans in flight, bare wires sparked inside a tank of pure oxygen, and the tank burst, blowing off an outer panel of the service module.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his team had to work out how to keep three men alive a long way from home. There was no turning around. The spacecraft could not simply reverse direction; it had to swing around the Moon and use the Moon's gravity to head back toward Earth. That trip would take about four days.

The answer was the lunar module. Aquarius had its own oxygen, water, batteries, and engine, and it was still undamaged. About an hour after the explosion, as the last power in Odyssey faded, the crew moved into the lander and used it as a lifeboat. The command module was shut down entirely to save its batteries for the one thing only it could do: carry the crew through reentry at the end.

Aquarius had been built to support two men for about two days. Now it had to keep three men alive for roughly four. Controllers worked out power budgets down to the amp, switching off almost every system aboard. The heaters went off too. Temperatures inside the cabin fell to a few degrees above freezing, and condensation beaded on the walls. The crew could barely sleep. Water was rationed, and the men drank so little that Haise developed a urinary infection and a fever.

Getting home also meant steering. Earlier in the flight, Apollo 13 had left the course that would have looped it back to Earth automatically, so that it could aim for the landing site. A few hours after the explosion, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine, which was meant to lower the craft to the Moon's surface, to put the ship back on a path home. On April 14 they swung behind the far side of the Moon and briefly lost contact with Earth. During that pass they flew farther from Earth than any people before or since. Two hours later they fired the descent engine again to speed up and shorten the trip home by several hours.

Then a new danger arose. With every breath, the crew exhaled carbon dioxide, which the lunar module removed with canisters of lithium hydroxide. Its canisters were meant for two men over two days, and the carbon dioxide level began to rise. The command module had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers in Houston worked out a fix using only items they knew were aboard: plastic bags, cardboard from the flight manuals, hoses from the spacesuits, a sock, and grey duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the contraption, which they called "the mailbox", and attached it. The carbon dioxide reading began to fall.

To save power, the lunar module's navigation computer was switched off, which made the course corrections that followed harder. For one burn, Lovell steered by hand, keeping the edge of the Earth lined up in his window while Haise and Swigert watched the controls and called out the timing.

The final challenge was waking up Odyssey. No command module had ever been shut down in flight and restarted, and the capsule was cold and wet. In Houston, engineers and astronauts, among them the grounded Ken Mattingly, worked through the procedure in simulators to find a power-up sequence that would not drain the batteries before splashdown. The steps were read up to Swigert, who copied pages of them by hand.

About four and a half hours before reentry, the crew released the damaged service module. As it drifted away, they saw the damage for the first time: an entire side panel was gone. Lovell told Houston that the damage was extensive. About an hour before reentry, they climbed back into Odyssey and let Aquarius go. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," a controller said.

Every spacecraft returning from the Moon lost radio contact for a few minutes as superheated air surrounded it during reentry. Apollo 13's blackout lasted noticeably longer than expected, and the room in Houston waited in silence. Finally, Swigert's voice came through. Three parachutes opened over the South Pacific, and on April 17, 1970, Odyssey splashed down near Samoa. A helicopter brought the three men aboard the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima.

They never reached the Moon. NASA later called the mission a "successful failure", because the crew and the people on the ground had turned what might have been a disaster into a safe return. The investigation that followed led to redesigned oxygen tanks, an extra tank, and an emergency battery on later Apollo spacecraft. Four more crews would go on to land on the Moon.
