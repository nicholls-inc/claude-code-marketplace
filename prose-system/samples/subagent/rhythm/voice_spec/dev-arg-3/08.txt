Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. My reason is a practical one. A child who can write fluently by hand has a tool that costs almost nothing, works in every classroom and exam hall, and can keep pace with thought. Joining letters is the most dependable method schools have found for getting most children to that point. I do not think the case rests on tradition or on the beauty of a well-formed page, and I will try to set out what the evidence shows, where it is thin, and how much school time the subject deserves.

Start with what schools are actually asked to do. The national curriculum for England, revised in 2014, tells teachers to introduce joins in Year 2, when children are six and seven, and to have pupils using them routinely by the later years of primary school. It does not name a style. Schools pick a scheme, and many choose one in which each letter ends with a small exit stroke, so that joining grows out of the way the letters are made. The time involved is small. A school that gives fifteen minutes to handwriting practice three or four times a week is spending about an hour a week on it. The question is whether that hour buys enough.

The first thing it buys is fluency. In print, each letter is a separate set of movements and the pen lifts between them. Joining removes many of those lifts, so a word becomes closer to a single run of movement. Teachers who favour joined writing say this makes children quicker and less tired by the bottom of a page. The research here is less tidy than its supporters sometimes suggest. Studies of handwriting speed often find that the fastest writers join some letters and leave others unjoined, which is the hybrid style most adults end up with. I read that as support for teaching joins. A child who has been taught a joined style has a model of efficient movement to adapt, whereas a child left to print has nothing comparable to adapt from. That is a view and not a proven result, but it fits what most teachers see in practice.

The second thing it buys is performance in exams. GCSE and A-level papers in England are still written by hand, in sessions that can run to three hours. A student who writes slowly loses time, and one who writes illegibly risks losing marks that the examiner cannot award. Examiners' reports from the boards have mentioned hard-to-read scripts for years. A sixteen-year-old with a poor hand cannot fix it the week before the exam. The habits are set far earlier, and most of the groundwork is laid between the ages of six and eleven. If a school wants its pupils to write for three hours at sixteen, the sensible time to begin is when they are seven.

The evidence on hands and brains is often quoted in this debate, and it is worth being exact about. In 2014 the psychologists Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer reported that students who took lecture notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than students who typed. Later attempts to repeat the result found a smaller effect or none, so it is best treated as suggestive. In 2024 a Norwegian team led by Audrey van der Meer recorded the brain activity of 36 students and found more widespread connectivity when they wrote by hand than when they typed. The sample was small and the participants were adults. Neither study compared joined writing with print. What the two together support is a modest claim, that handwriting does something for learning that keyboards do not fully reproduce. They do not show that joined handwriting is better than printed handwriting, and I will not pretend they do. The argument for joining rests on fluency and legibility, with the wider evidence supporting handwriting in general.

Other countries have gone the other way, which is a fair test of my position. Finland changed its national curriculum in 2016 so that schools no longer had to teach a joined style, and it gave more weight to typing. Handwriting is still taught there, in print letters, and Finnish pupils are not short of literacy. In the United States the Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out, and a number of states have since passed laws to put it back. I take two things from this. The first is that a country can do without compulsory joining and keep strong results, so my claim is about what is better and not about what is essential. The second is that the decision reverses easily in both directions, which suggests the evidence does not settle it and that each side is arguing from the experience of its classrooms.

The strongest objection is that print is enough. Children already learn print letters in Reception, they match the type in their books, and joining asks them to learn a second way of writing the same alphabet. I think this overstates the burden. Joining adds a handful of strokes to letter shapes the child already knows. Most children can manage them within a year, and the school has the child's attention for that year in any case. A second objection is that the time should go to keyboard skills, since adults type far more than they write. I agree that touch-typing deserves proper teaching. It should be taught as well as handwriting and not instead of it, because the hour a week at stake is small next to the time children spend with screens.

Some children do find joining hard, and a fair account has to say so. Children with significant motor difficulties, including some with dyspraxia, can be slowed by it, and the sensible response is to let them keep a clear print style and use a keyboard where it helps. For other children with dyslexia the advice is mixed. Some specialists recommend joining because the continuous line makes b and d harder to confuse and keeps the letters of a word together. Others find that it adds strain. A school should have a rule that joining is taught to everyone and a route for those who cannot manage it, and should not drop the rule because a minority struggle. Teaching to the majority and adapting for the few is how most of the curriculum works.

There is also the matter of reading. Children will meet joined writing in a teacher's marking, in birthday cards, in family papers and in the signatures on forms. A pupil who has never been shown how the joins work will find all of this harder to read than a pupil who has been taught. That is a small cost for any one child, but it falls most heavily on children whose homes have the fewest books and letters to learn from. Signing one's name is also a joined movement for most adults, and a child who has learnt a fluent hand has something to build a signature from.

If schools keep joined writing, they should teach it with some restraint. Reception and Year 1 should give children well-formed print with the correct starting points and exit strokes, so that later joining is a short step. Joins should be introduced in Year 2 and practised in short, regular sessions, not in long copying exercises that children come to dislike. Teachers should judge legibility and speed, not elegance, and by the end of primary school children should be allowed to settle into a personal style, including the hybrid that most of them will choose. The aim is a hand that other people can read and that does not hold up the writer. Anything beyond that is decoration.

My conclusion is a limited one. Joined-up handwriting is not the pillar of literacy that its keenest defenders claim, and the research does not prove that it outperforms print. Even so, it is cheap and quick to teach and builds fluency, and it serves children in exams and in everyday reading. Given how little it costs, the burden of proof lies with those who want to drop it. They would need to show that the hour a week could be spent on something that helps children more, and so far nobody has shown that. Until someone does, primary schools should keep teaching it.
