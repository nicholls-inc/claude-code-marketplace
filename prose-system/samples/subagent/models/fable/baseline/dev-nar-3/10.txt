Shortly after half past noon on January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unseasonably mild day. The temperature had climbed above 40 degrees Fahrenheit after a bitter cold snap, and workers along Commercial Street were eating lunch outdoors. Towering over them stood a steel tank fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, destined to be distilled into industrial alcohol.

The tank had been a poor piece of work from the start. Built in haste in 1915 to meet wartime demand for munitions-grade alcohol, it was never properly tested by filling it with water. Its steel walls were too thin for the load they carried, and it leaked so persistently that neighborhood children collected the drips in pails. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown so the streaks would not show.

Witnesses later recalled a deep rumble and a rapid series of cracks, like machine-gun fire, as the rivets shot out of the seams. Then the tank split apart. A wave of molasses, by most estimates around twenty-five feet high at its peak, surged outward at something like thirty-five miles an hour. It was far too fast to outrun.

The wave flattened wooden buildings, pushed a firehouse off its foundation, and buckled a steel support of the elevated railway, nearly throwing a train from the tracks. Fragments of the tank flew like shrapnel. People, horses and wagons were swept up and carried along, then left stranded as the flood spread across several blocks and settled waist-deep in places.

The rescue was grim. Among the first to arrive were more than a hundred sailors from a training ship moored nearby, the USS Nantucket, followed by police, firefighters, Red Cross nurses and soldiers. As the afternoon cooled, the molasses thickened, gripping victims and hampering those who waded in after them. Searchers worked for days. In the end twenty-one people died, among them laborers, teamsters, a firefighter and two ten-year-old children, and about 150 were injured. Many horses were so hopelessly mired that police shot them.

Cleanup took weeks. Crews pumped salt water from the harbor to cut the syrup and spread sand to soak it up, while rescuers and sightseers tracked the residue onto streetcars, pay phones and doorsteps across the city. The harbor ran brown into the summer.

The company blamed anarchists, claiming a bomb had destroyed the tank. Survivors and victims' families sued, and their claims were combined into one of the earliest large class-action cases in Massachusetts. After roughly three years of hearings and testimony from thousands of witnesses, a court-appointed auditor found that the tank had failed structurally. The company paid over $600,000 in settlements.

The lasting consequences were regulatory. Massachusetts, and later other states, began requiring that engineers and architects certify plans for major structures and that those plans be filed with authorities and inspected. The site is now a public park, marked by a small plaque. Local legend insists that on hot summer days the North End still smells faintly of molasses.
