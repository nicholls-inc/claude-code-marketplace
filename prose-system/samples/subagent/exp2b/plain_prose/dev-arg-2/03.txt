Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, or at least stop treating those estimates as something worth the hours we spend producing them.

Consider what an estimate actually is. It is a guess about the future made at the moment you know the least about the task. You haven't opened the unfamiliar module yet. You haven't discovered that the vendor API behaves differently from its documentation. You haven't learned that the "simple" migration touches a table nobody has understood since 2019. Decades of evidence, from the planning fallacy research to every project retrospective you have ever sat through, say the guess will be wrong. Not randomly wrong, either, but wrong in one direction, and by a margin that grows with the interesting parts of the work.

The standard defence is that estimates are not promises. Everyone nods, and then the number goes into a roadmap, the roadmap goes to a customer, and the date becomes a commitment with a sticky note saying otherwise. Engineers know this, which is why they pad. Managers know that, which is why they trim the padding. The result is a negotiation about fiction, conducted with great seriousness, where the real output is mutual distrust. When the date slips, the team doesn't conclude that estimating is hard. It concludes that someone was sandbagging or someone was bullying, and the argument moves to blame.

Then there is the cost. Planning poker, story point calibration, re-estimation after scope changes, the meeting about why velocity dropped: add it up and a team can burn a tenth of its capacity forecasting work instead of doing it. That time buys a number that is least accurate on exactly the projects where accuracy would matter.

None of this means you abandon planning. It means you change what you plan with. Slice work into pieces small enough that nobody argues about their size, then count the pieces finished per week. That throughput is measured rather than imagined, it absorbs the unknowns that individual estimates miss, and it gives you a forecast with honest error bars. If you must tell a stakeholder when something will land, say that, in a range, and update it weekly as real data arrives. A forecast built from what the team has actually shipped is worth more than any amount of upfront estimating.

The stronger move is to ask a different question. Instead of "how long will this take?", ask "what is this worth, and what is the most we would pay for it?" Fix the budget, ship the most valuable slice first, and cut scope when the money or patience runs out. This turns time from a prediction into a constraint, which is a thing teams can actually manage. It also forces the conversation managers should have wanted all along, about priorities rather than dates.

The objection I hear most is that the business needs predictability. It does. But predictability comes from small batches, short feedback loops, and a team that is allowed to say "this is bigger than we thought" without being punished for it. Estimates deliver none of those things. They deliver the feeling of control, which is precisely why they are so hard to give up.

Try it for a quarter. Stop estimating, track throughput, and publish ranges. If your forecasts get worse, you can always go back to guessing.
