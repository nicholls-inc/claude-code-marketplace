On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about forty degrees Fahrenheit, a sharp rise after days of bitter cold. Children played in the streets, dockworkers ate lunch, and families went about their business near the harbor. Looming over the neighborhood, at 529 Commercial Street, stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held more than two million gallons of molasses.

The molasses had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, which was used in munitions and, as Prohibition loomed, in liquor. The tank had been built quickly in 1915, and the company had cut corners. The steel was thinner than it should have been, and the rivets were poorly set. No one had run a proper structural inspection. The tank leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in pots. Rather than repair it, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. When workers filled it for the first time, they heard it groan, and they saw it shudder.

Around 12:40 that afternoon, a fresh shipment had just been pumped in, and the warming weather had made the contents expand and ferment. Witnesses described a rumbling, then a sound like machine gun fire as the rivets popped loose. Then the tank tore open.

A wave of molasses perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high surged through the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles per hour. It weighed millions of pounds. It smashed buildings off their foundations, knocked a freight car from the elevated railway tracks, and crushed wagons, horses, and people in its path. A firehouse was hit and partially collapsed. The wave carried debris along with it, and anyone caught inside found the sticky mass pulling at their limbs, thickening as it cooled.

Rescuers rushed in, among them sailors from the USS Nantucket, which was docked nearby, along with police, Red Cross workers, and cadets. They waded through knee-deep syrup searching for survivors. In the end, twenty-one people died, and about one hundred fifty were injured. Some victims were not found for days. The harbor ran brown for months, and the smell of molasses lingered in the neighborhood for decades, especially on hot summer days.

The aftermath became a landmark in American law. Residents filed one of the first major class action lawsuits in the state. The company claimed that anarchists had bombed the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court examined the evidence over several years and concluded that the structure was simply too weak. In 1925 the company was found liable and paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars in damages. The case helped push cities toward stricter building codes and requirements that engineers sign off on their designs.

Today a small plaque marks the site, a quiet reminder of a disaster that sounds almost comical but was devastatingly real.
