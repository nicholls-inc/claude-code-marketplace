In the last days of August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district of London where tradesmen, laborers and the very poor lived packed into old houses with cesspools in their cellars and no sewers worth the name. The disease killed with a speed that frightened even doctors who had seen it before, draining the body of fluid through violent vomiting and diarrhea until a healthy person could be dead within a day, and within a few days of the first cases around Broad Street, more than a hundred people had died. Most physicians of the time believed that cholera, like plague and malaria, was carried on foul air, a "miasma" that rose from rotting refuse and low ground and made people ill when they breathed it. John Snow, a physician who lived nearby on Sackville Street and who had made his name by administering chloroform to patients in surgery and in childbirth, thought this was wrong. He had been arguing since the epidemic of 1848 and 1849 that cholera was something swallowed rather than inhaled, that it multiplied in the gut and was passed on in the waste of the sick, and that it reached new victims when that waste found its way into drinking water. He had no microscope evidence to show for it, because the organism responsible had not yet been recognized as the cause, and so his argument rested entirely on patterns in who fell ill and who did not.

When the Soho outbreak began he did what he could to find those patterns on the ground. He went door to door, asked families where they had gotten their water, and plotted the deaths on a map of the neighborhood, marking each house with a bar for every person who had died there, so that the bars piled up along Broad Street in a dense cluster centered on the public water pump at the corner with Cambridge Street. A handful of deaths lay closer to other pumps, and Snow went looking for the reason, finding in most cases that the families involved had sent children to the Broad Street pump because they preferred its water or because the school was nearby. The exceptions interested him as much as the rule. The workhouse on Poland Street, which housed more than five hundred people in the middle of the worst-hit blocks, lost only a few of them, and Snow learned that it had its own well and did not use the pump at all. The men at the Lion Brewery on Broad Street were spared as well, apparently because they had a well of their own on the premises and were in any case given malt liquor to drink. One of the most striking cases was that of a widow living far away in Hampstead, who had once lived in Soho and who had a cart carry bottles of water from the Broad Street pump to her house because she liked the taste, and she and a niece who drank it with her both died of cholera in the same week that the neighborhood was dying.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow took his evidence to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish, the local body responsible for the district, and urged them to act. He was not an official, he could not prove how the water had become poisonous, and what he asked for was strange to men who had been taught that the disease came from the air, but he persuaded them enough that the handle of the pump was taken off the next day, so that nobody could draw water from it. The outbreak was already waning by then, since many residents had fled the district and many of the most vulnerable were already dead, and so the removal of the handle has never been a clean proof of anything, a point Snow himself acknowledged in his later writing.

The final link in the chain was supplied by someone else, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, a curate at the nearby church of St Luke who had begun by doubting Snow's theory and who knew the families of the parish far better than any physician did. Working through the registers and talking with the bereaved, Whitehead found that the first case seems to have been an infant girl at 40 Broad Street, who had fallen sick with diarrhea in the last days of August and died soon after, and that her mother had rinsed the child's soiled linen into a cesspool only a few feet from the well supplying the pump. When the cesspool was later dug up, its brickwork was found to be decayed and leaking, which made it plausible that sewage had been seeping into the well for some time. Whitehead came away convinced, and his name now stands beside Snow's in the history of the episode.

The authorities, however, were not persuaded, and an official inquiry concluded that the evidence did not support water as the cause, while the miasma theory remained the orthodoxy for years afterward. Snow went on to publish his full argument, including the map and a comparison of the homes supplied by two rival water companies south of the Thames, where one drew its water from a polluted stretch of river and the other from further upstream, and the first suffered far more deaths than the second. He died in 1858 without seeing his view accepted. It was only after Robert Koch isolated the cholera bacterium in 1883 that his work was recognized as the foundation of modern epidemiology.
