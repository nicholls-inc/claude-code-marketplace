On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three astronauts toward what was meant to be NASA's third landing on the Moon. Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights, led the crew, with Fred Haise as lunar module pilot and Jack Swigert as command module pilot, who had joined only days before the launch, when Ken Mattingly was grounded after exposure to German measles. The launch itself was nearly flawless, apart from an engine on the second stage that shut down early, and the remaining engines made up the difference as the spacecraft headed out of Earth orbit. Two days of ordinary, even tedious, flight followed, during which the crew named their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius. On the evening of April 13, they finished a television broadcast that the networks largely ignored, showing viewers around the cabin and saying goodnight.

About nine minutes after the broadcast ended, mission controllers asked Swigert to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine task that keeps the supercold contents from stratifying. He flipped the switch, and fifty-odd seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert radioed the words that became famous in slightly altered form, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," and Lovell repeated them as warning lights lit across the panel. Voltage on one of the main electrical buses had dropped, and the crew soon saw that the pressure in an oxygen tank was falling toward zero.

The cause lay years in the past: the oxygen tank had been damaged during ground testing, and its internal heater wiring had been left with its insulation scorched. When Swigert stirred the tank, a spark ignited the Teflon insulation in a pure oxygen environment, the pressure surged, and the tank ruptured, blowing off a panel of the service module. The second oxygen tank was damaged too, and soon both were leaking into space, taking with them the oxygen on which the fuel cells that made the spacecraft's electricity and water depended, and on which the astronauts breathed.

Lovell looked out a window and saw gas venting into space, and when he reported it, the controllers on the ground understood what it meant: the command module could not be kept alive. The crew would have to use the lunar module as a lifeboat, a craft designed to carry two men for two days on the Moon's surface and now asked to sustain three for four. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams worked through the night, abandoning the landing within hours, so that the only question left was how to bring the men home.

The first decision was how to turn around. Apollo 13 was on a path that would carry it around the Moon and, had nothing been done, miss the Earth on the way back. Engineers considered a direct abort, but that would have required firing the damaged service module's main engine, which no one trusted, so they chose instead to swing around the Moon and use the lunar module's descent engine to speed the return. The first burn put the ship back on a course that would bring it home, and a second burn, made about two hours after the craft passed the far side of the Moon, shortened the trip by many hours. As they passed the Moon, the crew were farther from Earth than any humans had ever been, nearly 250,000 miles away, and though they had little time for sightseeing, Lovell later recalled the sight of the lunar surface slipping by below.

Each system now became a puzzle about scarcity, and power was the first. The lunar module's batteries had to last until reentry, so the crew switched off almost everything, including the guidance computer and most of the lights, and the temperature in the cabin fell to near freezing. Condensation formed on the walls, the men slept little and ate cold food, and Haise, who developed a fever from a urinary infection, suffered through the chill. Water was rationed to about six ounces per man each day, and dehydration, as much as anything, wore them down, so that all three lost weight over the journey.

The air was the next problem. The lunar module's carbon dioxide scrubbers used lithium hydroxide canisters that were rated for two men over a short stay, and as they filled up, the levels of carbon dioxide in the cabin rose toward the point where the crew would be poisoned. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the materials that the crew would have aboard, including cardboard, plastic bags, suit hoses, and duct tape, and devised an adapter, which they read to the astronauts over the radio and which the men assembled into the device that became known as the mailbox. The carbon dioxide level fell, in one of the most improvised and celebrated repairs in the history of spaceflight.

Navigation was the third challenge. With the computer largely off and the usual alignment methods unavailable, Lovell needed another way to hold the burn on course, and he used the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, as a reference to align the spacecraft by hand for a crucial correction burn. The maneuver worked, and later, as the spacecraft drifted slightly off its aim, the crew performed a further manual burn, timed by a watch, to keep the entry angle within its narrow corridor.

Meanwhile, the controllers faced the problem of restarting the command module after days without power, since Odyssey would need to be revived for reentry and its batteries could not be wasted. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, worked in a simulator with the engineers to write a startup procedure that drew as little power as possible, and after testing it again and again, they finally read it up to the crew.

On April 17, as the ship neared Earth, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module, and photographs showed one whole side blown away, a sight that stunned the men, who realized how close they had come. Then they moved into Odyssey, powered it up using the new procedure, and let go of Aquarius, the little lander that had saved them, which burned up in the atmosphere over the Pacific.

Reentry brought a final anxious silence. Normally, a few minutes of radio blackout occurs as the heat of reentry ionizes the air around the capsule, but this time it lasted longer than expected, and the controllers waited, watching the clock. Then, at last, Swigert's voice came through, the parachutes opened, and the capsule splashed down in the South Pacific, near the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, on the afternoon of April 17, after almost six days in space, with a crew that was tired, thin, and cold, but well.

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon, yet NASA called it a successful failure. The investigation, led by Edgar Cortright, traced the accident to the tank's faulty design and testing, and the tanks were redesigned. The episode taught the agency that the spacecraft could survive a disaster that no one had planned for, because of the people on the ground and in the cabin who refused to give up, and the mission proved how much skill, improvisation, and teamwork lay behind the Apollo program. Lovell later wrote of the flight as a lesson in what people can do when the situation demands it. For many, it remains the finest hour of the space program, a case in which a lost mission became a rescue.
