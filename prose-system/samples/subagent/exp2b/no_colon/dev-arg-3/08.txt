In Defence of the Joined-Up Line

Every few years someone declares that handwriting is finished. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, so children should spend their precious classroom hours on coding or typing instead of looping their letters together. Finland moved away from compulsory cursive in 2016. Several American states dropped it from their curriculums after the Common Core reforms, and some have since restored it. The question is a fair one. Schools are crowded places, the school day is short, and every minute given to one skill is taken from another. But I think primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and I think the case for doing so is stronger than its critics admit. Joined writing is faster than print, it supports spelling and thinking, it keeps children able to read the written record of their own culture, and it gives pupils who struggle a route to fluency that print alone does not.

Begin with speed, because speed is the most practical argument. Printed letters require the pen to lift after every character. Each lift is a small interruption, a tiny decision about where the next letter begins. A joined script reduces those interruptions, so the hand moves in longer, more rhythmic strokes. Once a child has learned it properly, the writing flows, and the child can write more in the same time. This matters most in exactly the places where handwriting still dominates, namely examinations. Children in most countries still sit their important tests with a pen and paper. A student who writes legibly at speed has more working memory left over for the content of the answer. A student who labours over each letter is paying a hidden tax on every sentence. Teaching a fluent joined style in primary school is an investment that pays out at sixteen and eighteen, when the stakes are highest.

The second argument concerns what handwriting does to the mind. The research here deserves care, because enthusiasts sometimes overclaim. Still, the findings are consistent enough to take seriously. Studies by Virginia Berninger and colleagues found that primary children who wrote by hand produced longer and more fluent compositions than those who typed, and showed more activity in the brain regions linked to reading and idea generation. Work by Karin James has suggested that children who draw letters by hand recognise them better than those who only type or trace them. More recent studies of students taking notes found that handwriting tends to produce deeper processing, because the writer cannot transcribe everything and must choose what matters. None of this proves that joined writing in particular beats print. But the evidence for handwriting in general is strong, and joined writing is the form of handwriting that a child can sustain for a whole page without tiring. If we want children to enjoy the cognitive benefits of writing by hand, we should teach them a style that lets them do it comfortably for long stretches.

A third point is often overlooked, and it concerns spelling. Cursive treats a word as a single movement. The hand learns the shape of the whole word, in the way a pianist learns a phrase rather than a series of separate notes. For a child learning that the letters in "ing" or "tion" always travel together, the joined stroke builds a physical memory of the pattern. Teachers who work with children with dyslexia have long argued that this motor memory helps, and some specialist programmes are built around it. Print, by contrast, encourages the child to treat a word as a pile of separate letters. It also makes the common reversal of b and d easier, since the two letters are mirror images that differ only in the direction of a lift. In joined writing, the b and d begin their strokes in different ways, which makes them more distinct to the hand. This is a modest advantage, but for a struggling reader modest advantages are the ones that count.

Which brings me to fairness. Critics sometimes say that cursive is an elite accomplishment, a relic of copperplate schooling. The reverse is closer to the truth. Children from homes with plenty of books, tablets and patient adults will pick up literacy skills in many ways. Children from homes with fewer resources depend on the school to teach them everything explicitly. If we drop joined writing from the curriculum, middle-class families will teach it anyway, as they teach piano and swimming, and the children of the poorest will be left with only whatever scrawl they have picked up by themselves. A taught, consistent script is a form of equality. It gives every child the same tool and the same chance to write something that other people can read and admire.

Then there is the matter of reading itself. A child who cannot read joined writing is cut off from a large part of the world. Grandparents write birthday cards in cursive. Teachers write comments in the margins of exercise books in a flowing hand. Museums and archives are full of letters, diaries and ledgers written in joined script. Historians now warn that a generation unable to read these documents will depend on transcribers and will be unable to check the originals. A child who has learned to write cursive can read it, and reading it is a quiet form of citizenship, a way of being able to look at the evidence for oneself. It would be a strange thing to teach children history while withholding the skill needed to read its primary sources.

The best objection to all this is the one about time. Handwriting lessons cost hours, and those hours could be used for keyboard skills that every child will need. I accept the premise and reject the conclusion. Keyboard skills matter, and schools should teach them. But the two skills are not rivals in the way the objection assumes. Handwriting practice takes perhaps ten to fifteen minutes a day in the early years, and much of it can be folded into spelling and composition lessons that are already on the timetable. Typing, meanwhile, is a skill children pick up quickly, at home and at school, because the devices are everywhere and the motivation is built in. No one worries that children will fail to learn to use a phone. What children will not pick up unaided is a fluent, legible hand, because nothing in their daily lives requires it of them. The skill that is under threat is the one that deserves the protection.

Another objection says that joined writing is hard and that many adults abandon it for a hybrid of print and loops anyway. That is true, and it is not an argument against teaching it. Adults abandon many things they learned at school, including long division and the rules of the semicolon, and we do not conclude that these were a waste. What a school teaches is a foundation, and individuals adapt it. Most adults who write by hand develop a personal style, and that style usually grows out of the joined script they learned as children. The hybrid is the product of the teaching, not evidence against it. A child who has never been taught to join letters has nothing to adapt, and will often develop a slow, cramped, idiosyncratic print that tires the hand and baffles the reader.

I should say what I am not arguing. I am not arguing for the harsh methods of the past, with rulers across knuckles and marks deducted for a messy slant. Nor do I think that every child needs to master an ornate, looping hand with elaborate capitals. The sensible modern approach, used in many English schools, is to teach a simple, clean script with entry and exit strokes from the start, so that the child never has to unlearn print and relearn cursive at the age of seven. That approach is gentle, quick and effective. It treats handwriting as a tool, to be taught with the same patience we bring to reading and number work, and not as a test of character or a display of tidy penmanship.

The deeper point is that schools exist partly to pass on the technologies of thought that a society has built up over centuries. Handwriting is one of them. It is not the only way to record language and it will not always be the dominant one. But we do not abandon a useful tool simply because a newer one has arrived. Children still learn to do mental arithmetic in a world of calculators, because the practice builds understanding that the machine cannot supply. In the same way, they should learn to write by hand in a world of keyboards, and they should learn to do it well enough that the act feels easy rather than laborious. Joined-up handwriting is the form that makes this possible, giving speed, rhythm and a settled physical memory for the shapes of words.

So the answer is yes, schools should keep teaching it. They should teach it simply, early and kindly, without nostalgia and without cruelty, and they should alongside it teach typing and every other digital skill a child will need. The choice between the pen and the keyboard is a false one. A well-educated child can use both, and the pen, with its joined and flowing line, remains the tool that most directly connects thought to the page.
