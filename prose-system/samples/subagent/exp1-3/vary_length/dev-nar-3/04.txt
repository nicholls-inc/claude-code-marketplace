On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, a startling jump from the deep cold of the previous days. Around the waterfront, workers ate lunch, children played in the streets, and horses pulled carts along Commercial Street.

Towering over them all stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly 90 feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sticky syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions and, with Prohibition only days from taking effect, in rum's legal absence.

The tank had never been trusted. It leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the dripping molasses in cans. The company's response was not to repair it but to paint it brown, so the stains would not show. It was never properly tested. No engineer had signed off on the design, and the walls were thinner than the load demanded.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, the tank gave way.

Witnesses described a sound like machine-gun fire, the rapid popping of rivets shooting from the steel. Then came a deep rumble, and the tank burst open. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen feet high at its crest, swept outward at roughly 35 miles an hour. It was dense, heavy, and unstoppable.

The wave crushed everything in its path. It tore the Boston Elevated Railway's overhead structure from its supports, knocked a firehouse off its foundation, and flattened freight cars and wooden buildings. Horses, wagons, and people were swallowed and carried along. Some were killed by the force of the impact alone. Others drowned in the thick, clinging liquid, unable to swim or climb free.

Then the cold arrived. As the molasses cooled in the winter air, it thickened, and rescuers struggled to reach those trapped inside. Firefighters, police officers, sailors from a nearby ship, and Red Cross workers waded in knee-deep, pulling out survivors. Searching for victims took days. Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. The harbor ran brown for weeks, and locals insisted that on hot summer days the neighborhood still smelled sweet.

The aftermath changed American law. Lawsuits followed, and the company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank. The court appointed an auditor, who spent years hearing testimony from hundreds of witnesses. In 1925, he concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, and the company paid roughly a million dollars in damages to victims and families.

The disaster also helped reshape how buildings and structures are approved. It encouraged the requirement that architects and engineers must sign and stamp their plans, a standard now routine across the country. A strange, almost comic event had left a serious legacy.
