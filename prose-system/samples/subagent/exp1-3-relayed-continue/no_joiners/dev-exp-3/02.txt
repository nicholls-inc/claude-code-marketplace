**How Strangers Can Share a Secret in Public**

**The puzzle**

Suppose you and I have never met. We want to agree on a secret number, perhaps to use as the key to a lock for our messages. The only way we can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. A spy hears every word we say. It sounds impossible that we could end up with a secret the spy does not know. Yet it can be done, and the idea behind it is lovely. It rests on one fact about arithmetic. Some calculations are easy to do forwards and extremely hard to undo.

**Mixing paint**

Start with a picture. Imagine a large tub of yellow paint that we both agree to use. We say so out loud, so the spy knows about the yellow paint too.

Each of us now privately picks a secret colour. I pick red and you pick blue. I mix my red into a jug of the yellow and send you the jug. You mix your blue into a jug of the yellow and send it to me. The spy sees both jugs travel past. Each one is a blend of yellow and a secret colour.

Now I take your jug and add my own red. You take my jug and add your own blue. We both end up with a mixture of yellow, red and blue, in exactly the same amounts. That muddy final colour is our shared secret.

The spy is stuck. The spy has two jugs, one with yellow and red and one with yellow and blue. Adding them together gives too much yellow. Worse, paint cannot be unmixed. Nobody can look at a jug and pull out the red. The whole trick depends on mixing being easy and unmixing being close to impossible.

Numbers can behave in the same way, and that is what computers use.

**Clock arithmetic**

You already know a kind of arithmetic that wraps around, because you read clocks. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock. You did 10 plus 5, got 15, and then threw away a full circle of 12. What remains after throwing away full circles is called the remainder.

We can use a clock with any number of marks. Let us use a clock with 23 marks. Whenever a number goes past 23, we throw away whole circles of 23 and keep the remainder. For example 25 becomes 2, because 25 is one full circle of 23 plus 2 more.

We also need the idea of a power. Five to the power 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. Five to the power 6 means six fives multiplied together.

Now here is the key. If I tell you that on the 23 mark clock, 5 to the power of my secret number leaves a remainder of 8, can you work out my secret number? With a small clock you could simply try every possibility. But the remainders jump around in a way that looks random, with no pattern to follow. When the clock has hundreds of digits in its size, trying every possibility would take longer than the age of the universe. Meanwhile, going forwards, from the secret number to the remainder, stays quick. Clever shortcuts, such as squaring over and over, let a computer do it in a blink.

This is our paint mixing. Going forwards is mixing. Going backwards is unmixing.

**Doing it with real numbers**

Here is the full exchange using the small clock of 23 marks. In real life the clock is enormously larger.

First, we agree out loud on two public numbers. One is the size of the clock, 23. The other is a base number, 5. The spy hears both. They play the part of the yellow paint.

Next, I choose a secret number, 6. I never say it to anyone. I work out 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625. Throwing away full circles of 23 leaves a remainder of 8. I announce 8 to you. That is my jug.

You choose your own secret number, 15. You work out 5 to the power 15 on the same clock and get 19. You announce 19 to me. That is your jug.

Now comes the clever part. I take your announced number, 19, and raise it to the power of my secret, 6. On the clock this gives 2. You take my announced number, 8, and raise it to the power of your secret, 15. On the clock this also gives 2.

We both land on 2. We never said it aloud, and neither of us ever told the other our secret number.

**Why both of us get the same answer**

This is not luck. Look at what happened. My number was 5 raised to my secret. Your number was 5 raised to your secret. When I raise your number to my secret, I am really taking 5 raised to your secret and then raising that to mine. That equals 5 raised to the product of both secrets. When you raise my number to your secret, you do the same thing in the other order. Multiplying two numbers gives the same result whichever way round you do it, so we meet at the same place. It is the same as the paint ending up with the same amounts of red, yellow and blue regardless of who added what first.

**What the spy knows, and what the spy lacks**

The spy knows the clock size, 23, the base 5, my announcement 8 and your announcement 19. To get the secret 2, the spy would need either my secret number or yours. Finding either one means undoing the clock arithmetic, working out which power of 5 gives 8. On our tiny clock the spy could try a handful of cases and succeed. That is why the tiny example is only a demonstration. With a clock of many hundreds of digits, the spy faces a search so large that no machine can finish it. The spy is left holding two jugs of mixed paint.

**Public keys and private keys**

This exchange is the heart of what is called public key cryptography. The name comes from a neat way of seeing it. Each person has a pair of numbers. One is private and never leaves them. The other is public and can be shouted from the rooftops. The two are linked by arithmetic that is easy in one direction and hopeless in the other. In our example the private number was the secret power and the public number was the announced remainder.

A related scheme turns the idea into a padlock. Imagine I make thousands of open padlocks and hand them out to anyone who asks. I keep the only key. If you want to send me a message, you put it in a box, snap one of my padlocks shut on it, and post it. Everyone can see the padlock and even own copies of it, but only I can open it. Real systems do this with numbers instead of metal. The padlock is a public number and the key is a private number that is related to it. The link between them is a calculation that is simple to build and practically impossible to reverse. A common version relies on the fact that multiplying two huge prime numbers together is quick, but starting from the answer and finding those two primes is not.

**Why this matters**

Whenever your phone connects to a website with a padlock symbol, a conversation like ours happens within a fraction of a second. Your device and the website agree on a secret number over a public line, using this kind of arithmetic. Once they share it, they use it as the key for a fast and ordinary lock that protects everything else you send. No one had to meet beforehand, no one had to hand over a key in person, and anyone listening learns nothing useful.

The wonder of the idea is that secrecy does not come from hiding the method. The spy can know every step and still fail. Security comes from the gap between what is easy and what is hard, between mixing paint and unmixing it.
