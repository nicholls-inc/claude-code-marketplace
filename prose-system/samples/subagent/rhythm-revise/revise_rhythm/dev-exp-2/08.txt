A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. One is astronomical: a day is the time the Earth takes to spin once, so that the sun is back in the same place in the sky. The other is atomic: since the 1950s, clocks that count the vibrations of atoms such as caesium have kept time with extraordinary steadiness, losing perhaps a second over hundreds of millions of years. The Earth is a far less reliable timekeeper, since tides drag on its rotation and shifts in the planet's molten core, ice sheets and oceans nudge it faster or slower, and over the long run it has been slowing slightly, so that the average day is a little longer than the atomic clocks' 86,400 seconds.

In 1972 the international community introduced Coordinated Universal Time, the basis for civil clocks everywhere, and agreed that it would follow atomic time but be kept within a second of the Earth's rotation. Whenever the gap threatened to grow too large, an extra second would be slipped in, usually at the end of June or December, so that a minute briefly had 61 seconds. Twenty-seven have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016.

For most people this made no difference, but for the computer systems that run modern life it has been a persistent headache. Software generally assumes that every minute has exactly 60 seconds, and a minute with 61 can confuse it. Past leap seconds have caused outages at well-known websites and airline systems, and engineers at the large technology companies have complained that the problem is difficult to test, since it happens so rarely. Financial markets, power grids, satellite navigation and telecommunications networks all depend on precisely synchronised time stamps, and an irregular extra second raises the risk of errors.

There is also a new twist: in recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second is removed. That has never been tried, and few trust their software to cope.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. Instead, the gap between atomic time and the Earth's rotation will be allowed to grow, to a larger limit yet to be decided, perhaps a minute or more, which is expected to take a century or so to matter. Whether a different fix will then be needed, perhaps an occasional bigger correction, is left for future generations to decide.
