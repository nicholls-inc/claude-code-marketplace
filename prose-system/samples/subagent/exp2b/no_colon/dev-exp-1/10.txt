A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists outside and relocates it indoors. That is why it can warm a house even when the air outside feels freezing.

The key idea is that "cold" is relative. Air at 0 degrees Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy. Heat only becomes unavailable at absolute zero, which is about minus 273 degrees. So there is plenty of heat in winter air, just at a low temperature. The job of a heat pump is to collect it and lift it to a useful temperature.

It does this with the same cycle your fridge uses, run in the opposite direction. A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, often well below minus 20 degrees. The cycle has four steps.

First, the outdoor unit passes the refrigerant through a coil while a fan blows outside air across it. Even cold air is warmer than the refrigerant, so heat flows into it and the liquid boils into a gas.

Second, a compressor squeezes that gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, in the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. The gas may now be 50 to 70 degrees or hotter.

Third, this hot gas flows through a coil indoors. Because it is hotter than your home's water or air, heat flows out of the refrigerant into your radiators, underfloor heating or hot water cylinder. As it gives up heat, the gas condenses back to a liquid.

Fourth, an expansion valve drops the pressure, which makes the liquid very cold again, and the loop restarts.

The only significant energy input is the electricity driving the compressor and fans. Most of the heat delivered was simply picked up from outside.

This is why heat pumps are measured by a coefficient of performance, or COP. A COP of 3 means that for every 1 unit of electricity, you receive 3 units of heat. Modern units commonly achieve a seasonal figure of 2.5 to 4. A gas boiler, by contrast, tops out below 1, since it can at best convert the fuel's energy to heat, and good condensing models reach about 90 to 95 percent.

Performance does fall as it gets colder, because the compressor has to lift the heat further. A unit might manage a COP of 4 on a mild day and around 2 to 2.5 at minus 7. Good modern models keep running and keep heating down to minus 15 or below, though less efficiently. Defrost cycles also occur in damp, cold weather, when frost builds on the outdoor coil and the unit briefly reverses to clear it.

What this means for your decision

Your home matters as much as the machine. Heat pumps work best at lower flow temperatures, typically 35 to 55 degrees, compared with 65 to 80 for many boilers. A well insulated house with larger radiators or underfloor heating suits them well. A poorly insulated house with small old radiators may need upgrades first, or the pump will run harder and cost more.

Costs vary. Installation is usually more expensive than a new boiler, though grants may cover a good part of it depending on where you live. Running costs depend on the ratio between your electricity and gas prices. Where electricity costs more than about three times as much per unit as gas, savings shrink, and a heat pump with a COP near 3 roughly breaks even. Cheaper tariffs, time of use rates or solar panels improve the picture considerably.

Practical points include space for an outdoor unit, some noise from its fan, and possibly a hot water cylinder indoors if you currently have a combi boiler.

My suggestion is to ask for a heat loss survey from a certified installer. It will show whether your home is ready, which radiators need changing, and what the realistic running cost would be on your tariff. Get at least two quotes, and ask each installer for the expected seasonal COP for your house. If the numbers look good, a heat pump is a sound, lower carbon replacement for a gas boiler.
