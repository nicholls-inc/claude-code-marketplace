The Pump on Broad Street

On the night of 31 August 1854, people in Soho, a crowded working-class district in the heart of London, began falling violently ill. The illness was cholera, and it was terrifying. It struck without warning: severe vomiting, then endless watery diarrhoea that drained the body of fluid so quickly that a healthy adult could be dead within a day. Within three days, 127 people living around Broad Street had died. Within ten days the toll passed 500. Families fled their homes, and shops shut their doors. Whole streets fell silent apart from the rumble of carts carrying away the dead.

Most Victorian doctors thought they knew what caused cholera. The accepted theory was "miasma": the idea that disease rose from rotting filth as a poisonous vapour, a "bad air" that people breathed in. Soho certainly smelled bad. Cellars overflowed with sewage, and animals were kept and slaughtered in back yards. To most observers, the outbreak looked like a plain case of foul air.

One local doctor disagreed. John Snow was forty-one, the son of a Yorkshire labourer, and he had become one of London's most respected physicians. He was an expert in the new science of anaesthesia and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during the birth of her son Leopold the year before. He had also spent years thinking about cholera. In 1849 he had published a short pamphlet arguing that cholera was not breathed in at all. Because it attacked the gut first, he reasoned, it must be swallowed, most likely in water fouled by the waste of people already sick. Few people took the idea seriously. Snow lived only a few streets from Broad Street, and he saw the outbreak as a chance to test his theory.

He started by gathering facts. He got the list of cholera deaths from the General Register Office, the government body that recorded births and deaths, and he walked the streets, knocking on doors and talking to grieving families. As he marked each death on a street map, a pattern appeared. The deaths clustered tightly around one spot: the public water pump on Broad Street. Londoners had no taps in their homes. They carried water in buckets from pumps that drew on shallow wells beneath the street, and the Broad Street pump was popular because its water was thought to be cool and clear.

The details Snow uncovered were even more convincing than the overall pattern, especially the cases that seemed to break it. A large workhouse, a home for the destitute, stood just around the corner on Poland Street and housed more than 500 people, yet only a handful of them died. The workhouse turned out to have its own well. A brewery on Broad Street employed about seventy men, and not one of them died. The workers were given free beer every day and, according to the owner, never drank from the pump. If bad air were to blame, the inmates and brewers should have suffered as badly as their neighbours. Snow saw that what protected them was their water.

Then there were the deaths far from Soho. A widow living in Hampstead, miles away, died of cholera, though nobody else in her neighbourhood fell ill. Snow learned that she had once lived near Broad Street and liked the taste of its water so much that she had a bottle of it brought to her by cart every day. Her niece, who visited and drank the same water, died too. To Snow, cases like these were as telling as a fingerprint.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow presented his evidence to the local parish authorities, the Board of Guardians. They were sceptical, but they agreed to act. The next day, the handle was removed from the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. In the end the outbreak killed around 600 people. Snow admitted honestly that deaths had already been falling before the handle came off, partly because so many residents had fled. Removing the handle did not end the epidemic single-handed. What mattered was the reasoning behind it.

The final piece of the puzzle came from an unexpected ally. Henry Whitehead, a young local clergyman, set out to prove Snow wrong and ended up proving him right. Through his own interviews, Whitehead traced the likely first case: a five-month-old baby at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill just before the outbreak. Her mother had soaked the baby's soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspit at the front of the house. When the cesspit was dug up, it was found to be crumbling and leaking into the well beside the pump, only a few feet away.

The authorities still did not accept Snow's explanation. The pump handle was soon put back, and official reports went on blaming the air. Snow died in 1858, before his ideas won wide acceptance. Over the next few decades, though, as scientists identified the bacterium that causes cholera and cities built proper sewers and clean water supplies, he was proved right. His map of the Soho deaths is now one of the most famous images in medicine. He is remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the science of tracking where diseases come from by patiently gathering evidence about the people they strike.
