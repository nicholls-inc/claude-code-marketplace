Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. The case is not that cars are bad or that nobody should park. It is that a city has no good way to know how much parking each building needs, and the people who do know are the owners and developers who pay for it.

A minimum parking requirement is a rule that obliges anyone building a home, shop or office to provide a set number of spaces. A common standard is two spaces per dwelling, or one space for every 300 square feet of office floor. These numbers usually come from manuals of parking counts taken at suburban sites with plenty of free space. They then become law in places that look nothing like those sites. The result is a rule that is exact on paper and arbitrary in practice.

The first cost is money. A space in a multi-storey garage commonly costs more than $25,000 to build, and an underground space can cost twice that. That cost does not disappear. It is added to the price of the flat, the rent on the shop, or the fare on the restaurant bill, and it is paid by people who do not own cars as well as those who do. Donald Shoup, the economist whose book The High Cost of Free Parking made this argument in 2005, found that the rules hide the price of parking inside everything else. In my view, that is a poor way to run a city, because it asks households to pay for something they have not chosen.

The second cost is land. In many American cities, a large share of the central area is given over to parking. A lot that could hold twenty homes holds fifteen homes and a car park, or a small business that fails the arithmetic and is never built. Small sites suffer most. A narrow plot cannot fit the required spaces and a building on top of them, so it stays empty or is turned into a surface lot. The requirement works as a ban on certain kinds of development, and the ones it bans are often the cheaper and more modest ones.

The usual objection is that without a minimum, developers will build no parking and the streets will fill with cars. The evidence from places that have tried it does not support this. Buffalo, New York, removed its minimums across the whole city in 2017. Auckland did the same in 2016, and Minneapolis followed in 2021. In each case, developers kept building parking where they judged buyers and tenants would want it. What changed is that they could build less where it made sense, near transit or in older neighbourhoods with many people who do not drive. A lender or an owner has every reason to provide enough parking to fill the building. Few of them will gamble on a project that tenants cannot reach.

Removing the minimum is also not the same as removing parking. A developer who wants thirty spaces can still build thirty. A city that wants to manage its kerbs can still charge for them. Shoup argued that the better tool is a fair price for on-street space, set so that about one space in eight stays empty. A requirement tries to solve a shortage by forcing supply. A price solves it by letting use find its level, and the revenue can pay for street improvements in the same area.

There is a fair worry about neighbourhoods where parking is already tight and where residents fear spillover from new buildings. That worry is best met with permit schemes and metered kerbs, which target the problem directly. A blanket minimum for every new building in the city is a blunt answer to a local question. Cities can also phase the change in. Some have dropped the rules first near stations and main streets, where the case is strongest, and widened the area later. California took a version of that route in 2022 for sites close to frequent transit.

There is also a matter of what the rules say about priorities. A city that requires two spaces per home but sets no requirement for a bicycle store, a larger kitchen or a ground-floor shop has decided, without much debate, that cars come first. Many residents might agree if asked. But the decision is usually buried in a zoning schedule that few people read, and it has lasted for decades because it looks technical.

None of this means every city will see a flood of car-free buildings. Where people drive, most new buildings will still have parking, and they should. The point is who decides. At present a rule written in an office years ago decides, and it is applied to every site whatever the local conditions. Removing it hands the decision to those who bear the cost and know the site, and keeps public tools for the cases where parking truly needs managing. That is a more modest role for planners, and I think a more useful one.

Cities that keep the minimums are paying to protect a standard that was never well founded. The sensible step is to repeal them, watch what gets built, and manage the kerb with prices and permits where the evidence shows a problem.
