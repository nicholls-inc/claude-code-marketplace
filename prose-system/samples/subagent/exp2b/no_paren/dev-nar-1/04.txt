The Pump on Broad Street

In late August of 1854, a baby girl living at 40 Broad Street, in the crowded London district of Soho, fell ill with violent diarrhoea. She died on the second of September. By then the neighbourhood was already in the grip of cholera, a disease that makes the body lose water so quickly that a healthy adult can be dead within a day. Within ten days of the outbreak's start on the last day of August, more than five hundred people living within a few streets had died. Many families were wiped out. Some shops stood empty because no one was left to open them.

Almost everyone in London, including most doctors, believed they knew what caused such sickness. They blamed miasma, a foul vapour that was thought to rise from rotting rubbish, sewage and damp ground. Cholera, in this view, was something you breathed in. The remedy was to clean the streets and ventilate the houses.

One physician disagreed. John Snow, who lived a short walk from Broad Street, was a quiet, abstemious man, a teetotaller who drank only water. He was already famous for making surgery bearable. He had worked out how to give ether and chloroform in careful, measured doses, and he had personally given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. But for years he had also been troubled by cholera. In 1849 he had published a short pamphlet arguing that the disease was not breathed in at all. It was swallowed. Something in contaminated water or food, he reasoned, passed from the bowels of the sick into the mouths of the healthy. The illness began in the gut, not the lungs, and that, he thought, was a strong clue.

When the Soho outbreak erupted, Snow saw his chance to test the idea. He did not have a microscope image of the culprit, because no one yet knew that a living organism caused cholera. What he had was shoe leather and arithmetic. He obtained the official list of the dead from the local registrar and began walking the streets, knocking on doors and asking where each household got its drinking water.

The pattern emerged quickly. The deaths clustered tightly around the public water pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street. People living near other pumps were much less affected. Snow drew up a map, marking each death with a black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars piled up around the pump like iron filings around a magnet.

The exceptions mattered as much as the rule. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed hundreds of poor residents, yet only a handful died. Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well and never touched the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street lost not a single worker. The men there were given free beer as part of their pay, and the brewery also had its own supply. Meanwhile, far away in Hampstead, a widow and her niece died of cholera. Snow tracked down the reason. The widow had once lived in Soho and loved the taste of the Broad Street water, so a servant carried a bottle to her every day.

On the seventh of September, Snow took his evidence to the local Board of Guardians, the men who ran parish affairs. He asked them to remove the pump handle so that no one could draw water. The board was sceptical, but the request was modest and the stakes were high, so they agreed. The handle came off the next day.

Here the story is often told too neatly. The outbreak was already fading before the handle was removed, partly because many residents had fled the district. Snow himself admitted this. But his case did not rest on the handle. It rested on the pattern of the deaths and on the stubborn exceptions.

He found an unlikely ally in Henry Whitehead, a young curate at a local church. Whitehead had begun as a doubter, convinced the water could not be to blame. Yet he knew the neighbourhood and its people far better than Snow did, and his own inquiries slowly turned him around. He eventually discovered the likely starting point. The baby at number 40 had been ill before the outbreak, and her mother had washed the child's soiled cloths and thrown the dirty water into a cesspool just beside the house. When workmen later dug up the area, they found the old brick cesspool leaking close to the well's shaft.

Snow's reward was not immediate. An official inquiry in 1855 politely declined to accept his conclusions, and he died in 1858 at only forty-five, with miasma still the dominant doctrine. Yet the proof he had gathered survived. In 1884 the German scientist Robert Koch identified the cholera bacterium, and the argument was over. London built great sewers to carry waste away from its drinking water, and Snow's map became a founding document of modern public health.
