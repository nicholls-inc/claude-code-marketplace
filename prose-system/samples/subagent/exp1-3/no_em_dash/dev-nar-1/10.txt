In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell on Soho, a crowded, poor district of London. The disease was cholera. It begins with sudden, violent diarrhea and vomiting, and it can drain a healthy person of so much fluid that they die within a day or two. In the space of about ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few streets of one another died.

Almost everyone in authority believed they knew what caused it. The dominant idea was the miasma theory: that disease spread through "bad air," a foul vapor rising from rotting waste, sewers and swamps. The smell of London in those years was appalling, so the theory seemed sensible. Few people had any notion that tiny living organisms, too small to see, could make people ill. Germs had not yet been identified as a cause of disease.

John Snow thought differently. He was a physician who lived and worked in Soho, and he had already made a name for himself by studying anesthesia and giving chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. Snow had become convinced that cholera was not breathed in. He suspected it was swallowed, carried in something people ate or drank, and that it attacked the gut first. He had even published this argument in 1849, but few were persuaded. The Soho outbreak gave him the chance to test it.

Snow began with the simplest method he could think of. He went door to door and asked questions. Where had people died? Where did they get their water? London had no pipes bringing clean water into homes in the modern sense. Many residents collected water from public street pumps, and different pumps drew from different sources, some of them badly contaminated by sewage.

He also obtained the official records of deaths from the local registrar and began marking them on a map of the neighborhood. Each death became a small bar drawn at the address where the person had lived. Soon a pattern emerged. The bars clustered thickly around one spot: the water pump on Broad Street. The farther a house was from that pump, the fewer deaths it had.

A map alone could be explained away, so Snow looked for the exceptions, and they proved the most revealing part of his work. A workhouse near the pump housed hundreds of poor residents, yet very few of them died. Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well inside the building, so its residents had no need to use the Broad Street pump. A nearby brewery had almost no cases either. The workers there were given free beer as part of their pay and, it seems, drank little else. They were not drinking from the pump.

Then there were the odd cases that seemed to contradict his idea but ended up supporting it. A woman who lived far away in Hampstead died of cholera. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her each day. Her niece, who drank it too, also died. Meanwhile, other people in the Broad Street area who avoided the pump, by choice or because they had fled, stayed healthy.

By now Snow was sure enough to act. On the evening of September 7, he went before the local board of guardians, the officials who ran the parish, and urged them to disable the pump. They were skeptical, but they agreed to remove the pump handle the following day. Without the handle, no one could draw water. It is one of the most famous small actions in the history of public health.

The outbreak had, in fact, already been fading. Many residents had fled the area, and the death toll was falling before the handle came off. For this reason some historians caution against saying the removal of the handle ended the epidemic by itself. But the evidence Snow gathered did not depend on that single moment.

Later investigation added a crucial piece. A local clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who had initially doubted Snow, did his own careful inquiry. He traced the first case to a baby girl who had fallen ill just before the outbreak. Her family's cesspool, it turned out, lay very close to the Broad Street well, and the brickwork was cracked. Waste from the sick infant's diapers had likely leaked into the water supply. A single contaminated well had set off the whole catastrophe.

The authorities were slow to accept Snow's conclusions, and the miasma theory held on for years. Snow died in 1858 without seeing it overturned. But his method endured: gather data, map it, look for the exceptions, and follow the evidence to the source. Many people regard it as the foundation of modern epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations.
