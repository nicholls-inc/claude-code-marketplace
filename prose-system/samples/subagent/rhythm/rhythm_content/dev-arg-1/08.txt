Every city that still requires developers to build a fixed number of parking spaces per apartment, shop or restaurant should repeal those rules, because they force everyone to pay for something only some people use, they distort the shape of the city in ways nobody voted for, and they rest on a confidence about future demand that planners have never earned.

Consider what a minimum parking requirement actually does. A planning office decides, usually by consulting a manual that averages counts from suburban sites with free lots, that a restaurant needs one space per hundred square feet or that an apartment block needs two spaces per unit, and then the developer must build them whether or not the market would have supplied them. A surface space costs a few thousand dollars and a structured or underground one can run past fifty thousand, and that cost does not vanish; it is folded into the rent, the menu prices and the price of the groceries, so a household that owns no car subsidizes the parking of the household that owns two. Donald Shoup, whose book on the subject became the founding text of this argument, estimated that the requirements amount to a large hidden transfer from people who walk, ride and cycle to people who drive, and nothing about that transfer was ever debated as a matter of fairness because it arrives disguised as a technical standard.

The housing consequences are the part that should trouble even people who like driving. When a lot can hold only so many buildings, every required space displaces something, and what it displaces is often homes. A small site that could carry eight apartments with no parking might carry five once the ramp and stalls are laid out, and the three that disappear are the ones that would have rented for less than the rest. Requirements also kill off the modest, incremental projects that a city needs most, such as converting an old corner shop into a flat above a cafe, because the old building sits on a lot too small to fit the spaces the code demands and so the project is simply never attempted. Buenos Aires, Auckland and Minneapolis have each moved in the direction of removing these mandates, and the early evidence from places that did so is that builders do not stop providing parking; they provide what their tenants and customers will pay for, which in car-dependent neighborhoods is still a great deal and in dense ones is noticeably less.

That last point answers the objection most often raised, which is that without mandates the streets will fill with cars and neighbors will fight over curb space. Removing a requirement does not prohibit parking, any more than the absence of a rule requiring bakeries to sell rye bread prohibits rye bread. Developers who build for buyers who need cars will keep building spaces, lenders will usually insist on them for projects in outlying districts, and anyone who believes a tenant will refuse to lease a flat without a stall can offer one for a separate fee. What changes is that the decision moves from a formula in the zoning code to a negotiation between the person building and the person using, who know far more about local conditions than a ratio drawn up decades ago. Where spillover onto public streets becomes a real problem, the answer is to price the curb, through permits for residents or meters that rise until one or two spaces on each block stay open, which treats scarce street space as the valuable thing it is and raises money that can be spent on the neighborhood instead of leaving it as a free good that everyone races to grab.

There is also the matter of what a city looks like when it has been shaped by these rules for seventy years. Walk through a downtown that has had them since the 1950s and you will find buildings set back behind seas of asphalt, businesses separated from each other by distances too long to walk, and entire blocks given over to storage for vehicles that sit idle most of the day. The requirements made the car necessary by making everything else far away, and then the necessity was cited as the justification for more requirements. Breaking that loop does not mean banning cars or declaring a war on drivers; it means letting a street become compact enough that some of its residents can choose not to own one, a choice that the current rules make nearly impossible in most American and many British and Australian cities.

The strongest case for repeal, though, is modest rather than ideological. Nobody knows how much parking a given building will need in fifteen years, when ride-hailing, remote work, electric bikes and driverless vehicles will have rearranged demand in ways no manual predicts, and a rule that locks in today's guess in concrete is a bet that planners are almost certain to lose. Letting the people who put up the money and live with the result make that call is cheaper, fairer and more adaptable. A city that wants more housing, lower rents and livelier streets can get all three by deleting a few lines from its code, and it is hard to find another reform that costs the public treasury so little and returns so much.
