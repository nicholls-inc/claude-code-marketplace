A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two different ways of measuring a day. Astronomers can time a day by watching the planet rotate, but since the 1960s scientists have also kept time with atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of atoms and are far steadier than any spinning planet. The Earth's rotation is slightly irregular and, on the whole, slowing down because of tidal friction from the Moon, so a rotational day has gradually become a little longer than the 86,400 seconds that the atomic clocks count.

To stop the two timekeepers drifting apart, international authorities introduced the leap second in 1972. Whenever the gap between atomic time and Earth time threatened to exceed 0.9 of a second, an extra second was inserted at the end of June or December, so that the last minute of the day lasted 61 seconds instead of 60. Twenty-seven of them have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016. The system worked well enough for astronomers and navigators, who need civil time to match the position of the sky.

For the computers that now run modern life, however, the extra second has been a persistent headache. Most software assumes that every minute has exactly 60 seconds, and a clock that briefly reads 23:59:60 can confuse it. Leap seconds have caused outages and glitches at major websites, and engineers at large technology companies have developed awkward workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across many hours. Because the adjustments are announced only a few months ahead and cannot be predicted far in advance, they are difficult to plan for. Financial markets, telecommunications networks, satellite navigation and power grids all depend on precisely synchronised time, and any irregularity raises the risk of error.

There is also an odd new twist. In recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which could eventually require a negative leap second, removing a second rather than adding one. That has never been done, and engineers fear it would expose far more bugs.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that governs the international definition of time, voted to abandon the leap second by 2035. After that date the gap between atomic time and Earth time will be allowed to grow much larger, probably to about a minute, before any correction is considered, and perhaps not for a century or more. Most people will never notice the difference.
