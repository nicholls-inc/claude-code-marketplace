Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years someone declares joined-up handwriting dead. Finland dropped its cursive requirement in 2016 in favour of print and typing. The American Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out altogether. The argument is easy to make: children will spend their adult lives at keyboards and touchscreens, the school day is crowded, and an hour spent looping the tail of a "g" into the next letter is an hour not spent on coding, reading or mathematics. Joined handwriting, on this view, belongs with the slide rule and the log table.

I think this view is wrong, and that primary schools should go on teaching children to join their letters. The case does not rest on nostalgia or on any claim that cursive is magic. It rests on three plainer points: handwriting still matters for learning, a fluent hand matters more than a merely legible one, and joining is the most reliable route to fluency that schools have. The cost of teaching it is small, and the cost of dropping it falls hardest on the children who can least afford it.

Start with handwriting in general, because the case against cursive often slides into a case against the pen. The evidence that writing by hand helps young children learn to read is reasonably strong. Brain imaging work by Karin James and colleagues found that pre-literate children who practised forming letters by hand showed activity in the brain's reading networks when they later saw those letters; children who typed or traced them did not. Forming a letter stroke by stroke seems to help fix what it is. Studies of older children by Virginia Berninger and others have found that primary pupils composing by hand wrote more, and expressed more ideas, than when they typed. None of this is surprising. A six-year-old hunting for keys is spending attention on the hunt. Handwriting, once learned, gets out of the way.

That last phrase is the heart of the matter. Writing is two jobs done at once: working out what to say and getting it onto the page. Working memory is limited, and whatever the hand demands is unavailable to the mind. A child whose letter formation is slow and effortful has less attention left for spelling, sentence structure and ideas. Researchers call this the transcription bottleneck, and it shows up consistently: handwriting fluency in the early years predicts the length and quality of children's compositions later, even after allowing for general ability. Teachers see it without the research. The child who writes three laboured lines in twenty minutes is often not short of ideas. They are short of a hand that can keep up.

So the real question is not whether children should write by hand, which almost nobody seriously disputes for the early years, but whether they should be taught to join. Here honesty is needed, because advocates of cursive have overclaimed. There is no good evidence that joined writing makes children cleverer, cures dyslexia, or activates the brain in ways that print cannot. Studies comparing the speed of pure cursive with pure print give mixed results. If the argument for joining depended on those claims, it would fail.

It does not depend on them. The best-supported finding in this area, from Steve Graham's work in the 1990s, is that the fastest and most legible writers use neither pure print nor pure cursive. They use a mixed hand: mostly joined, with lifts where a join would be awkward. That is how most fluent adults write. And it is a style you can only arrive at if you have been taught the joins. A child taught only print has to invent their own shortcuts when speed starts to matter, usually around age nine or ten, and the improvised results are often what makes teenage handwriting unreadable. A child taught to join has a repertoire of efficient movements and can drop the ones that do not suit them. Teaching joined writing is not about enforcing a copperplate ideal. It is about giving children the components of a fast personal hand.

Joining has other practical merits. Writing a word as a connected movement helps with spacing, since the gaps between words become obvious when the letters within them touch. It discourages the reversals that plague early print, because a joined "b" and "d" start in different places and feel different to make. There is also a plausible link with spelling: common letter strings such as "ing" or "tion" become single motor patterns, so the hand learns them as units. The evidence for this is suggestive, not conclusive, but it fits what is known about how motor memory supports recall, and it costs nothing extra to obtain.

Now consider the objections.

The first is that keyboards have made all this irrelevant. But they have not, at least not for children. In England, pupils still sit almost all their public examinations by hand. A sixteen-year-old may write for two hours and more in a single paper, several times a week for a month. A student whose hand cramps or whose script collapses under time pressure is at a real disadvantage, and it is not one that reflects their knowledge. One can argue that exams should move to screens, and perhaps they will, but schools must prepare children for the system that exists. Beyond exams, people still write notes in meetings, annotate documents, fill in forms, and leave messages. And there is some evidence, though contested in replication, that students who take notes by hand process material more deeply than those who type, because they cannot transcribe verbatim and must summarise. Typing should be taught too. The choice between the two is a false one; children need both, as they need both mental arithmetic and a calculator.

The second objection is time. The curriculum is full, and something must give. But the time involved is modest. Effective handwriting teaching is short and frequent: ten or fifteen minutes a day in the early years, tapering off as fluency arrives. England's national curriculum expects children to begin joining in Year 2 and to be writing fluently by the end of primary school. Compared with the time later lost to slow, effortful writing in every subject for a decade, this is a cheap investment. Schools that drop explicit handwriting teaching do not gain the minutes back; they pay them out again, with interest, in every lesson where a child cannot get their thinking down.

The third objection is that joined writing is hard on some children, particularly those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties, and that insisting on it causes misery. This is a serious point, and the answer is flexibility, not abolition. Some children will always do better with print or with a keyboard, and a good school lets them. But for many children with coordination difficulties, a continuous joined movement is actually easier than print, which requires repeatedly lifting the pen and finding the right place to put it down again. That is why several specialist dyslexia organisations have long recommended continuous cursive. A sensible policy teaches joining to all, and adapts for the individual, which is what we do in every other subject.

The fourth objection is that cursive is simply a cultural relic, valued for its own sake by people who had it drilled into them. There is some truth in the charge as applied to the fussier traditions: the elaborate loops, the marks deducted for an unorthodox "r". Schools should not teach penmanship as an art form or punish a clear hand for being unconventional. The goal is speed and legibility, and a plain, lightly joined script serves it best. But the relic argument cuts the other way too. A generation that cannot write joined script will struggle to read it, and a great deal is written in it: grandparents' letters, parish records, the marginal notes of the past. That is not the main reason to teach it, but it is not nothing.

Finally, consider who loses if schools stop. Children from homes with books, tutors and attentive parents will learn a fluent hand anyway, as they learn most things anyway. The children who depend on school for the skill are those with the fewest advantages elsewhere. It is telling that after a decade of experiment, many American states have reversed course; California made cursive instruction compulsory again in 2024, and more than twenty states now require it. They did so not out of sentiment but because teachers reported pupils who could not write quickly enough to show what they knew.

Joined-up handwriting is not sacred. If the day comes when no child ever needs to write more than a signature, the case will lapse. That day has not arrived, and primary schools are not the place to bet that it will. Until then, a few minutes a day spent teaching children to join their letters buys them a hand that keeps pace with their thoughts. Few things on the timetable offer so much for so little. Schools should keep the joins.
