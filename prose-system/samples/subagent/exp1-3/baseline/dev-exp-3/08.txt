## The problem

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to talk privately, but every message passes along a line that anyone can listen to. For thousands of years, secret codes needed a shared key. This is a secret, such as a word or a number, that both sides know and nobody else does. Sending that key was the weak point. If you could meet in person to swap it, you wouldn't need the line at all. If you sent it over the line, an eavesdropper could copy it.

In the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around this. Alice and Bob can build a shared secret out of messages that an eavesdropper sees every word of, and the eavesdropper still can't work out the secret. This sounds impossible, so it's worth seeing how it works.

## The idea: easy one way, hard the other

The trick rests on a task that is easy to do but very hard to undo. Everyday life has plenty of these. Mixing paint is easy: you can't un-mix yellow and blue back into the original tins. Multiplying is easier than factorising. You can multiply 101 by 113 in seconds to get 11,413. But if I hand you 11,413 and ask which two numbers multiplied to make it, you have to hunt.

Here is the paint version of the secret-sharing scheme.

1. Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common colour, say yellow. Everyone can see it.
2. Alice secretly picks a colour of her own, say red. She mixes it with the yellow and sends the mixture to Bob.
3. Bob secretly picks his own colour, say blue. He mixes it with the yellow and sends the mixture to Alice.
4. Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her secret red. Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his secret blue.

Both now hold yellow + red + blue. Order doesn't matter when mixing, so they hold the same colour. The eavesdropper saw the yellow and the two mixtures. To get the final colour, they would need to strip out one of the secret colours. That is the un-mixing that can't be done.

The real version replaces paint with arithmetic that works the same way.

## The clock-arithmetic version

We need a mixing operation that is easy to do and hard to reverse. Here it is, using "clock arithmetic". On a 12-hour clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, because you wrap around after 12. We write this as "remainder after dividing". So 15 divided by 12 leaves 3.

Real systems use a bigger clock, with a prime number of hours. Let's use a clock with 23 hours, and write "mod 23" to mean "take the remainder after dividing by 23".

The public starting point is two numbers that everyone knows: **23** (the clock size) and **5** (the "base").

The mixing step is to raise 5 to some power, then take the remainder mod 23. For example, 5 to the power 2 is 25. Dividing 25 by 23 leaves 2. So 5² mod 23 = 2.

This is easy going forwards. Going backwards is the hard part. If I tell you that 5 to some power leaves remainder 19, and ask you what the power was, there is no neat formula. The remainders jump around in an erratic way, so you are reduced to trying powers one by one. With a 23-hour clock that takes seconds. With a clock of several hundred digits, the number of tries is more than the number of atoms in the universe.

### A worked example

Here is the whole exchange with small numbers.

**Public:** clock size 23, base 5.

**Alice** takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power: 19⁶ mod 23 = **2**.

**Bob** takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power: 8¹⁵ mod 23 = **2**.

They both get **2**. That is their shared secret, and it never travelled along the line.

### Why they always match

Alice's calculation amounts to 5 raised to (15 × 6). Bob's amounts to 5 raised to (6 × 15). Multiplication doesn't care about order, so both reach the same place. Each of them used a number the other never saw. That is the same trick as the paint: each adds their own secret to a mixture that already holds the other's.

### Why the eavesdropper is stuck

To get the secret, the eavesdropper needs 6 or 15. They would have to look at the 8 and ask "5 to what power gives remainder 8?" That is the hard backwards step. With our toy clock they could just try every power and succeed in seconds. With a clock of 600 digits and secrets of similar size, the mixing takes a computer a fraction of a second, but the backwards search is out of reach with any computer we know how to build.

## From a shared secret to private messages

The shared number is then used as the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method, which both sides can run because they now hold the same key. The slow clever maths is used only to agree on the key. The fast scrambling does the work of protecting the conversation itself.

## The second famous method: locks and keys

There is another approach, which gives public-key cryptography its name. Imagine Bob makes a padlock and a key. He keeps the key to himself and gives away copies of the open padlock to anyone who wants one. Alice snaps a padlock shut around her message and sends the box. Only Bob has the key, so only he can open it. The eavesdropper may well have a padlock of their own, but a padlock can't open something it has already locked.

In the maths, the "padlock" is a big number made by multiplying two enormous primes. Anyone can use it to scramble a message. Unscrambling needs the two primes, and finding them from the product is the hard backwards task. This is the same easy-forwards, hard-backwards pattern as before. This method is known as RSA, after its inventors.

## What this does and doesn't guarantee

Two caveats are worth knowing.

First, the security rests on the belief that the backwards step really is hard. Nobody has proved it. It has resisted decades of effort from very capable people, but it is a bet, and new kinds of computers could change the odds. That is why researchers are already designing replacements.

Second, the scheme protects against someone listening. It does not by itself protect against someone who intercepts the line and pretends to be Bob. Alice would then agree a secret with the impostor. To prevent this, real systems add a way of checking identity, such as certificates that vouch for who owns a public number.

## The takeaway
