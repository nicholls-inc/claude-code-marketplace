Thirteen Minutes from Disaster: The Story of Apollo 13

On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 2:13 in the afternoon, Houston time. It was the third mission meant to land humans on the Moon, and by then the public had begun to treat lunar flight as routine. Television networks had stopped broadcasting from the spacecraft. The crew was commander Jim Lovell, on his fourth spaceflight; Fred Haise, a lunar module pilot on his first; and Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons shortly before launch. Swigert, the backup, took his seat.

The flight began almost normally. The center engine of the second stage shut down early, but the other engines burned longer to compensate, and the spacecraft reached orbit. After a trans-lunar injection burn, the command and service module, named Odyssey, separated from the third stage, turned around, and docked with the lunar module, named Aquarius. Over the next two days the crew settled into a quiet cruise toward the Moon.

Nothing could have told them that the accident had been set up years earlier. Oxygen tank number two in the service module had been manufactured with thermostatic switches rated for 28 volts, but the launch pad power supply ran at 65 volts. During a ground test before launch, the heaters in the tank ran for a long time and the switches fused shut. That damaged the insulation on the wiring inside, leaving bare copper wire in a tank of liquid oxygen. No one noticed.

On the evening of April 13, about 56 hours into the flight and roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, mission control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that keeps the contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A few moments later, at 9:08 p.m. Houston time, there was a bang. A spark in the damaged wiring ignited the insulation, the pressure in the tank spiked, and the tank ruptured, damaging the service module. Lovell felt the shudder through the spacecraft. Swigert then said the words that became famous in a slightly altered form: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, adding that there had been a main B bus undervolt.

At first, controllers thought they might be looking at faulty instruments. Then the numbers got worse. Oxygen tank two read empty. Tank one was leaking. Fuel cells, which made the spacecraft's electricity and water from oxygen and hydrogen, began failing. Looking out a window, Lovell saw gas venting into space. He reported it, and everyone on the ground and in the spacecraft understood what it meant. Odyssey was dying.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his team had to change the goal of the mission within minutes. Landing on the Moon was no longer possible. The task now was to bring the crew home alive. The command module could not sustain them for the roughly four days the trip would take, and it needed to preserve its small batteries for reentry. The answer lay in the lunar module still docked to it. Designed to keep two men alive for about two days on the Moon, Aquarius would serve as a lifeboat for three men for four.

The crew powered down Odyssey almost completely and moved into Aquarius. Swigert had to carefully shut down the command module in a sequence that had never been done in flight. The team on the ground also had to work out a trajectory. The spacecraft was on a free-return path that would swing around the Moon and back toward Earth, but earlier course adjustments had moved it off that path. A burn using Aquarius's descent engine, made after the craft had passed behind the Moon and emerged, put the ship back on a faster route home. A later burn corrected the trajectory again.

The days that followed were a test of endurance. To save power, nearly everything was turned off, and the spacecraft grew bitterly cold; temperatures fell to a few degrees above freezing, and condensation formed on the walls. Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection. The crew had little to drink because water was rationed to preserve what was needed for cooling the electronics. They slept little and, to save water, ate little.

A more urgent problem came from the air. The lunar module's canisters of lithium hydroxide, which remove carbon dioxide from the cabin, were meant for two people over a short stay. With three men aboard for days, the carbon dioxide level climbed toward dangerous levels. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round. Engineers on the ground, working with what the crew had on board, devised an adapter from cardboard, plastic bags, hoses, and duct tape. They read out the instructions over the radio, and the crew assembled it, and the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

Back on Earth, the effort was enormous. Engineers in Houston, along with contractors and astronauts working in simulators, wrote and tested procedures for the most difficult one of all: restarting the command module. It had to be brought back to life after days of being cold and dead, using only the limited power in its batteries, in an order that would not drain them before reentry. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been taken off the flight, worked in a simulator for long hours on the procedure that would be sent up to the crew.

On April 17, as Apollo 13 approached Earth, the crew shed the damaged service module. For the first time they could see what had happened. One whole panel of the service module was gone, and the equipment inside was visibly damaged. Lovell described the sight to Houston. Then they said farewell to Aquarius, which had saved them, after moving back into Odyssey and powering it up. Once the lunar module was released, it burned up in the atmosphere.

Reentry came with one more uncertainty. Radio contact normally drops out for about three minutes as the plasma around the capsule blocks signals. This time the blackout lasted longer, and at mission control people waited in silence while the clock passed the expected time. Four and a half minutes in, then longer. Finally, Swigert's voice came through, and parachutes were seen. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa at about 1:07 p.m. Houston time on April 17, 1970, close to the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The crew had been in space nearly six days.

An investigation board, led by NASA's Edgar Cortright, traced the cause to the tank's damaged wiring and the thermostat switches, and recommended redesigning the oxygen tanks and removing the fire hazards. Later flights carried changes, including an additional oxygen tank and battery in the service module.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, but many people think of it as one of NASA's finest moments. The mission was called a successful failure. A crew survived because a roomful of engineers and three astronauts chose to solve a problem together rather than accept an outcome that looked inevitable.
