Imagine you and a stranger want to agree on a secret number, but every word you say passes through a room full of eavesdroppers. It sounds impossible. If they hear everything, how can anything stay hidden? Yet this is what your phone does every time it opens a secure website. The trick is called public-key cryptography, and its core idea fits on a page.

**The problem with ordinary secrets**

For thousands of years, secret messages worked like a locked box with one key. You scramble a message with a key, and your friend unscrambles it with the same key. This works well, but only if you both already hold the key. If you must send the key itself, anyone listening can copy it, and the scheme collapses.

For centuries this was simply accepted. To talk secretly, people had to meet first, in person, and share a key. That is useless for a shop you have never visited or a friend on another continent.

In the 1970s, researchers found a way out. The key idea is to use a calculation that is easy to do in one direction and extremely hard to undo.

**Easy one way, hard the other**

Think of mixing paint. Mixing yellow and blue to get green takes seconds. Looking at a pot of green and working out exactly which two paints went in is much harder, and if the mix was complicated, it is close to impossible.

Maths has its own versions of this. Multiplying two large prime numbers is easy. Given only the answer, finding the two primes is brutally slow. Another one is the subject of this essay: a kind of clock arithmetic, where going forwards is quick and going backwards is a nightmare.

**Clock arithmetic**

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You divide by 12 and keep the remainder. Mathematicians call this working modulo 12, and we will use the word "mod" as a shortcut. Fifteen mod 12 is 3.

We can use a different clock size. Suppose our clock has 23 hours. Then 30 mod 23 is 7, because 23 goes into 30 once with 7 left over.

We also need powers. Five to the power of 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. In clock arithmetic with 23 hours, 125 becomes 125 mod 23. Since 23 times 5 is 115, the remainder is 10.

Here is the key fact. If I tell you that 5 to the power of some secret number gives 10 on the 23-hour clock, finding the secret number is a guessing game. With small numbers you can just try them all. But when the clock has hundreds of digits, there are more possibilities than there are atoms in the universe, and no known shortcut for the guesser.

**The exchange, step by step**

Now meet our characters. Alice and Bob have never met. Eve is listening to everything they say.

First, Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers. They say them out loud, and Eve hears. The first is the clock size, which we will make 23. The second is a base number, which we will make 5. These are not secret at all.

Second, each of them privately picks a secret number and tells nobody. Alice picks 6. Bob picks 15.

Third, each of them does a calculation with their secret and sends the result to the other.

Alice works out 5 to the power of 6, then takes the remainder on the 23-hour clock. Five to the power of 6 is 15625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 with 8 left over. So Alice sends the number 8.

Bob works out 5 to the power of 15 on the same clock. The raw number is huge, but he can keep shrinking it as he goes. The result is 19. So Bob sends 19.

Eve hears both messages. She now knows the clock size 23, the base 5, and the two numbers 8 and 19. She does not know 6 or 15.

Fourth, and this is the clever part, each person takes the number they received and raises it to the power of their own secret.

Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret 6, then takes the remainder on the 23-hour clock. The answer is 2.

Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret 15, then takes the remainder. The answer is also 2.

They have the same number. That number, 2, is their shared secret. Neither of them ever sent it. It was never spoken aloud.

**Why does it match?**

This is not luck. Look at what each person actually computed. Alice built 5 to the power of her secret, then Bob raised that to his secret. Bob built 5 to the power of his secret, then Alice raised that to hers. In both cases the result is 5 raised to the power of Alice's secret times Bob's secret.

Multiplication does not care about order. Six times 15 equals 15 times 6. So both routes end in the same place. The shared secret is 5 to the power of 90, taken on the clock, and each person reached it by a different path.

**Why Eve is stuck**

To get the secret, Eve would need either 6 or 15. Those are hidden inside the numbers 8 and 19. To dig them out she would need to answer a question such as: 5 to the power of what gives 8 on the 23-hour clock? With a clock this small, she can try the numbers one at a time and find the answer in a minute. That is why real systems never use such a small clock.

In real use, the clock has more than six hundred digits, and the secrets are similarly huge. Alice and Bob's computers can still do their calculations in a fraction of a second, using a trick of squaring again and again to avoid writing out gigantic numbers. But for Eve, trying every possible secret would take longer than the age of the universe. The calculation is easy to do forwards and hopeless to undo.

Paint again: Alice and Bob each mix a private colour into a shared base colour, swap the mixtures, then add their own private colour a second time. Both finish with the same final shade. Eve sees the base and the two swapped mixtures, but cannot unmix them.

**Where the name comes from**

The version above, usually called Diffie-Hellman after its inventors, is the simplest form. A close cousin, called RSA, uses the multiply-primes trick instead and is described with a public key and a private key. Think of a padlock. You can make thousands of copies of an open padlock and hand them to anyone. Anyone can snap one shut around a message, but only you hold the key that opens it. The padlock is the public key, and the key in your pocket is the private key. That is where the name public-key cryptography comes from.

**What happens next**

Once Alice and Bob share the secret number, they use it as an ordinary key with a fast, traditional scrambling method. The slow, clever maths is used only to agree on the key. After that, everything they say is locked, and Eve, who heard every word of the setup, hears only noise.

Each time you see a padlock symbol in your browser, something like this has just happened in the blink of an eye. Two machines that had never met agreed on a secret in public, using nothing more than remainders, powers, and one very lopsided calculation.
