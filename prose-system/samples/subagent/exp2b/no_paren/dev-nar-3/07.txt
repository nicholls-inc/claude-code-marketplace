On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to around 40 degrees Fahrenheit, a sharp rise from the freezing cold of the previous days. Just after noon, workers, children and families went about their business along Commercial Street, near the waterfront.

Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions and, with Prohibition only days from taking effect, in rum and other drink.

The tank had been built hastily in 1915, and it had a troubled history. It leaked so badly that the company painted it brown to hide the stains. Neighborhood children collected the molasses that dribbled from its seams. Inspections were minimal, and the builder had never tested the tank by filling it with water. A fresh shipment had been pumped in the day before, and the sudden warmth had thinned the cold, thick syrup.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, residents heard a low rumble, followed by a sound some compared to a machine gun, as the rivets shot out of the steel. Then the tank burst. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged down the street at around thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a force heavy enough to knock buildings off their foundations and to buckle the supports of the elevated railway. Wagons, horses and people were swept up in the flood.

Those caught in the wave found themselves in a sticky, cold trap. As the molasses cooled, it thickened, and rescuers struggled to reach victims who were pinned beneath debris or lay stuck in the goo. Boston police, firefighters, Red Cross workers and sailors from a nearby ship waded in to help. Some of the dead were so coated in syrup that they were hard to identify. In all, twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Horses perished too, and the cleanup stretched on for weeks. Fire crews washed the streets with salt water from fireboats, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer. For decades afterward, locals said that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled faintly of molasses.

The disaster led to one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company blamed anarchists, claiming that a bomb had destroyed the tank. But after years of testimony, an auditor concluded that the structure had simply been poorly designed and built, with steel too thin and safety checks absent. The company was ordered to pay damages, and the families of victims received settlements of about 7,000 dollars each.

The flood also reshaped American building practice. It helped push states to require licensed engineers and architects to sign off on construction plans, and it strengthened the case for independent oversight of structures. A once-comic image became a sober lesson about corporate neglect.
