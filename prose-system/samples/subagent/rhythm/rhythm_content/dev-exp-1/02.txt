A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything; it moves heat that already exists in the outdoor air into your house, and the reason this works on a cold day is that "cold" is relative. Air at minus five Celsius still holds a great deal of thermal energy, because absolute zero is far below that, at about minus 273. A heat pump is essentially a refrigerator running in reverse, with the cold box being the outdoors and the warm room being your home.

Inside the sealed loop is a refrigerant, a fluid chosen because it boils at very low temperatures, well below freezing. In the outdoor unit, a fan pulls air across a coil in which the refrigerant is colder than the air, so heat flows from the air into the refrigerant and boils it into a gas. That gas then passes through a compressor, which squeezes it and in doing so raises its temperature sharply, much as a bicycle pump warms up when you work it hard, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor hotter than your radiators need to be. It then flows through a second coil indoors, where it gives up that heat to your heating water or to the air in the house, and as it cools it condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve drops its pressure and temperature again, so that it is cold enough to start absorbing outdoor heat once more, and the cycle repeats continuously.

The compressor is the only major part that consumes electricity, and the key figure is that it moves far more heat than the electricity it uses. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance. A typical modern air-source heat pump might achieve around three on a mild day, meaning each unit of electricity delivers three units of heat, and perhaps two to two and a half when it is freezing. A gas boiler, by comparison, can never exceed roughly 90 to 95 percent efficiency, so even the weaker winter figure for a heat pump is more than double what the boiler offers. The colder it gets outside, the harder the compressor has to work to lift heat to the required temperature, so efficiency falls gradually rather than the system suddenly failing. Most current models keep delivering their rated output down to minus ten or even minus twenty, although you should check the specification for your climate.

For a homeowner weighing a replacement, several practical points matter more than the physics. Heat pumps deliver water at lower temperatures than a boiler, commonly 35 to 55 degrees against 70 or more, and they are most efficient when running steadily at those lower temperatures. This means your existing radiators may be too small, and a good installer will do a room-by-room heat loss calculation and may recommend larger radiators or underfloor heating in some rooms. The house itself matters as well, because a poorly insulated home with draughty windows demands more heat, which costs more to supply, so loft insulation and draught-proofing often pay back alongside the installation. You will usually also need a hot water cylinder, and space outside for the unit, which makes some noise, comparable to a quiet fridge at a short distance.

Running costs depend on the relationship between your electricity and gas prices. Because electricity usually costs several times as much per unit as gas, a heat pump needs a coefficient of performance above that price ratio to save you money, and in many regions it only does so narrowly, so a time-of-use tariff or solar panels can tip the balance considerably. Upfront costs are higher than for a new boiler, often by several thousand pounds or dollars, although government grants in many countries offset a large part of this. A fair way to decide is to get two or three quotes that each include a heat loss survey, ask for the predicted seasonal efficiency and annual running cost for your specific house, and compare those with your current gas bills, rather than relying on general claims in either direction.
