Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, or at least stop treating those estimates as promises, because the practice costs far more than it returns. Most engineers have lived the same story. A manager asks for a number, the team gives a guess wrapped in caveats, and within a week the guess has hardened into a deadline. When the work runs long, as it almost always does, nobody discusses what the guess was built on. The conversation turns to who slipped and why.

The trouble is that estimating is hard for a reason that effort cannot fix. Software work is mostly the discovery of things you did not know, and you cannot price what you have not yet found. The unfamiliar library, the tangled legacy module, and the requirement that turns out to contradict another one all surface after the estimate is given. Decades of research on planning, along with the ordinary experience of every team, shows that people underestimate consistently and that the errors are not random. Better technique helps a little at the margins, but it does not change the underlying uncertainty.

Meanwhile the estimating itself eats real time. Planning poker sessions, story point debates, and quarterly roadmap negotiations pull the most senior people into hours of meetings whose output is a number that will be wrong. Worse, the number distorts behavior. Engineers pad their figures to protect themselves, managers quietly trim them, and everyone learns that the safest move is to hit the date rather than to build the right thing. Teams cut corners on quality to meet a forecast they never believed in, and the debt that follows is paid by the same people a quarter later.

The alternative is less radical than it sounds. Keep work in small pieces, ship them continuously, and let the flow of finished items tell you what the team can actually deliver. A team that completes six or eight small changes a week can say, with far more honesty than any estimate allows, roughly where it will be in a month. That forecast comes from observed history rather than hopeful prediction, and it updates itself as reality changes. If a piece of work cannot be broken into small steps, that is itself valuable information, and it is better learned early than discovered at the deadline.

Managers will reasonably object that the business needs dates, and that is fair. Salespeople make commitments, launches need coordination, and money has to be budgeted. But those needs are better served by honest ranges, clear priorities, and a willingness to cut scope as the deadline nears than by a false precision that collapses on contact. A fixed date with flexible scope is a promise a team can keep. A fixed scope with a guessed date is a promise it usually cannot.

Dropping estimates will not make the uncertainty disappear. It will stop us from pretending the uncertainty is not there, and it will give engineers and managers an honest footing for the conversations they most need to have. That alone is worth the discomfort of giving up a ritual we have all agreed to perform.
