Let Cities Stop Dictating Parking

Walk through almost any American city and you will see the same strange sight. Vast fields of asphalt sit empty beside shops, apartments and offices, while the buildings themselves shrink to make room for them. This is not an accident of the market. It is the direct result of minimum parking requirements, rules that force developers to build a set number of spaces whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these rules, and the sooner the better.

Start with what the requirements actually are. A planning code might demand two spaces per apartment, one space for every 300 square feet of retail, or a certain number of spaces per restaurant seat. These ratios did not come from careful study of what the market would supply. Many were borrowed from other towns, or from a handbook that rested on a handful of suburban surveys. The numbers are guesses dressed up as science, and they are applied to every lot regardless of whether it sits beside a train station or at the edge of a highway.

The first problem is cost. A surface space can run several thousand dollars to build, and a space in a garage or underground can cost tens of thousands. Someone has to pay for that. Developers fold the expense into rents and sale prices, which means a tenant who owns no car still subsidizes the parking of the neighbors who do. Housing is already scarce and expensive in most growing cities. Forcing every new home to carry the price of a parking space it may not need makes the shortage worse. Some projects never get built at all, because the required spaces eat so much of the lot that the numbers stop working.

The second problem is space. Land is the most valuable thing a city has. Every acre given over to parked cars is an acre that cannot hold homes, shops, a park or a workplace. Downtowns that could be lively become gaps between buildings, with walking distances stretched until nobody wants to walk. Small businesses suffer in particular. A corner lot that could hold a bakery with a few tables may be impossible to develop once the code demands a dozen spaces. Old buildings in historic districts often could not be built today, because the rules that produced them would now forbid them.

The third problem is how the rules shape behavior. When parking is mandated in abundance and then offered free, driving becomes the default for every trip. Streets fill with traffic, transit struggles to attract riders, and the places people most want to visit become the hardest to reach on foot. Cities that say they want cleaner air, safer streets and lower emissions often undermine those goals with their own zoning code.

Defenders of the rules raise a fair worry. If developers build less parking, will cars simply spill onto neighborhood streets and make life miserable for residents? It is a reasonable concern, but it points to the right tool. Street parking can be managed with permits, time limits and modest pricing. Cities that charge fairly for curb space find that spots turn over, drivers circle less, and nearby streets stay usable. Nothing in this approach requires a developer to pour concrete that nobody asked for.

Another objection is that removing the minimum means removing parking. It does not. Repeal does not ban a single space. It simply lets the builder decide. A suburban grocery store will keep a large lot because its customers arrive by car, and a lender will insist on adequate parking for a project that needs it. What changes is that a developer who knows the site is near a bus line, or who is serving residents who prefer to go without a car, is no longer forced to overbuild. The decision moves from a distant planning office to the person who actually bears the risk and understands the location.

The evidence is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and developers there went on to build projects with fewer spaces and plenty of housing. Other cities, including Minneapolis and Anchorage, have followed with their own reforms, and states such as California and Oregon have limited the reach of these mandates near transit. The sky has not fallen in any of these places. Most new buildings still include parking, but in amounts that match demand rather than a formula.

There is also a question of basic fairness. Households that do not own cars are disproportionately young, elderly or low income, and they are exactly the people who pay twice under the current system. They bear the hidden cost of parking they do not use and often live in places made harder to navigate without a car. Repeal treats all residents more even-handedly by letting prices reflect real choices.

Cities worry, understandably, about change. But minimum parking requirements are a policy that has had decades to prove itself and has delivered costlier housing, emptier streets and more traffic. Removing them is cheap, requires no new spending, and hands decisions back to people with the best information. Planners should keep managing the curb and investing in transit, walking and cycling. They should stop telling every builder how many cars to plan for. Let the market and the neighborhood decide, and let the asphalt give way to something better.
