How two strangers agree on a secret in full view of an eavesdropper

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met, and the only way they can talk is over a line that anyone can listen to. Alice wants to send Bob a message she does not want others to read. The usual answer is to scramble the message with a shared secret key, so that only someone holding the same key can unscramble it. That creates a problem. To share the key, Alice has to send it to Bob, and anyone listening on the line gets it too. Cryptographers call the listener Eve, and Eve sees every message sent. For most of history, the only fix was for the two people to meet, or to pass a key by courier, before they needed to communicate in secret.

Public-key key agreement, first published by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976, removes that requirement. Alice and Bob exchange only messages that Eve is allowed to see, yet at the end both hold the same secret number, and Eve, who saw everything, cannot work out what it is. The technique is best described as a way of agreeing on a key, not a way of encrypting a message. Once they share the key, they can use a faster, conventional cipher to do the actual scrambling.

A paint analogy

Before the arithmetic, consider a picture that captures the shape of the idea. Alice and Bob agree in public on a base colour, say yellow. Each then picks a private colour that they keep to themselves. Alice's is red; Bob's is blue. Each mixes their private colour into the shared yellow and sends the mixture to the other. Alice receives a yellow-blue mixture from Bob and adds her red to it. Bob receives a yellow-red mixture from Alice and adds his blue. Both end up with the same colour: yellow mixed with red and blue. Eve sees the yellow, the yellow-red mixture and the yellow-blue mixture. To find the final colour she would need to separate out the red or the blue, and the mixture is hard to unmix.

The analogy has a flaw that matters. Paint is easy to unmix in a lab, so the real mathematical version needs an operation that is easy to perform in one direction and hard to reverse. Modular arithmetic provides exactly that.

Clock arithmetic

Most people already know modular arithmetic without the name. On a clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, because after 12 the count wraps round. Mathematicians write this as 10 + 5 = 3 (mod 12). In general, "a mod p" means the remainder when a is divided by p. So 17 mod 5 is 2, because 17 = 3 × 5 + 2.

Cryptography uses the same wrap-around idea with a very large p, often a number with hundreds of digits. The numbers stay enormous but always fall in a fixed range, from 0 up to p minus 1.

The one-way step

Key agreement uses powers. We write g^a to mean g multiplied by itself a times, and we always take the result mod p. Two facts from school algebra make the scheme work. First, (x^m) raised to the power n equals x^(m×n). Second, since everything is taken mod p, you can compute huge powers quickly by repeated squaring, so even a number with hundreds of digits takes only a few hundred multiplications.

The reverse problem is different. Given p, g and the result A = g^a mod p, finding the exponent a is called the discrete logarithm problem. For suitably large p, no method is known that solves it in any practical time. Computing the power is easy, and finding the exponent from it is believed to be very hard. That asymmetry is the one-way door that the whole scheme relies on.

A worked example with small numbers

Let p = 23 and g = 5. These numbers are far too small to be safe, but they show every step.

Alice picks a secret a = 6. She computes A = 5^6 mod 23 = 8 and sends 8 to Bob in public.

Bob picks a secret b = 15. He computes B = 5^15 mod 23 = 19 and sends 19 to Alice in public.

Alice receives 19 and computes 19^6 mod 23 = 2.

Bob receives 8 and computes 8^15 mod 23 = 2.

Both now hold 2. Why is this not a coincidence? Alice computed (g^b)^a = g^(a×b). Bob computed (g^a)^b = g^(a×b). Both are the same number because multiplication does not care about order.

Eve has seen 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the shared 2 she needs either a or b, which means solving a discrete logarithm. With numbers this small she could simply try every exponent, but a real system uses primes of around 2048 bits, or elliptic-curve groups with equivalent strength, for which that search is hopeless with any known method.

What the scheme does not do

Diffie-Hellman answers one question, how to agree on a secret, but it leaves others open. The biggest is identity. Nothing in the exchange proves that the number arriving from "Bob" really came from Bob. An attacker, usually called Mallory, can sit in the middle, run one exchange with Alice pretending to be Bob and another with Bob pretending to be Alice, and read everything. The standard fix is authentication: each side signs its public number with a long-term key whose owner is verified in advance, often through a certificate issued by a trusted authority.

A second property worth knowing is forward secrecy. If each conversation uses freshly chosen private numbers that are then discarded, then even if a long-term key is stolen later, past conversations cannot be decrypted, because the secrets that protected them no longer exist anywhere. Modern secure web connections are built this way.

A third concern is the future. A sufficiently large quantum computer could solve the discrete logarithm problem efficiently, using an algorithm published by Peter Shor in 1994. Standards bodies have therefore been developing post-quantum key agreement methods, and some are already being deployed alongside the classical ones.

The central idea

The secret is never sent. Each side builds it separately, from its own private number and a public number received from the other. The public numbers are safe to reveal because turning them back into the private numbers is the hard, one-way step. Two strangers, speaking in full view of an eavesdropper, can arrive at the same secret by doing easy arithmetic in opposite orders, and the power of public-key cryptography lies in that asymmetry.
