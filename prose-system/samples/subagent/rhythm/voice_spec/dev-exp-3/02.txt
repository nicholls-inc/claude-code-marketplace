Two people, whom I will call Alice and Bob, want to agree on a secret number. They have never met and they share no earlier secret. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to, and a third person, whom I will call Eve, hears every word. It sounds impossible, because whatever Alice tells Bob, Eve hears as well. It can be done. The method was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, and it depends on a kind of sum that is quick to do in one direction and very slow to undo.

The sums use clock arithmetic. On a clock with twelve hours, ten o'clock plus five hours is three o'clock, because 15 is one full lap of 12 with 3 left over. Only the leftover counts. The same idea works for a clock of any size. Here we will use a clock with 23 positions, numbered 0 to 22. On this clock 30 counts as 7, since 30 is one lap of 23 plus 7, and 50 counts as 4, since 50 is two laps, which is 46, plus 4.

Now take the number 5 and multiply it by itself, reducing to the clock after every step. The results run 5, then 25, which is 2 on this clock, then 10, 4, 20 and 8. So six fives multiplied together give 8. Anyone who knows the clock size, the 5 and the number of fives can work this out in a minute with a pencil. Going the other way is harder. If you are told only that the answer was 8, the general method is to try one count after another until 8 turns up. On a clock with 23 positions that is at most 22 tries. The rest of this explanation is about what changes when the clock is made very large.

Alice and Bob begin by agreeing, out loud, on the clock size 23 and the starting number 5. These are not secret, and Eve hears them. Each of them then picks a private number and tells nobody. Alice picks 6 and Bob picks 15. Alice multiplies six fives together on the clock and gets 8. Bob multiplies fifteen fives together and gets 19. Alice sends 8 to Bob and Bob sends 19 to Alice. Eve hears both.

Alice now takes the 19 she received and multiplies six copies of it together on the clock. The answer is 2. Bob takes the 8 he received and multiplies fifteen copies of it together. His answer is also 2. This follows from how the numbers were built. Bob's 19 is fifteen fives multiplied together, so Alice's sum is six lots of fifteen fives, which is ninety fives. Bob's 8 is six fives, so his sum is fifteen lots of six fives, which is also ninety fives. Multiplication gives the same result in either order, so both of them end with the same number, and neither of them ever sent it.

Eve knows the clock size, the 5, and the two numbers 8 and 19. To get the shared 2 she needs one of the private numbers, 6 or 15. On this small clock she can find one by trying 1, 2, 3 and so on until the fives reach 8, which takes six tries. The example therefore gives no real protection. In real use the clock is much larger. A typical clock today has a size with about 617 digits, which computers call a 2048-bit number, and the private numbers can be hundreds of digits long. The possible private numbers then far outnumber the roughly 10 to the power of 80 atoms estimated to be in the observable universe. Eve cannot try them all, and nobody has published a fast general method for a well-chosen clock.

Alice and Bob are not slowed in the same way. Raising a number to a huge count does not need that many separate multiplications. Squaring a result and then squaring it again doubles the count each time, so a private number of 600 digits needs only about two thousand squarings, plus up to as many further multiplications. A phone does this in a fraction of a second. The gap between a few thousand steps for the honest pair and an impossible search for Eve is what makes the method work.

It is worth separating what is known from what is believed. No one has proved that the backward step must be slow. What exists is a record of mathematicians working on the problem since the 1970s without finding a fast method for these clocks, and most of today's security rests on that record. I think it is a reasonable basis, but it is a judgement, and no theorem backs it. A large quantum computer could in principle undo the sum quickly, using a method published by Peter Shor in 1994. As far as public information shows, no machine of that size has been built, and new schemes are being introduced with that possibility in mind.

What Alice and Bob end with is a shared number. They use it as the key for an ordinary cipher, which scrambles a message using a key that both sides hold and which is fast enough for video or large files. The public-key part only starts the process. When a browser opens a secure web page, the browser and the site carry out an exchange of this kind in a fraction of a second before anything else is sent. Most current software uses a variant built on a different kind of arithmetic, called elliptic curves, with the same structure.

The numbers 8 and 19 are called public keys and 6 and 15 private keys, which is where the name comes from. A related scheme, published in 1977 by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman, lets someone lock a message with a public number so that only the holder of the matching private number can open it. Both ideas had been found earlier in secret at GCHQ in Britain, where James Ellis, Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson worked on them in the early 1970s. Their work was made public in 1997.

One weakness remains. In this account Eve only listens. If she can also change messages, she can sit between Alice and Bob, agree one secret with Alice and another with Bob, and pass their messages along while reading them. To prevent this, Bob's public number is signed by an organisation whose own public number is already stored in Alice's software. Alice must therefore trust something in advance, but it does not have to be Bob. With that check in place, two strangers can agree on a secret in the open.
