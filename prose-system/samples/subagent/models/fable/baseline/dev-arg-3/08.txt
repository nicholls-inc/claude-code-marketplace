Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years someone proposes that joined-up handwriting be quietly retired from the primary classroom. The argument is easy to make. Children will spend their adult lives typing, dictating and tapping. Curriculum time is scarce, and an hour spent on the diagonal join from "o" to "r" is an hour not spent on coding, reading or fractions. Finland dropped its cursive requirement in 2016 in favour of keyboard skills, and the American Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out altogether. To its critics, joined handwriting is the educational equivalent of the fountain pen: charming, and beside the point.

I think this is wrong. Primary schools should go on teaching children to join their letters, and they should teach it properly rather than as a decorative extra. The case does not rest on nostalgia, and it does not require pretending that cursive has magical cognitive properties. It rests on a plainer claim: fluent handwriting is still one of the most useful tools a child can own, and joining is the cheapest reliable route to fluency.

Start with the claim that handwriting itself has been overtaken. It has not. Children in primary school still write by hand for most of the day, and will continue to sit handwritten examinations well into their teens. More importantly, writing by hand appears to do things for young learners that typing does not. Brain imaging work by Karin James and colleagues found that pre-literate children who practised forming letters by hand later showed activity in reading-related brain regions when they saw those letters, while children who typed or traced them did not show the same pattern. EEG studies by Audrey van der Meer's group in Norway have reported richer patterns of brain connectivity during handwriting than during typing. The mechanism is plausible: to form a letter you must produce its shape yourself, stroke by stroke, whereas every key press is the same movement. Building the shape seems to help fix it in memory.

That evidence supports handwriting in general, and honesty requires admitting that it does not by itself settle the question of joins. A child who prints neatly gets the same motor engagement with letter shapes. The research directly comparing joined and unjoined scripts is thinner and more mixed than cursive enthusiasts tend to admit. So the case for joining specifically has to be made on other grounds, and I think there are three.

The first is speed, and through speed, thought. Handwriting researchers such as Virginia Berninger have shown over many years that handwriting fluency in the primary years predicts the quality and length of what children compose. The reason is working memory. A child who has to think about how to form each letter has less attention left for spelling, sentence structure and ideas. Writing only becomes a tool for thinking once the transcription itself is automatic. Printing means lifting the pen after every letter, which interrupts the movement and slows the line. Joining removes most of those lifts. In practice the fastest adult writers tend to use a hybrid, joining some letters and not others, which is exactly what a child taught to join will settle into once the skill is their own. A child never taught to join does not have that choice. Teaching the joins gives pupils the full range and lets efficiency sort out the rest.

The second ground is that joining treats the word as a unit. When letters flow into one another, a child writes "said" as a single practised movement rather than four separate marks. Many teachers of spelling have long held that this motor pattern supports memory for common letter strings, and specialist teachers of dyslexic pupils have traditionally favoured continuous cursive for that reason, along with the fact that joined letters are harder to reverse and the spaces between words become more obvious. The research base here is modest, and I would not stake the whole argument on it. But it points the same way as the fluency evidence, and nothing points the other way: nobody has shown that learning to join harms spelling or reading.

The third ground is access. A person who never learned joined writing often struggles to read it. That shuts them out of a grandparent's letters, a teacher's comments in the margin, a doctor's note, a parish register, a soldier's diary. American universities have begun to report students who cannot read handwritten archival sources, and the historian Drew Gilpin Faust has written about discovering that many of her undergraduates could not read cursive at all. This concern was part of why California reinstated cursive instruction in its elementary schools from 2024, joining a string of other states that have reversed the earlier drift. A society that stops teaching its children to read its own handwriting has made its recent past accessible only to specialists. That is a strange thing to do in order to save a few lessons a week.

Which brings us to cost, the strongest part of the opposing case. Time is finite, and everything in the curriculum has to justify itself. But the cost here is routinely overstated. Joined handwriting is not a subject; it is a skill taught in short, frequent sessions, typically ten or fifteen minutes a few times a week across two or three years, and then practised for free in every other lesson that involves writing. It needs no devices, no licences, no charging trolley and no technician. Few things on the timetable are cheaper. And the choice between handwriting and keyboards is a false one. Children should learn to type, and to type well. Touch typing is itself a motor skill best taught explicitly, and most schools teach it badly or not at all. The sensible position is that children need both, in sequence: fluent handwriting established early, when it supports learning to read and spell, and keyboard fluency built alongside and after it. England's national curriculum takes roughly this line, expecting pupils to begin joining in the lower juniors and to write legibly, fluently and with increasing speed by the end of primary school.

There is also a practical argument about resilience. Much of the enthusiasm for abandoning handwriting dates from a period when it seemed obvious that assessment would move wholly on screen. Since then, the arrival of generative AI has pushed many schools and universities back towards handwritten, supervised work as the one form of assessment they can trust. Whatever one thinks of that trend, it means pupils who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand for an hour are at a real disadvantage, and will be for the foreseeable future. Skills that work without power, connection or software have a habit of remaining useful.

Some objections deserve a direct answer. One is that joined writing is hard for some children, particularly those with motor coordination difficulties, and that insisting on it causes misery. This is true, and it is an argument for flexibility, not abolition. A child with dyspraxia should be allowed to print, or type, without penalty, just as a child with a visual impairment is given adapted materials. We do not drop a skill from the curriculum because a minority need an alternative route.

A second objection is that schools sometimes fetishise the look of the script, awarding "pen licences" and marking down content for imperfect loops. That is a fair criticism of bad practice. The aim is speed and legibility, not calligraphy. A school that spends time policing the exact slope of an ascender has mistaken the means for the end, and a pupil who arrives at a quick, readable, partly joined hand has succeeded even if it would win no prizes.

A third is that the evidence for cursive over print is not conclusive. Again, true. But the burden of proof is being placed on the wrong side. Joined handwriting is an established, low-cost practice with plausible benefits for fluency, some supporting evidence, and clear cultural value. The proposal to remove it rests mostly on a prediction that handwriting will stop mattering, a prediction that has been made confidently for thirty years and has not yet come true. Where the cost of keeping a practice is small and the cost of wrongly abandoning it would fall on a whole cohort of children, caution favours keeping it.

What is at stake is modest but real. A child who leaves primary school able to write quickly and legibly by hand can take notes, plan an essay, sit an exam, annotate a text, and read what earlier generations wrote. Joining the letters is how most children get there. It takes a few minutes a day for a few years and lasts a lifetime. Schools should keep teaching it, teach it for fluency and not for show, make room for the children who need another way, and teach typing as well. That is a better bargain than almost anything proposed to replace it.
