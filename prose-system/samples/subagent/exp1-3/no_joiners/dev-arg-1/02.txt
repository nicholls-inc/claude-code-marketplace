Free the Curb: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will see two cities layered on top of each other. One is the city people live in, with homes, shops, offices and sidewalks. The other is a vast storage system for empty cars. Minimum parking requirements are the rule that built the second city, and it is time to retire them.

These rules tell developers how many spaces they must build with every new project. A restaurant might need one space for every hundred square feet of dining room. An apartment building might need two spaces per unit, whether or not the future tenants own cars. The numbers rarely come from careful study of local demand. Many were copied from neighboring towns or drawn from a handbook of parking counts taken at suburban sites with ample free parking, then treated as law.

Start with the cost. A single parking space in a surface lot can run thousands of dollars to build, and a space in a garage can cost tens of thousands. Underground spaces cost more still. Those costs do not vanish. They land in rents, in menu prices, in the price of a haircut. Someone who never drives pays for parking anyway, folded invisibly into everything they buy. A rule meant to deliver free parking actually delivers parking that everyone pays for, including those who do not use it.

Housing is where the damage is clearest. Cities everywhere complain about unaffordable homes, yet they require builders to set aside land and money for cars before a single person can move in. Parking mandates can kill small projects outright. A lot that might hold eight modest apartments instead holds five, because the rest of the site must be paved. Older buildings tell the story. Charming corner shops, duplexes and courtyard apartments are often illegal to build today, since they could never satisfy modern parking math. Removing the mandate does not ban parking. It simply lets builders respond to what their tenants and customers want.

Critics raise a fair worry. If we stop requiring parking, will streets fill with cars and neighbors fight over spaces? The answer is that developers are not stupid. A landlord who rents apartments to people with cars will build parking, because those tenants will pay for it. A grocery store in a suburban corridor will keep its lot, since shoppers there arrive by car. What changes is that the decision moves from a distant planning office to the person who actually knows the market and carries the risk. Where demand for parking is real, it gets built. Where it is not, land goes to something more useful.

Cities that have tried this offer evidence. Buffalo scrapped its citywide requirements in 2017, and builders there did not stop providing parking. They adjusted it to fit each project. Minneapolis, Anchorage and others have followed, and early results show new housing proposals gaining flexibility without the predicted chaos. Even in places that retained some parking, developers often chose to build less than the old law demanded, which tells us the old numbers were too high all along.

Then there is the question of what a city is for. Every acre given to parking is an acre not producing homes, jobs, tax revenue or public space. Dense, walkable neighborhoods generate far more property tax per acre than car-oriented strips, which means mandates quietly drain municipal budgets. Sprawling lots also push destinations apart, making walking and transit less practical, which then makes driving feel mandatory. The rule creates the very car dependence it claims to serve.

There is a fairness argument too. Households without cars, which skew poorer, younger and older, subsidize the parking habits of everyone else. Requiring a space per bedroom is a regressive tax on people who can least afford it. It also works against climate goals, since cities that encourage driving by design find it hard to persuade anyone to leave the car at home.

None of this means cities should ignore parking altogether. Curb space is valuable and should be managed. Smart pricing of on-street spaces, with revenue returned to the neighborhood, keeps spots available and funds local improvements. Residential permit programs can protect existing streets from spillover while new buildings find their feet. These tools deal with scarcity directly, instead of forcing every builder to guess at it in advance.

The deeper principle is humility. Planners cannot know how many spaces each building will need, and they certainly cannot know it forty years from now, when autonomous vehicles, ride sharing and changing habits may reshape demand again. A rule that fixes a number in law will be wrong somewhere, and the cost of being wrong falls on housing and small business. Letting the market set parking supply, with sensible pricing of public curbs, is more likely to be right and easier to correct.

Cities love to say they want affordable homes, vibrant main streets and lower emissions. Minimum parking requirements work against all three. Repealing them costs the public purse nothing and frees up land for people instead of cars. Any city serious about its stated goals should begin by striking the mandate from its code. Let builders build what their neighbors need. If the demand for parking is as large as the rules assume, it will take care of itself.
