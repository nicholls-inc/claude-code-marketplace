The Great Molasses Flood

January 15, 1919, started out ordinary in Boston's North End. After a hard cold spell the weather had turned mild, and by midday the temperature was near 40 degrees. People in the crowded, mostly Italian immigrant neighborhood were outside running errands. Workers were eating lunch, and children were playing near the waterfront on Commercial Street.

Above them stood a steel tank about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which was waiting to be turned into industrial alcohol, an ingredient used in munitions. The tank had gone up quickly in 1915, during the rush of wartime demand. Nobody had properly tested it, and it had leaked from the start. Neighbors collected the brown drips in cans to take home. The company's answer to the leaks was to paint the tank brown so they would be harder to see.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, witnesses heard a deep rumble, then a rapid popping like machine-gun fire as the rivets sheared away. The tank split open. A wave of molasses, by some accounts 25 feet high, rolled into the streets at roughly 35 miles an hour.

Nothing in its path stood a chance. The wave knocked buildings off their foundations and bent the steel girders of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue. It lifted a firehouse and threw it toward the harbor. Horses, wagons and people were picked up and carried along. As the molasses spread and cooled in the winter air it thickened, and people who had survived the first impact found themselves stuck in it. The more they struggled, the more tightly it held them.

Rescuers arrived almost at once. Among the first were sailors from the training ship USS Nantucket, which was docked nearby. Police, firefighters, Red Cross volunteers and neighbors waded in after them, sometimes up to their waists, pulling out anyone they could reach. A makeshift hospital was set up close by. The search for victims went on for days, and some bodies were not recovered until months later, when they turned up in the harbor.

The flood killed twenty-one people, including two ten-year-old children, and injured about 150. Several of the dead were workers caught at their jobs.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews hosed the streets with salt water from fireboats and scraped up molasses with sand and shovels, and the harbor stayed stained brown for months. Rescuers and onlookers tracked the sticky residue all over the city, onto streetcars, into homes and across sidewalks. For decades afterward, longtime residents said the North End still smelled faintly sweet on hot summer days.

The company first claimed that anarchists had blown up the tank. Survivors and victims' families sued, in one of the first class-action-style cases in Massachusetts. After years of testimony, a court-appointed auditor concluded in 1925 that the tank had simply been too weak to hold its load and that the company was responsible. United States Industrial Alcohol paid out about $628,000 in settlements.

The disaster changed how buildings were made. Massachusetts and other states began requiring that engineers check and sign off on construction plans, a safeguard that is still in place today. A small plaque at the edge of a park on Commercial Street now marks the site, and many people walk past it without knowing what happened there.
