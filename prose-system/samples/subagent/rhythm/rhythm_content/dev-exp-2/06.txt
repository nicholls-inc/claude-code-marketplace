The Earth does not spin at a perfectly steady rate, and a leap second is the small patch that keeps our clocks in step with it. Since 1972 the world has kept time in two ways at once: the atomic clocks that define the second with extraordinary precision, and the planet itself, whose rotation determines when the sun is overhead. Atomic time ticks on without hesitation, while the Earth wobbles, being slowed by tidal friction from the Moon and nudged by shifts in the oceans, the atmosphere and its molten core, and so over the years the two drift apart. Whenever the gap between them threatened to exceed 0.9 of a second, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service announced that an extra second would be inserted at the end of June or December, so that a day ended with 23:59:60 instead of rolling straight to midnight. Twenty-seven of these have been added, the last at the end of 2016, and for most people they passed unnoticed, though they were never harmless for the machines that keep modern life running. Computers generally assume that every minute has exactly sixty seconds, and a minute with sixty-one confuses software that timestamps financial trades, coordinates power grids, routes telephone calls and synchronises data centres, and leap seconds have caused outages and crashes at major internet companies, with engineers inventing a variety of improvised fixes, such as smearing the extra second across a whole day, that do not agree with one another. The trouble also grew with time, since the Earth's rotation has recently been running slightly faster than expected, which raised the unprecedented prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second would be removed, a procedure that almost nobody has tested and that could be far more dangerous to software than an addition. In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body responsible for the definition of the second, voted to abandon leap seconds by 2035, and the decision was driven largely by the people who build and rely on digital systems, who argued that a rare, irregular correction is exactly the sort of event that breaks things precisely because it is so seldom rehearsed. The plan is not to ignore the Earth's rotation indefinitely but to let the difference between atomic and astronomical time grow far larger, perhaps a minute or more, before anyone has to deal with it, which would leave future generations to decide on a gentler method, possibly a correction of a whole minute made once in a century or so. For ordinary readers nothing about daily life will change, because sunrise and sunset will not noticeably shift, and astronomers, who need to know the planet's orientation precisely, will keep their own records of the difference. What will change is that the official time will no longer be a compromise between the heavens and the machines, and will simply follow the atomic clocks.
