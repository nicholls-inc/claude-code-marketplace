Apollo 13 left Kennedy Space Center on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, carrying James Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon, in the highlands of the Fra Mauro formation. Swigert was aboard only because the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been grounded three days before launch after exposure to German measles, and the substitution had gone smoothly enough that the crew's main concern in the first two days was a minor one: the center engine of the Saturn V's second stage had shut down early, its vibrations having grown violent, and the remaining engines had burned longer to make up the speed. Lovell, who was making his fourth flight and had already circled the Moon on Apollo 8, regarded the ship as healthy, and for fifty-five hours it behaved that way, with the command module, named Odyssey, and the lunar module, named Aquarius, riding together some two hundred thousand miles from home while the crew finished a television broadcast that the networks chose not to carry.

The flaw that ended the mission had been built into the spacecraft years earlier and was invisible to everyone. Oxygen tank number two, which sat in the service module and supplied both breathable oxygen and the reactants for the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity, had been dropped about two inches during removal from its shelf at the factory in 1968, probably damaging the internal fill tube. During a countdown rehearsal at the Cape a few weeks before launch, technicians found that they could not empty the tank normally and resorted to running its heaters for hours to boil the oxygen off, powering them from the ground supply at sixty-five volts, even though the thermostatic switches protecting the heaters had been designed years before for the spacecraft's twenty-eight-volt system. The switches welded shut under the higher voltage, the heaters ran unchecked, and the Teflon insulation on the wires inside the tank was scorched without anyone noticing, so that the tank went to the Moon with bare conductors immersed in liquid oxygen.

At 9:08 in the evening Houston time on April 13, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the tank's contents, a routine request meant to keep the supercold oxygen from stratifying and fooling the gauges. A few moments after Swigert flipped the switch there was a bang, felt through the structure of the spacecraft, and the first words that reached the ground were Swigert's, that they had had a problem, which Lovell repeated with the added information that the main B bus was undervoltage. A spark from the damaged wiring had ignited the Teflon, pressure inside the tank had climbed until it burst, and the blast had blown the side panel off the service module and ruptured the plumbing of the neighboring tank, which began to leak into space. Out the window Lovell saw gas streaming away from the ship, and he reported it, and over the following minutes the fuel cells died one after another and the oxygen pressure fell toward nothing, which meant that the command module was losing both its air and its power source with the Earth a little under three days away.

Gene Kranz and his white team of flight controllers spent the first hour trying to save the command module by closing valves and isolating the fuel cells, but the readings kept sinking, and once it was clear that Odyssey could not be revived the decision was made to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had been designed to keep two men alive for about two days on the lunar surface and now had to sustain three for four, so its systems were pared to the minimum, with Swigert and Haise powering up the module while Lovell, the only one who had the training for the job, aligned its guidance platform from the data in the command module's computer before that computer was shut down to save the batteries. The route home posed its own difficulty, because the spacecraft had earlier been moved off the free-return path that would have swung it around the Moon and back to Earth without any engine burn, in order to reach Fra Mauro, and ground teams worked out that a burn of the lunar module's descent engine, an engine never designed to push a docked stack of spacecraft, could put the ship back on a return trajectory. They did it about five hours after the explosion, and after rounding the far side, with Apollo 13 farther from Earth than any humans had ever been, at roughly 248,000 miles, they fired the engine again for more than four minutes to shorten the trip and aim for a landing in the Pacific.

The three days that followed were a long endurance test. To save electricity they switched off nearly everything, including the cabin heaters, and the temperature inside dropped to around forty degrees Fahrenheit, with moisture condensing on the walls and the crew sleeping badly in their lightweight garments, while water was rationed to a few ounces per person per day because the lunar module's supply was needed to cool its electronics, and Haise developed a fever from a kidney infection that he told almost no one about. The most pressing hazard turned out to be carbon dioxide, which was building up in the lunar module because its lithium hydroxide canisters, built for two men, were filling up, and the spare canisters from the command module were square while the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers in Houston gathered the materials that were also aboard, which were cardboard from a flight plan cover, plastic bags, a suit hose and duct tape, built a working adapter on a table, and read the instructions up to the crew, who built a copy that came to be called the mailbox and watched the carbon dioxide level fall back into the safe range.

Course corrections had to be flown by hand, since the guidance platform was off to conserve power. At one point the crew used the sunlit edge of the Earth as a reference to hold their attitude for a burn, and in the meantime Mattingly, who had stayed on the ground, worked in a simulator with other engineers to devise a procedure for restarting the command module from a cold, dead state using less power than the ship's batteries could supply, in a sequence nobody had ever planned for, since the batteries had to carry the capsule through reentry. About four hours before arrival, the crew separated the damaged service module and, as it drifted away, saw for the first time that an entire panel was gone and that equipment hung loose in the exposed bay, a sight that explained why the fuel cells had failed and also raised the question of whether the heat shield on the command module had been damaged, something that nobody could determine. Aquarius was released last, after its final job of carrying the crew home, and Odyssey, awake again and warming up, plunged into the atmosphere with the communications blackout stretching about a minute longer than predicted, a minute in which the controllers waited in silence for any word from a spacecraft that might no longer exist.

The signal came back, and on April 17, 1970, three parachutes slowed the capsule over the South Pacific near Samoa, and the crew was lifted aboard the USS Iwo Jima after almost six days in space. The review board chaired by Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank's history and to the thermostats, and the redesign that followed removed the heater switches that had failed, added a third oxygen tank and a spare battery, and made other changes to the command module's electrical system, and Apollo flights resumed the following year. Lovell never walked on the Moon, and the mission did not reach it, but it is remembered less as a failure than as an example of what a team of people can do when they have been trained to treat the unforeseen as a problem to be solved, one calculation and one piece of improvised hardware at a time.
