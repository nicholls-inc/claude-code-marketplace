Apollo 13 left Cape Kennedy on the afternoon of April 11, 1970, carrying James Lovell, John Swigert and Fred Haise toward a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands of the Moon, on a mission that was meant to be the third lunar landing and that, by the spring of 1970, had begun to feel to the public like routine business. Lovell was the most experienced astronaut alive, a veteran of Gemini 7, Gemini 12 and Apollo 8, Haise was a rookie preparing for his first flight, and Swigert had joined the crew only days earlier, after flight surgeons grounded the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles and had no immunity. The launch was nearly flawless apart from the center engine of the second stage, which shut down about two minutes early because of violent vibration while the remaining engines burned longer to make up the difference, and over the following two days the crew docked their command and service module, which they had named Odyssey, with the lunar module Aquarius, drew the lander out of its housing atop the spent third stage, and settled into the long coast toward the Moon, with a television broadcast planned for the evening of April 13 that none of the three major networks chose to carry.

The cause of what followed had been set in place years earlier, in the factory and on the launch pad. An oxygen tank bound for Apollo 13's service module had been dropped about two inches while being removed from an earlier spacecraft in 1965, probably damaging an internal fill tube, and in March 1970, during a countdown rehearsal, technicians found that the tank would not empty normally and boiled off its oxygen with an internal heater that had been designed for the spacecraft's 28-volt supply but was connected to the 65-volt supply at the launch complex, which welded shut the thermostatic switches meant to cut the heater off and scorched the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside. Nobody understood that this had happened, and so the tank flew with bare wires sitting in an atmosphere of supercooled pure oxygen.

At about fifty-five hours and fifty-five minutes into the flight, with the ship roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, Houston asked Swigert to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that agitates their contents so the gauges read correctly, and when he threw the switch a spark from the damaged wiring ignited the insulation, pressure in tank number two climbed until the tank failed, and the blast tore a panel off the side of the service module and broke the plumbing of the neighboring tank. The crew felt a bang and a shudder, Swigert reported that they had had a problem, and Lovell, a few moments later, repeated it in the words the world remembers, though what he actually said was that Houston had a problem, a small difference from what the films later put in his mouth. Over the next minutes the instruments told a story the men did not want to read, with voltage sagging, pressure in one oxygen tank at zero and the other falling, two of the three fuel cells starving, and, in Lovell's window, a stream of gas venting steadily into space, which he understood to mean that the spacecraft was losing the oxygen that supplied both its breathable air and its electrical power.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his team, joined soon by controllers from every shift, concluded within a couple of hours that Odyssey was dying and that the Moon was out of reach, and the only vehicle that could keep the men alive was the lunar module, a spidery craft built to support two men for about two days and now asked to sustain three for four. Lovell and Haise powered Aquarius up while Swigert shut Odyssey down to save the small reserve of battery power it would need for reentry, and the crew moved into the lander, where they would spend most of the remaining flight. Because the ship had been steered off the free-return path that would loop it around the Moon and back to Earth in order to reach the landing site, the first job was to get back onto that path, which the crew did with a thirty-second burn of the descent engine about six hours after the explosion, and once they had rounded the far side and passed the greatest distance from Earth that humans have ever traveled, nearly 249,000 miles, they fired the same engine for four minutes to speed the return and steer it toward the Pacific recovery area.

Survival, meanwhile, became a matter of consumables. Aquarius had to be powered down almost completely, with its guidance computer and most of its lights and equipment switched off to stretch the batteries and the water that cooled the electronics, so cabin temperatures fell toward freezing, moisture condensed on the walls, and the men shivered in their flight suits while rationing themselves to a few ounces of water a day and very little food, and Haise developed a fever from a kidney infection that the public did not learn about until afterward. The most dangerous problem was carbon dioxide, because the lithium hydroxide canisters that scrubbed the lander's air were sized for two men for two days and were filling up, while the spare canisters in the command module were square and the lander's receptacles took round ones, so that by the evening of April 15 the gauge was climbing toward levels that would bring confusion and then death. Engineers on the ground assembled, from a plastic bag, cardboard from a flight manual cover, a suit hose and duct tape, all items known to be aboard, a contraption that forced cabin air through the square canisters, read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and watched the carbon dioxide level fall once the men had built it.

Navigation presented a quieter kind of risk. With the guidance platform off and the ship's attitude disturbed by venting, the crew had to align the spacecraft by hand, and for a course correction about a day and a half before reentry Lovell held the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, in a fixed place in his window to point the ship and timed the burn himself. On the ground, Mattingly, whose grounding had seemed a misfortune, worked in a simulator with engineers to write a procedure for bringing Odyssey back to life on less power than anyone had thought the command module could survive on, and that procedure was read up to the crew, who followed it step by step in the cold during the last hours.

On April 17 the crew separated the service module, and as it drifted away they saw the damage for the first time, a whole panel gone and the equipment bay exposed, scorched and trailing debris, which Lovell described to Houston and which became a key piece of evidence for the investigators. They moved into Odyssey and released Aquarius, which had served as their lifeboat and now burned up over the Pacific. At reentry the radio blackout ran longer than the expected three minutes, and in the control room, where the attention of much of the world had by then been captured, the silence stretched past the point at which contact should have returned, until Swigert's voice came through and the parachutes appeared on television. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa shortly after noon, central time, on April 17, within sight of the recovery ship Iwo Jima and almost six days after launch, and the three men, thinner, dehydrated and exhausted, were helped out of the capsule and flown to the carrier.

The review board chaired by Edgar Cortright traced the accident to the tank's history, to the drop, the faulty heater circuit and the failure of the tests to reveal either, rather than to any error made in flight, and NASA redesigned the tanks to remove the damaged-wiring hazard, added a third oxygen tank and a battery to the service module, and changed its contingency planning before the next mission flew. Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, and what it left behind was less a record of exploration than a demonstration of what a crew and a ground team could do with a crippled machine, an improvised lifeboat and a hard deadline, to bring three men home.
