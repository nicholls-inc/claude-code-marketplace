The Long Way Home

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third lunar landing. By then the public had grown used to the idea, and the networks had not even bothered to broadcast the crew's television shows from space. That would change within three days.

The commander was Jim Lovell, a veteran of two earlier flights and the Gemini 7 endurance mission, who was making his fourth trip into space. Fred Haise, a rookie, was to pilot the lunar module. The third seat had been meant for Ken Mattingly, but a few days before launch doctors learned he had been exposed to German measles and might fall ill in flight. Jack Swigert, the backup, took his place on short notice. The two linked spacecraft carried names. The command module was Odyssey, and the lunar module, built to land on the Moon, was Aquarius.

The first two days were routine. The crew settled into the long coast outward, swapped jokes with Houston, and made a small course change that put them on a path to land in the Fra Mauro highlands. On the evening of April 13, roughly fifty-five hours into the flight, Mission Control asked them to stir the tanks of supercooled oxygen and hydrogen that fed the ship's fuel cells. The tanks tended to form layers, which confused the gauges, and a routine stir cleared that up.

Swigert threw the switch. A little over a minute later the crew heard a bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights flared. Swigert reported the trouble first, and Lovell repeated it, the phrasing that would become famous. "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause lay in a tank that had been damaged on the ground. Before launch, a heater in oxygen tank number two had run far too hot during a test and had stripped insulation from the wiring. When Swigert switched on the stirring fans, a spark ran along the bare wire, and the oxygen around it burned. The pressure rose until the tank burst, blowing off a panel of the service module and crippling the neighboring tank as well.

At first nobody on the ground or in space understood how bad it was. Pressure in one tank was falling, then both. Electrical power was draining away. Within about two hours the fuel cells would be dead, and with them the command module's power, water and oxygen. Lovell looked out of a window and saw gas streaming into space. The ship was bleeding its life away.

The decision was made to treat the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own batteries, its own oxygen and water, and an engine. It had been built to support two men for about two days on the surface. Now it would have to sustain three men for four. The crew floated into it, powered down Odyssey to save what little battery life it had, and began the work of survival.

Another problem was the path of the ship itself. Apollo 13 was on a trajectory that would carry it around the Moon and, if nothing was done, back to Earth, but this path had been altered earlier to allow for the landing. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams in Houston judged that the safest course was to loop around the Moon rather than turn back. After rounding the far side, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine, the one meant to lower them gently to the surface, to speed the trip home. At their greatest distance they were nearly two hundred fifty thousand miles from Earth, farther than any humans had ever travelled, and they have not gone farther since. Aboard the ship, the mood was grim. Nobody had much interest in the view of the Moon.

The next days were a long test of endurance. With the electrical systems shut down, the cabin grew cold, close to freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The crew slept little. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, which left Haise, who developed a fever and a painful infection, particularly miserable. Lovell later said the cold and thirst were the worst parts of the ordeal.

The most dangerous problem was the air. Carbon dioxide from the men's breathing was building up in the lunar module, and the filters meant to remove it were running out. Odyssey had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square and the receptacles in Aquarius were round. Engineers in Houston gathered the same materials the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a suit hose and a lot of duct tape, and talked the astronauts through building an adapter. It worked. The carbon dioxide reading fell, and the men could breathe easily again.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the man who had been left behind, spent long hours in a simulator working out how to bring Odyssey back to life using as little power as possible. The command module would be needed for reentry, and it had never been started up from a cold, nearly dead state in space. He and the engineers wrote a new checklist, tested it again and again, and read it up to the crew.

Two more course corrections were needed. One of them had to be done by hand, because the ship's guidance systems were off to conserve power. Lovell lined up the spacecraft by looking at the line between day and night on Earth and held it steady while the engine burned for a few seconds. It was a feat of old-fashioned piloting, and it kept them in the narrow corridor through which a returning spacecraft must pass.

On April 17 the crew prepared for the end. They separated the service module, and as it drifted away they got their first good look at the damage. An entire side panel was gone, and the equipment behind it was shredded. Lovell reported that the damage was far worse than anyone had imagined. Then they moved back into Odyssey, which was cold and wet but alive, and released Aquarius, which had saved them. It would burn up in the atmosphere over the Pacific.

Reentry meant a communications blackout, usually about three minutes, as a sheath of hot gas surrounds the capsule. The minutes ticked by in Mission Control, and the silence went on longer than expected. Nobody knew whether the heat shield had been damaged in the explosion. Then, after about a minute more than planned, Swigert's voice came through. Parachutes bloomed in the sky above the South Pacific, and Odyssey settled into the water near Samoa, a few miles from the recovery ship, USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted almost six days.

The crew was tired, thin, and in Haise's case sick, but all three were alive. Millions of people around the world had followed the story, and it had held the attention of a public that had grown bored with the Moon. President Nixon honored the ground team with the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

The investigation that followed traced the accident to the tank design and its testing, and the tanks were rebuilt before the next flight. Apollo went on to land on the Moon four more times. Lovell never walked on its surface. But he and his crew had shown how well people and machines could work together when everything went wrong, and the flight was later called a successful failure. Its legacy was not the landing that never happened but the return that did.
