Let Cities Stop Dictating Parking

Walk through almost any American city and you will see a quiet monument to bad planning: the half-empty parking lot. It sits in front of the pharmacy, behind the apartment block, beside the church. It exists not because a business owner decided it was wise, but because a rule said it must. Minimum parking requirements, which force developers to build a fixed number of spaces per apartment, per square foot of retail, or per restaurant seat, are among the most consequential and least examined regulations in urban life. Cities should scrap them.

Start with where the numbers come from. Many of the ratios in zoning codes trace back to guidance based on a handful of suburban sites, often measured at peak demand, often free of any price signal. Planners then copied them from one town to the next. The result is a standard with the authority of law and the evidentiary foundation of a guess. When researchers have surveyed buildings built under these rules, they routinely find that lots sit largely empty even at the busiest hours. We have legislated an oversupply.

That oversupply is not free. Structured parking can cost tens of thousands of dollars per space to build, and underground spaces cost more. Those costs do not vanish. They are passed on in higher rents and higher prices, whether or not a tenant owns a car. A household that gets by without a vehicle still pays for the space it never uses. Requirements therefore act as a hidden tax that falls hardest on people with the least, including the many low-income renters who own fewer cars than the average.

The damage to housing is more direct still. A parking mandate competes with homes for the same scarce resource: land and money. On a small lot, the required spaces can eat the space a developer would have used for additional units, or make the project impossible to finance at all. Cities that say they want more affordable housing while demanding two spaces per unit are working against themselves. Every mandated stall is a bit of floor area, or a bit of affordability, that did not get built.

Then there is the effect on the shape of the city. Parking requirements spread buildings apart. Shops sit behind seas of asphalt, which makes walking unpleasant and distances longer, which in turn makes driving feel necessary. The mandate creates the demand it claims to serve. Older neighborhoods, built before these rules, are often the ones people love most: dense, walkable, full of small businesses squeezed onto narrow lots. Many of those buildings could not legally be built today. That is a remarkable indictment of the rulebook.

Critics raise a fair worry. If cities drop requirements, will streets fill with spillover cars and neighbors fight over curb space? It is a real concern, but it is an argument for managing the curb, not for mandating off-street supply. Cities can price street parking to keep a few spaces open on every block, issue residential permits where needed, and return the revenue to the neighborhood. Pricing does what a quota cannot: it adjusts to actual demand.

A second objection holds that developers will build too little and customers will suffer. This misunderstands what removal means. Repealing a minimum is not banning parking. Developers who think tenants want spaces will still build them, because a building that cannot attract tenants or shoppers loses money. Lenders often insist on parking too. What changes is that the decision moves from a rule written decades ago to the person with money at stake and local knowledge. A grocery store near a freeway will build a big lot. A apartment building beside a rail station may build a small one. Both outcomes are more sensible than one formula for everyone.

The evidence from places that have tried this is encouraging. Cities that have eliminated or sharply reduced their requirements, including Buffalo, Minneapolis, and a growing list of others, have not descended into gridlock. Instead they have seen builders adapt: more projects pencil out, more small commercial spaces reopen in old buildings, and parking gets provided where the market wants it. Reform has also proven politically durable once the sky fails to fall.

There is a climate and fiscal case as well. Surface lots generate runoff and heat, and they produce little tax revenue per acre compared with buildings. A downtown block of asphalt contributes far less to a city budget than the same block with shops and homes on it. Cities strapped for money are subsidizing low-value land use by law.

None of this means cars are the enemy or that everyone should give theirs up. Many people, especially in places with limited transit, depend on them, and good policy should respect that. The point is narrower: a city should not force every building to answer the question of how much parking it needs in the same way. That is a decision best left to owners, tenants, and prices.

A rule that has no basis in evidence, raises housing costs, and degrades the places it touches deserves to be repealed. Cities that have spent decades insisting on more parking than anyone wants should stop. Let builders build what the market asks for, let streets be priced sensibly, and let neighborhoods become the places that old zoning rules often made illegal.
