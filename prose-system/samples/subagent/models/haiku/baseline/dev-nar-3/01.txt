On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unusually mild. Temperatures had climbed into the low forties, and around 12:30 p.m. people were working, walking, and eating lunch along Commercial Street, close to the waterfront.

A short distance away stood a steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It rose about fifty feet and held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, a raw ingredient the company turned into industrial alcohol for manufacturers supplying the war effort. The tank had been built quickly and never properly tested. For months, neighbors had complained that it groaned and popped, and it leaked a sticky brown residue. Workers had even painted it to hide the seepage. Engineers had voiced doubts about its soundness, but the company kept filling it, and nobody stopped it.

Then it failed. Witnesses later described a sharp crack like artillery fire, followed by a roar. The tank's seams split, and a wave of molasses, by some estimates fifteen to twenty-five feet high, burst into the streets at perhaps thirty-five miles per hour. It tore apart the tank's supports, knocked buildings off their foundations, and twisted a section of the elevated railway above Commercial Street. Horses pulling delivery wagons were swept away or trapped. Men and boys were pinned in the thick, cold syrup, which clung to everything and made it hard to move, see, or breathe.

When the flood finally spent itself, twenty-one people were dead and about 150 were injured. Some had drowned in the molasses; others had been crushed by debris. The waterfront was coated in a brown crust, and the harbor turned the color of tea for days. Cleanup crews used sand, seawater, and fire hoses, and the work dragged on for weeks. Residents said the sweet, fermenting smell lingered in the neighborhood for decades, especially on hot summer days.

The company first blamed sabotage, a claim investigators never substantiated. The real cause, most engineers concluded, was poor design and construction combined with the strain of overfilling and temperature changes. Lawsuits followed for years. In 1925, after a lengthy trial, a judge found the company liable. The payouts were far smaller than the victims had sought, but the case became a landmark in establishing that a corporation could be held responsible for a disaster of its own making.

Today, the event is remembered as one of the strangest disasters in American history: a tragedy caused not by fire or storm but by a sticky, slow-moving liquid that behaved like a flood. A small plaque and the stories of longtime residents keep the memory alive.
