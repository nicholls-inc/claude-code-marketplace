Let Cities Stop Mandating Parking

Every city that requires a developer to build a fixed number of parking spaces with each new apartment, shop or restaurant is running an experiment on itself. The results are in, and they are not good. Cities should repeal minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon.

Start with how these rules came to exist. Most were adopted in the middle of the twentieth century, often copied from a handbook or a neighboring town, with little evidence behind the numbers. Planners estimated peak demand at a suburban shopping center on the busiest day of the year, then wrote that figure into law for everyone. A restaurant must supply one space for every hundred square feet of dining room. An apartment must supply two spaces per unit, whether or not the tenants own cars. The rules were guesses, and they hardened into code.

The first problem is that they raise the cost of everything. A surface space costs thousands of dollars to build and takes up the land that could hold a home or a business. A structured or underground space can cost tens of thousands. Developers do not absorb that bill. It lands in the rent, the sale price, and the menu. A household that owns no car still pays for the parking attached to its apartment, because the unit cannot be built or leased without it. A mandate that forces everyone to buy a product only some of them use is a hidden tax, and it falls hardest on people with the least money.

The second problem is that the rules strangle housing. On a small lot, the parking requirement can decide whether a project is possible at all. Fit the required spaces and the building shrinks by several units, or the numbers fail and nothing gets built. Many cities say they want more homes near jobs and transit while writing codes that forbid exactly those homes. Old neighborhoods that people love, with narrow lots and corner stores and walk-up apartments, would be illegal to build today because they lack the parking that current law demands.

The third problem is the land itself. Walk through a typical commercial strip and count the acres of empty asphalt on an ordinary afternoon. That pavement pays almost no property tax compared with a building on the same ground. It spreads destinations apart so that driving becomes the only sensible choice, which then seems to justify more parking. Cities end up subsidizing car storage at the expense of places for people to live and work, and then wonder why their downtowns feel hollow.

Defenders raise real objections, and they deserve answers. The most common is that removing requirements will flood the streets with parked cars and make life miserable for existing residents. But repeal does not ban parking. It removes the mandate and lets builders decide. Developers and lenders are not eager to build housing that nobody can reach, so projects aimed at car owners will keep providing spaces. The difference is that the amount will respond to actual demand rather than a formula. Cities that have tried this, from Buffalo to Minneapolis to a growing list of others, have seen parking continue to be built where it is wanted and new housing appear where it was previously blocked.

Another objection is that spillover onto public streets will cause conflict. That is a fair concern, and the answer is to manage the curb rather than to force private owners to solve a public problem. Residential permit districts, metered street parking, and pricing that tracks demand turn a free-for-all into something orderly. Revenue from the curb can even be returned to the neighborhood for sidewalks and trees. Pricing the scarce resource works better than hiding its cost inside every building.

A third objection is that people need cars, especially in places with thin transit. That is true, and nothing about repeal changes it. Plenty of residents will keep driving, and plenty of buildings will keep parking lots. The argument is not that cars are bad. It is that the government should not decide, in advance and across the board, how much parking every business and household must pay for. Some customers want more spaces and will choose the shop that offers them. Some tenants want cheaper rent and will choose the building that skips the garage. Both choices ought to be legal.

There is also a matter of honesty. Cities already grant exceptions to these rules constantly, through variances and special hearings that cost developers time and money and reward those with the best lawyers. A rule that must be waived so often is a rule that does not work. Better to write the sensible default into law and spare everyone the ritual.

Repeal is a modest reform with large effects. It lowers the cost of building, frees land for productive use, gives neighborhoods room to evolve, and treats residents as adults who can weigh their own needs. The old formulas were never grounded in evidence, and the cost of keeping them grows each year. Cities should strike minimum parking requirements from their codes, manage the curb with care, and let people build what people actually want.
