A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. Atomic clocks count seconds with extraordinary steadiness, based on the vibrations of atoms, and they would lose no more than a fraction of a second over many millions of years, whereas the Earth is a less reliable timekeeper, its rotation wobbling and, over the long run, tending to slow, nudged by the tides, by movements of molten rock deep inside the planet, and by shifts in ice and oceans. A day measured by the sun is therefore not exactly the same length as a day measured by atomic clocks.

Left alone, the two would drift apart, and noon on the clock would slowly stop matching the moment the sun is highest in the sky. To prevent that, international timekeepers introduced leap seconds in 1972: when the gap between atomic time and Earth time grew close to a second, an extra second was inserted at the end of June or December, so that one minute lasted 61 seconds. Twenty-seven have been added since, the most recent at the end of 2016.

The system worked for astronomers and navigators, but it has become a headache for the digital world, where computers, financial exchanges, telecommunications networks and satellite systems depend on precisely synchronised clocks and most are built on the assumption that every minute has 60 seconds. An extra second, arriving unpredictably, has caused software to crash, servers to freeze and records to be stamped out of order, and large technology companies have resorted to spreading it thinly over many hours, a workaround that is itself inconsistent from firm to firm. Engineers also dread the opposite case: because the Earth has recently been spinning slightly faster, a first-ever negative leap second, in which a second is removed, may eventually be needed, and almost no software has been tested for that.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of the second and timekeeping standards, voted to stop using leap seconds by 2035. Rather than abandon the idea entirely, it agreed to let the gap between atomic time and Earth time grow much larger than one second before anyone intervenes, probably not for a century or more, so that future generations can decide how to handle it, perhaps with a larger, rarer correction. For everyday life, nothing will visibly change: the sun will keep rising when it always has, and our clocks will simply stop lurching.
