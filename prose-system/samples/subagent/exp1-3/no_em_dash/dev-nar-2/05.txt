**Apollo 13: The Flight That Came Home**

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 13 away from Florida. Aboard were three men: Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights, Fred Haise, a rookie, and Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days before. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was pulled from the flight. Swigert stepped in. The goal was to land in the Fra Mauro highlands of the Moon. By then, many Americans had begun to treat lunar flights as routine, and the networks did not bother to carry the crew's television broadcasts live.

For two days, the mission went smoothly. The crew had named their command ship Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. On the evening of April 13, roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control in Houston asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module. The stir was routine, meant to keep the supercold contents evenly mixed. Swigert flipped the switch.

Moments later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Warning lights flickered and the voltage dipped. Swigert reported, "Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, and the phrase later entered the language in a slightly altered form. Over the next minutes it became clear that something was draining the ship of power and oxygen. Looking out a window, Lovell saw gas streaming into space. An oxygen tank had ruptured, and a second tank was failing. Without the oxygen, the fuel cells that made electricity and drinking water for Odyssey would die.

The cause lay in years of small mistakes. The tank's design had been changed to run on higher voltage, but a heater switch was never upgraded to match. During ground testing, the heaters were left on far too long and cooked off the wiring insulation. The bare wires waited for the stir. When it came, a spark ignited the oxygen and the tank burst.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his teams, working in shifts, had to reverse the mission's whole purpose. The Moon landing was gone. The question now was survival. The crew would use Aquarius as a lifeboat. The lander was built for two men for two days on the Moon. Now it had to sustain three men for four days. Odyssey had to be shut down almost completely, saving its small reserve of battery power for the final return to Earth.

The ship's path was another problem. Apollo 13 had been moved off the "free return" trajectory that would have looped it around the Moon and back to Earth without needing any engine. Controllers had to decide how to get the crew home quickly. They chose to swing around the far side of the Moon, firing Aquarius's descent engine, designed for landing, to speed up the trip and aim for the Pacific. The crew passed behind the Moon and saw its far side below them, nearly 200 miles up. Lovell, who had been looking forward to walking on its surface, would never do so. The engine burn after they rounded the Moon shaved hours off the journey.

Then came the quieter struggles. Power in Aquarius was rationed to a trickle. Water was limited to a few ounces per person each day, and the crew became dehydrated. Haise developed a fever and a kidney infection. The cabin grew cold, falling to near freezing, and the crew shivered in their flight suits while condensation dripped from the walls. They slept little.

The most famous problem was carbon dioxide. Humans exhale it, and in a sealed cabin it builds up until it makes people confused and then unconscious. Aquarius had canisters of lithium hydroxide to absorb the gas, but they were meant for two men over a short stay. Odyssey had plenty of canisters, but they were square, and the lander's system took round ones. As the warning gauge climbed, engineers in Houston gathered the same items the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard from the flight plan, suit hoses and duct tape. They built an adapter on the ground and read the instructions up to the crew, who copied the contraption from the description. It worked. The gauge dropped back to safe levels.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the man who had been grounded, worked in a simulator on the ground. His job was to devise a way to restart Odyssey from a completely cold state, using only the tiny amount of power left in its batteries. The usual procedure took hours and consumed far more energy than the ship had. Mattingly and the engineers had to write a new one. They tested each step in the simulator and sent it up to the crew.

There were navigation challenges too. Lovell had to line up the spacecraft for engine burns without the normal computer guidance, which was off to save power. At one point he steered by keeping the Earth's edge, the line between day and night, in the window. A burn to correct their course had to be exact. A shallow reentry would bounce them back into space, and a steep one would burn them up.

On April 17, the final day, the crew powered up Odyssey and cast off the damaged service module. As it drifted away, they saw the extent of the harm. An entire panel had been blown off, leaving the side of the ship torn open and trailing wires. Photographs of it were taken. Then they said goodbye to Aquarius, the lander that had saved them, and released it to burn up in the atmosphere. Lovell later noted that it had performed a duty beyond its design.

Reentry brought another long wait. As the capsule plunged into the atmosphere, a hot sheath of plasma blocked radio contact, as it always does. The silence was expected to last about three minutes. It stretched past that. In Houston, controllers watched a quiet radio and wondered whether the heat shield, which no one had been able to inspect, had survived. Then Swigert's voice came through. Parachutes bloomed over the Pacific, and television viewers watched Odyssey drop toward the sea.

Apollo 13 splashed down near Samoa on April 17, 1970, just under six days after launch. The crew was thin, tired and cold, but alive. The recovery ship USS Iwo Jima picked them up.

An investigation led by Edgar Cortright traced the failure through the tank's history, and NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks and added a spare battery and extra oxygen storage to the service module for later flights. The Moon landings resumed with Apollo 14 the following year.

Apollo 13 is often called a successful failure. It never reached the Moon, and its one great achievement was to return. The crew did not accomplish this alone. Thousands of people, on the ground and in the spacecraft, solved a series of problems that no one had rehearsed, one at a time, under pressure. The mission showed that careful engineering is no guarantee against disaster, but that disciplined improvisation can still turn a disaster into a homecoming.
