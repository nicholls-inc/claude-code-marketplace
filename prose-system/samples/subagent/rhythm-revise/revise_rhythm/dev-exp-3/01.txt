Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and who can only talk by shouting across a crowded room where everyone can hear every word, yet who by the end of the conversation both hold the same secret number that nobody else in the room has. That sounds impossible, because anything that passes between them is overheard. The trick, which was worked out in the 1970s and now protects almost everything you do online, is that some tasks in mathematics are easy to do in one direction and absurdly hard to undo, and public-key cryptography is a way of building a shared secret out of exactly that imbalance.

Start with an everyday picture. Suppose you mix a splash of yellow paint with a particular shade of blue to get green. Mixing is easy, but if someone hands you the green and asks which exact yellow and blue went into it, you are stuck, because you cannot unmix paint. Now suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say a pot of yellow, which everyone in the room may see. Alice secretly picks her own private colour and mixes it into the yellow, Bob does the same with a different private colour, and they then swap the mixed pots across the room. Anyone watching sees both mixtures but can't separate out the private colours. Next, Alice takes Bob's mixed pot and adds her private colour to it, while Bob takes Alice's pot and adds his, and both end up with the same final shade, because the order of mixing doesn't matter: yellow plus Alice's colour plus Bob's colour is the same blend either way. The eavesdropper holds only the two half-mixed pots and the starting yellow, and cannot reach the final shade without unmixing something.

The real method replaces paint with arithmetic, and the arithmetic needs nothing beyond school maths, though it uses a slightly unusual kind of counting. Think of a clock: if it is ten o'clock and you add five hours, you get three o'clock, not fifteen, because the numbers wrap around after twelve. Mathematicians call this remainder arithmetic, in which you do an ordinary calculation, divide by the clock size, and keep only the remainder. Instead of a twelve-hour clock, imagine one with 23 positions, numbered from 0 to 22; fifteen thousand six hundred twenty-five is a big number, but on this clock it lands on 8, because dividing it by 23 leaves a remainder of 8.

Here is the process with those small numbers. Alice and Bob publicly agree on two things, the clock size, 23, and a base number, 5, neither of which is secret, and the whole room can write them down. Alice now chooses a private number, say 6, and keeps it to herself; she calculates 5 multiplied by itself six times, which is 15,625, and reduces it on the 23-hour clock to get 8, and that 8 is her public announcement. Bob meanwhile chooses his own private number, 15, works out 5 multiplied by itself fifteen times, reduced on the same clock, which comes to 19, and announces 19 to the room.

Now the swap pays off. Alice takes Bob's announcement, 19, and multiplies it by itself six times, which is her private number, reducing on the clock as she goes, and the answer is 2. Bob takes Alice's announcement, 8, and multiplies it by itself fifteen times, his private number, again reducing on the clock, and his answer is also 2, so they have independently arrived at the same number without ever saying it aloud. The reason it works is the same as with the paint: Alice's route amounts to 5 multiplied by itself six times fifteen times over, and Bob's amounts to 5 multiplied by itself fifteen times six times over, and since six fifteens and fifteen sixes are the same total, the two results must match, so the order of the work does not change the outcome.

Now consider the eavesdropper, whom we might call Eve, who knows the clock size 23, the base 5, and the two announcements, 8 and 19, but lacks either private number. To find Alice's, she would have to ask how many times she must multiply 5 by itself, on this clock, to land on 8. With a clock this small she could simply try every possibility one after another and find the answer in a moment, which is why the example is only a toy, since the security rests entirely on size. Real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits, and private numbers just as large. Going forward is still quick, because there are clever shortcuts, such as repeated squaring, that let a computer multiply a number by itself an astronomically large number of times in only a few hundred steps. Going backward has no known shortcut: because the remainders jump around unpredictably, knowing that a result landed on 8 tells you almost nothing about how many multiplications it took, so Eve would have to try possibilities one at a time, and with numbers that long the count of possibilities exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe, and all the computers on Earth working together for the age of the universe would not get through them.

That is the sense in which the secret is safe: it is not hidden by concealment, since every step of the method is published and the whole scheme is public knowledge, but by difficulty. This is also where the name comes from, because each person has a public part, the announcement they shout across the room, and a private part, the number they never reveal, and the two are linked by a calculation that is easy to perform and practically impossible to reverse.

Once Alice and Bob share their secret number, they use it as the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method, a lock that both of them can open with the same key, and from then on they can exchange as many messages as they like. This is what happens, invisibly, when your browser shows a padlock beside a web address: your device and a website that has never seen you before agree on a key in a fraction of a second, across networks where anyone might be listening, and everything afterwards is scrambled with it.

There is one honest limitation worth knowing, which is that the method guarantees the shared secret is hidden from onlookers but does not by itself prove who you are talking to. If a more active villain stood between Alice and Bob and pretended to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, running the exchange separately with each, then Alice and Bob would each end up sharing a secret with the villain rather than with each other. To defend against this, real systems add a further layer in which each side can prove its identity, using related public and private number pairs to create something like a signature that only the true owner could produce and which anyone can check. A related and famous scheme, known as RSA, builds a lock anyone can snap shut with a public number but only the owner can open with a private one, using the difficulty of breaking a huge number back into the two primes that were multiplied together to make it, and again the pattern is the same: multiplication is easy, undoing it is hard.

What is striking is how little the idea demands: there is no secret meeting, no courier, no shared codebook, only the observation that some arithmetic is a one-way street, and that two people can each walk down it by different routes and still arrive at the same spot while an observer is left standing at the entrance.
