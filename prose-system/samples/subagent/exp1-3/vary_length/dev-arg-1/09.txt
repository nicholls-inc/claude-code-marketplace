**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American suburb and you will find a quiet monument to a rule nobody remembers voting for. It is the parking lot that is never full. It sits in front of the pharmacy, the dentist, the taco place, an expanse of asphalt sized for the busiest imaginable day and used, in practice, on none. Cities put it there on purpose. They wrote it into the code. It is called a minimum parking requirement, and cities should abolish it.

The rule is simple. Before you can build an apartment, a cafe or a clinic, you must also build a set number of parking spaces. Two per unit. One per 300 square feet of retail. One for every four seats in a restaurant. The numbers did not come from careful study of how people live. Many came from a handbook of rough traffic-engineering guesses, often drawn from a handful of suburban sites, then copied from one city's code into the next. Donald Shoup, the UCLA planner who spent decades dissecting them, called the result a mess of arbitrary figures that planners treated as science.

Consider what they cost. A single space in a surface lot eats roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles and the entry lanes. Structured parking is far worse, often running tens of thousands of dollars per space, and more still underground. That money does not vanish. It lands in the rent, in the price of the sandwich, in the decision not to build at all. A developer who could fit twelve apartments on a lot but must also fit twenty-four parking spaces will build eight, or none. Housing that never gets built is a cost too, though nobody sees it.

Defenders of the rule say it protects neighbourhoods from spillover, with cars crowding onto residential streets. That worry is real, and I do not wave it away. But look at the remedy. We force every new building to provide parking whether or not its occupants own cars, then act surprised when the supply is excessive and the land is wasted. A smarter fix exists. Price the curb. Charge for street parking at rates that keep a space or two open on every block, and let residents buy permits if they need them. Cities that have tried this, in places from Pasadena to Seattle, found that the supposed chaos did not arrive.

Notice also who the rule hurts most. Households without cars, which skew poorer, older and younger, pay for spaces they never use. A tenant who bikes to work subsidizes the neighbour with two SUVs. That is a regressive tax collected through the rent, and few people would defend it if it were written on a bill rather than buried in a zoning table.

The rule also bends the shape of a city. Because every building must carry its own private lot, buildings spread apart. Walking distances stretch. A corner shop that once sat beside the sidewalk now sits behind a moat of pavement, and so people drive to it, which seems to justify the lot. The rule produces the demand it claims to serve. Meanwhile, parking-flattened land produces little tax revenue per acre compared with the compact blocks that existed before the rules arrived. Old main streets, built before minimums, routinely out-earn the sprawling strip malls built after.

Does abolition mean no parking? No, and this is where critics often go wrong. Removing a minimum is not imposing a maximum. Developers will still build parking when customers and tenants want it, because a building with nowhere to park will be hard to lease in many places. The difference is that the quantity gets set by the market and by the project's actual needs, not by a number in a code. A suburban grocery will still build a big lot. A downtown studio building near a train station will build a small one, or none, and charge lower rent for it. Both outcomes are rational. Neither one should require a variance and a six-month hearing.

The evidence from cities that have moved is encouraging. Buffalo scrapped its minimums citywide in 2017. Minneapolis, Austin, and a growing list of others have followed in whole or in part. Developers there have not abandoned parking. They have simply built what fits, and some projects that were previously impossible have gone ahead. The sky has not fallen.

Cities face a housing shortage, a climate problem and chronic budget strain. Few single reforms touch all three. Ending parking minimums does: more homes, less driving induced by design, more taxable value from the same land. It costs the treasury nothing. It asks only that officials stop pretending they know, lot by lot, how many cars a stranger's building will need.

Let the market decide how much parking to build. And where it matters, let the price of the curb do the rest.
