In late August 1854, a terrible illness struck the crowded streets of Soho, in London. Within three days, more than a hundred people living near Broad Street had died. Within ten days, about five hundred were dead. The illness was cholera, a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhea, drains the body of water, and can kill a healthy adult in a day or two. Nobody in London knew where it came from, and the man who would find out was a doctor named John Snow.

At the time, almost everyone of standing believed in the miasma theory. This held that diseases like cholera spread through "bad air," foul smells rising from rotting waste, swamps and graveyards. It was a sensible idea in a city that stank. London had no real sewage system, and cesspools lay beneath many houses. But Snow, a physician who had pioneered the use of anesthesia and had attended Queen Victoria during childbirth, doubted it. Cholera, he noticed, attacked the gut first, not the lungs. If the cause were something breathed in, why would the sickness begin in the stomach? He suspected people were swallowing something, and that it came from contaminated water. He had published this idea in 1849, and most of the medical world had shrugged.

When the Broad Street outbreak began, Snow lived only a short walk away. He set out to test his idea in the most direct way possible: he went door to door. He obtained the list of the dead from the local registrar and began visiting the homes, asking where each family got its drinking water. Most households had no running water. They carried it from public pumps in the street, and the pump on Broad Street was popular because its water looked clear and tasted fresh.

The pattern emerged quickly. Nearly all the deaths clustered around that one pump. Snow went further than simply noting this. He drew a map, marking each death with a black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars piled up thickest around the Broad Street pump, thinning out with distance. It was a striking picture, though Snow knew a map alone was not proof. Skeptics could say the area was simply dirty, or that the air there was worse.

So he pursued the exceptions, which turned out to be the most persuasive evidence. Close to the pump stood a workhouse housing about five hundred people, yet only five died. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street employed seventy workers, and none of them died. The brewery had its own well too, and the workers were also given beer to drink, so they rarely touched the pump water at all.

Then there were the odd cases far from the neighborhood. A widow who lived in Hampstead, several miles away, died of cholera. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and loved the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her each day. Her niece, who visited and drank the same water, also fell ill and died. No bad air from Soho could explain either death.

By September 7, Snow had gathered enough to present his findings to the local board of guardians. He urged them to act, and the next day they removed the handle from the pump, so no one could draw water from it. The outbreak was already fading by then, since many residents had fled the area, so critics later argued the handle removal changed little. Snow himself was honest about this. But his broader case was growing stronger.

The source was found soon after. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who had begun as a skeptic of Snow's theory, conducted his own inquiries. He discovered that a baby living at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with diarrhea just before the outbreak, and that the family's cesspool lay only a few feet from the well. Examination showed that the brick lining of the well was cracked, allowing waste to seep into the drinking water. The mother had washed the infant's soiled cloths and emptied the water nearby. A single household's waste had poisoned a whole neighborhood.

Snow had also been at work on a larger study, comparing customers of two water companies that supplied south London. One drew its water from a stretch of the Thames downstream of the city's sewage, while the other had moved its intake upstream to cleaner water. Households served by the dirtier supply suffered far more deaths. Together, the evidence made his argument hard to dismiss.

Yet acceptance came slowly. The authorities largely rejected his conclusions at first, and Snow died in 1858 without seeing his theory triumph. It was only later, as bacteriologists identified the cholera microbe and sanitary reformers built proper sewers, that his work was recognized for what it was. Today he is regarded as a founder of epidemiology, the science of tracking how diseases spread through populations. His method, mapping cases, asking questions, and chasing down exceptions, remains the foundation of how public health investigators work.
