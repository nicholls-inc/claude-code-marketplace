Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Cursive

Every few years a headline announces that handwriting is dead. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, and children who will spend their working lives tapping screens should not waste precious classroom hours looping their letters together. Several education systems have acted on this logic. Some American states dropped cursive from their standards a decade ago, and many teachers elsewhere quietly let it slide. I think this is a mistake. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and teach it properly, because it serves children's thinking, their reading, their motor development and their independence in ways that a keyboard does not replace.

Start with the strongest objection, because it deserves a fair hearing. Time in a primary day is finite. If an hour a week goes to practising joins, that hour is taken from reading, mathematics, science or typing. Typing is certainly a real skill, and nobody sensible argues children should leave school unable to use a keyboard. But the choice is not between handwriting and typing. Fluent handwriting takes perhaps ten to fifteen minutes of focused practice a day in the early years, and much of that practice happens inside other lessons, because children are writing about history or stories anyway. The cost is modest. What matters is whether the benefit justifies it, and the evidence suggests it does.

The first benefit is cognitive. Researchers who compare handwriting with typing keep finding that the hand does something the keyboard cannot. A widely discussed study from Norwegian researchers, using electroencephalography, found that handwriting produced more widespread brain connectivity than typing, in regions associated with memory formation and learning. Earlier work by the psychologist Karin James showed that young children who practised forming letters by hand showed stronger activation in reading-related brain areas than children who typed or traced the same letters. These studies are small, and we should not oversell them. Still, the direction is consistent. Pressing a key is the same gesture for every letter. Forming a letter is a different movement each time, and that variety appears to help the brain learn what each letter is.

Cursive in particular adds something to plain print. When a child prints, every letter is a separate act, with a lift of the pen and a fresh start. When a child joins, the word becomes one continuous movement. This matters for spelling. A child who writes the word through as a single flowing shape is rehearsing the letter sequence as a unit, and the hand begins to remember common patterns such as the endings of words and the clusters inside them. Teachers who have used joined writing with children who struggle to spell often report that the rhythm helps. I would not claim this is proven beyond doubt, and the research on cursive specifically is thinner than the research on handwriting in general. But the plausible mechanism is sound, and the downside risk is nil.

The second benefit is speed and fluency, and here the practical case is strong. Print is slow. Every lifted pen costs a fraction of a second, and over a page of writing those fractions pile up. Children who write slowly and with effort run into a bottleneck. If forming letters absorbs their attention, they have less left over for what they are trying to say. Educational psychologists describe this as a limit on working memory. A child who has automated the physical act of writing can spend her mental effort on vocabulary, structure and ideas. Studies of primary pupils have linked handwriting fluency to the quality and length of their written compositions, even after accounting for ability. The same logic applies at the other end of school. Examinations in many countries are still handwritten, and a teenager who cannot write quickly and legibly for two hours is at a disadvantage that no amount of typing practice will remove.

A taught joined style is the quickest route to that fluency. Many schools now take an approach where children learn print first and then are left to develop a personal style by themselves. The result is often a muddle. Children invent their own joins, some efficient and some awkward, and by ten or eleven their writing may be fast but illegible, or legible but slow. Teaching a consistent joined style gives every child a sound foundation. They can depart from it later, as adults always do, but they depart from something good rather than from nothing.

The third benefit concerns reading. A child who cannot read cursive cannot read a great deal of the written world. Grandparents write birthday cards in it. Historical documents, from wartime letters to family recipes, are written in it. Archives are full of it. A generation that cannot read handwriting becomes dependent on transcription for anything that predates the word processor, and loses direct access to its own past. That sounds dramatic, but teachers in the United States, where cursive instruction has lapsed in many districts, already report students who cannot read the Declaration of Independence in its original form or a note from a relative. Reading cursive does not require writing it, a critic may reply, and that is true. But the surest way to learn to read a script is to learn to write it, and an hour spent forming the letters teaches recognition more quickly than any alternative.

The fourth benefit is the one least often mentioned, and it concerns children who find learning difficult. Pupils with dyslexia and dyspraxia often struggle with the reversal of letters such as b and d. In print, those two letters are mirror images that differ only in orientation. In a joined style each begins with a distinct entry stroke, and the movements feel different in the hand. Occupational therapists have long recommended cursive for such children for this reason. It is not a universal cure and some children find joining hard. But as a general approach it removes a particular confusion, and a school that abandons it takes away a tool from the pupils who most need support.

Now consider the best argument on the other side, which is that handwriting is becoming obsolete in adult life. Most people under forty rarely write more than a shopping list. Why train children for a skill they will hardly use? The answer is that the argument proves too much. Few adults use long division in daily life, now that every phone has a calculator, yet no one proposes dropping it from the primary curriculum. We teach children arithmetic by hand because doing so builds understanding that the machine cannot give them. The same is true here. We teach handwriting not only because adults write, but because the act of writing is part of how children learn to think in words. Many adults also find they use handwriting more than they expected. They take notes in meetings, sign forms, write cards, and annotate documents. Studies of university students suggest that those who take notes by hand tend to process the material more deeply and remember it better than those who type, although this particular finding has been challenged on replication and I would treat it with care.

There is also a question of dignity and pleasure that sits outside the research. A child who has learned to write a fluent, attractive hand takes quiet pride in it. Handwriting is one of the few places in the school day where a child makes something beautiful with her own body, and where a pupil who struggles with maths or reading may still excel. A hand-lettered thank-you note or a carefully copied poem is a small piece of craft. To tell children that this is pointless because a machine could do it faster is to teach them a rather narrow view of what is worth learning.

None of this means we should return to the harsh drilling of earlier generations. Children should not be punished for an untidy page or kept in at break to repeat a row of loops. The method matters. Short, frequent practice, good posture, a comfortable pen grip, and attention to letter formation from the start all help. Teachers should be trained to teach handwriting, since many today were never taught how themselves. Children with real physical difficulties should be given alternatives without shame. And typing should be taught alongside, with proper technique, from about the age of eight or nine. The two skills complement each other.

The fair way to judge any item in the primary curriculum is to ask what it gives a child in proportion to what it costs. Joined-up handwriting costs a few minutes a day. In return it offers a fluent hand for the years of written exams, a possible boost to spelling, a way into the handwritten past, a steadier path for children who struggle with letter confusion, and a small but real source of pride. The case against it rests mainly on the observation that adults type, which is true and beside the point. Schools exist to prepare children to think, not merely to copy what adults do on a given Tuesday. On those terms, cursive earns its place, and primary schools should keep teaching it.
