On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, Boston's North End was going about an ordinary winter day. The air had turned unseasonably mild. Teamsters guided horse-drawn wagons along Commercial Street, workers ate lunch on stoops, and children played near the waterfront. Beside the harbor stood a steel tank nearly fifty feet tall, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, which the company was converting into industrial alcohol for wartime munitions. Demand was high, and the tank was kept nearly full.

A little after half past twelve, the tank failed. Witnesses heard a sharp report, like artillery, followed by a deep rumble. Then the steel seams gave way, and a wave of molasses burst outward. Some estimates put its speed near 35 miles per hour and its height as much as 25 feet. The wave smashed through the streets, lifting wagons and flattening wooden buildings. It tore an elevated railway's supports from their footings and knocked a firehouse off its foundation. Horses were killed where they stood. People caught in the flood were pinned against walls and under debris. Molasses is far thicker than water, and it moved more like wet concrete than a river, so escape was nearly impossible.

Rescuers arrived quickly, but the work was slow and sticky. Molasses clung to clothing, tools, and the cobblestones, and the sweet smell hung over the district. Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Many of the victims were immigrants and laborers who lived in the crowded tenements nearby.

The cleanup stretched for weeks. Crews shoveled the sludge, hosed the streets with seawater, and spread sand over the pavement. Some molasses reached the harbor and stained the water brown. For years afterward, residents said that on warm days the North End still smelled faintly of molasses.

The disaster did not end when the streets were clean. Victims' families and neighbors sued the company, arguing that the tank had been poorly built, rushed into service, and never properly tested. The case dragged on for years. In 1925, a court found the company liable, and the damages, though far from enough to undo the losses, were a rare case of a corporation being held to account for an industrial failure.

The Great Molasses Flood remains a strange and sobering chapter of Boston history. A sweet, ordinary product, stored under pressure by people in a hurry, turned a busy street into a deadly flood, and it took years of litigation to hold anyone responsible.
