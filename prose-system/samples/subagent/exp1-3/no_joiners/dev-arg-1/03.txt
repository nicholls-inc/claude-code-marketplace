Let the Parking Lot Go

Every city has a rule that quietly decides what gets built, who can afford to live there, and how people move around. It does not appear in the glossy vision documents. It lives in the zoning code, and it says that every new home, shop, and restaurant must come with a fixed number of parking spaces. Cities should repeal it.

Minimum parking requirements began as a modest tool to keep street spillover in check. They have grown into one of the most expensive and least examined mandates in American land use. A typical rule asks for two spaces per apartment, one space for every few hundred square feet of retail, and a set number of spaces per restaurant seat. Planners chose many of these figures from a handbook of parking counts taken at suburban sites with ample free lots. Nobody tested whether those counts matched what a downtown block or a transit corridor actually needs.

Start with cost. A surface space takes up roughly 300 square feet once you include the aisles, and a space in a garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build. Underground spaces cost far more. Developers do not absorb that money. It is folded into rents and purchase prices, which means a tenant who owns no car still pays for a space she never uses. Studies of apartment buildings consistently find that many spaces sit empty even at peak hours. A rule that forces everyone to buy a product only some people want is a hidden tax on housing, imposed during a housing shortage.

Next, consider what gets lost. Parking requirements eat land and floor area that could hold homes, shops, or trees. On a small lot the math can kill a project outright. A corner building that would fit four apartments and a bakery may be allowed only two apartments, or nothing at all, because the required spaces will not fit. Old main streets show the damage. Many were built before these rules, with buildings pressed against the sidewalk and businesses packed tightly. Try to build that same street today and the code forbids it. Small landlords and local entrepreneurs suffer most, since large developers can absorb the cost and the paperwork while a family trying to convert a garage cannot.

Then there is the effect on the places people want to be. Surface lots break up walkable districts, push destinations farther apart, and make driving the only sensible choice. That in turn creates the traffic and the parking demand the rules claim to manage. It is a loop, and the minimums keep it spinning. Cities that want lower emissions, safer streets, and stronger local businesses cannot also mandate the infrastructure that works against all three.

The standard objection is that without requirements, new buildings will flood neighborhoods with cars searching for curb space. This worry deserves a real answer.

First, removing minimums does not ban parking. It lets the people who know the project best decide how much to provide. Lenders, who want reliable tenants, and developers, who want to fill their buildings, have strong reasons to include parking wherever demand exists. Suburban projects will keep building plenty of it. What changes is that a building near a train station or on a walkable street is no longer forced to build for a world that does not exist there.

Second, the evidence from cities that have acted is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017 and saw projects move forward that would otherwise have stalled. Minneapolis, Portland, Austin, and a growing list of other places have followed with sweeping reforms, and Auckland in New Zealand ended the requirements across the whole city. Reports from these places describe more housing proposals, many of them with fewer spaces than the old rules demanded, and no wave of parking chaos. Developers often still build parking, just not a mandated amount.

Third, where curb space truly is scarce, there are better tools than blanket mandates. Cities can price street parking so a few spaces are always open, issue neighborhood permits, and return the revenue to local streets. Pricing handles the real problem, which is the overuse of free space, directly rather than by forcing every building to oversupply it.

Some will say that reform is unfair to drivers or hostile to people who rely on cars. It is neither. Plenty of residents, including older adults and people with disabilities, need convenient access, and a flexible system can protect accessible spaces and loading zones far better than a rigid count. Letting a project size its parking to its setting is not the same as telling anyone to give up a car.

Cities spend enormous energy on subsidies, incentives, and special programs to coax housing into existence. Repealing parking minimums costs the treasury nothing. It removes an obstacle the city itself placed in the way. The honest case for keeping the rule is habit, and habit is a poor reason to make homes more expensive. Take the minimums out of the code, let builders respond to real demand, and manage the curb with prices instead of guesses.
