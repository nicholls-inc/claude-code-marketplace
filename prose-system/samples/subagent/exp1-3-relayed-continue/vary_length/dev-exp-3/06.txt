**Agreeing on a Secret in Public**

Imagine you want to share a secret with a stranger, but the only way to talk is by shouting across a crowded room. Everyone can hear every word. It sounds impossible. Yet this is exactly what your phone does each time it connects to a shop's website, and it works. The trick is a clever use of maths that is easy to do in one direction and almost impossible to undo.

**The paint-mixing picture**

Start with paint, because the idea is easier to see before any numbers appear.

Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow. Anyone can know this. Next, each of them privately picks a secret colour. Alice picks red and Bob picks blue. They never say these aloud. Alice mixes her red into the yellow and sends the resulting orange across the room. Bob mixes his blue into the yellow and sends over green.

Now comes the key step. Alice takes Bob's green and stirs in her own secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange and stirs in his own secret blue. Both end up with the same muddy brown, because each has combined yellow, red and blue. The order of mixing does not matter.

The eavesdropper, Eve, saw yellow, orange and green. She cannot get the brown, because she would need to take the red or the blue out of a mixed paint. Mixing is easy. Unmixing is hopeless.

Real cryptography replaces paint with numbers. We need a kind of "mixing" that really is one-way. For that, we use clock arithmetic.

**Clock arithmetic**

You already know this from reading the time. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, not 15. You wrapped round at 12. Mathematicians call this "arithmetic modulo 12", and the wrapping is written "mod". So 15 mod 12 equals 3.

We are not limited to 12. We can wrap at any number we like. Let us use 23, so that 30 mod 23 is 7, and 50 mod 23 is 4. You simply divide, throw away the whole number of times it fits, and keep the remainder.

We also need powers. Writing 5 to the power 3 means 5 × 5 × 5, which is 125. Powers grow quickly. Combine them with wrapping, and something odd happens. Powers of a number, wrapped at 23, jump around in a way that looks random.

**The one-way street**

Here is the heart of it. Suppose I tell you that 5 to the power of some secret number, wrapped at 23, comes out as 8. Can you work out the secret number?

With numbers this small, yes. You just try 1, 2, 3 and so on until you hit 6. It takes seconds.

But swap 23 for a number with 600 digits, and the secret for a number of similar size. Now trying every possibility would take longer than the age of the universe, even with every computer on Earth working together. Going forward, computing the answer from the secret, is quick, thanks to clever shortcuts. Going backward, finding the secret from the answer, has no known shortcut. This lopsidedness is the engine of everything that follows.

**The exchange, step by step**

This method was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, building on ideas by Ralph Merkle. Let us run it with our small numbers.

*Step one: public choices.* Alice and Bob agree, openly, on two numbers: the wrap-around value 23, and a base number 5. These are the "yellow" in our paint story. Eve hears them. That is fine.

*Step two: private choices.* Alice picks a secret number, 6. Bob picks his own secret, 15. Neither ever says their number to anyone. These are the red and the blue.

*Step three: the public mixes.* Alice computes 5 to the power 6, wrapped at 23. That is 15,625, and dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8. She shouts "8" across the room. Bob computes 5 to the power 15, wrapped at 23, and gets 19. He shouts "19". These are the orange and the green.

*Step four: the private finish.* Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power, 6, wrapping at 23. The answer is 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power, 15, wrapping at 23. His answer is also 2.

They have arrived at the same number without ever saying it. The shared secret is 2.

**Why do they match?**

It is not luck. Look at what each person really computed. Alice's result is 5 to the power 15, raised to the power 6. Bob's is 5 to the power 6, raised to the power 15. Raising a power to a power means multiplying the exponents, so both equal 5 to the power of 6 × 15. Multiplication does not care about order. Both people landed on 5 to the power 90, wrapped at 23, though neither ever calculated that directly.

This is the "mixing order does not matter" property from the paint, written in algebra.

**What Eve knows, and what she lacks**

Eve heard four numbers: 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the secret, she needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. Getting Alice's 6 means solving "5 to the power of what, wrapped at 23, gives 8?" With these tiny numbers she will crack it in a minute. With a 600-digit wrap-around, she is stuck. Nothing else she has heard helps. She cannot multiply 8 and 19 together or combine them in any simple way to produce 2.

The shared secret is then used as a key. Alice and Bob feed it into an ordinary scrambling method, one that is fast and that both of them can run, to encrypt everything else they say. The public-key part is only the handshake. The bulk of the conversation is protected by the secret that the handshake produced.

**Where the name comes from**

You may wonder why this is called "public-key" cryptography. In the exchange above, Alice's 8 is her public key: she is happy for anyone to see it. Her 6 is her private key. The pair is linked by the maths, but you cannot get from the public one to the private one. Later schemes, such as RSA, use this same pairing differently. Anyone can lock a message using your public key, but only you, holding the private one, can unlock it. The principle is the same: a lock that anyone can snap shut, and only one person can open.

**The catch: who are you really talking to?**

Everything above protects against someone who only listens. It does not protect against someone who interferes. Suppose Eve can intercept messages and swap them. She could pretend to be Bob when speaking to Alice, and pretend to be Alice when speaking to Bob, running one exchange with each. Alice would think she shared a secret with Bob, when she had in fact shared it with Eve. This is called a man-in-the-middle attack.

To defeat it, you need a way to be sure a public number really belongs to the person claiming it. The web solves this with certificates, which are digital statements, vouched for by trusted organisations, saying "this public key belongs to this website". Your browser carries a list of organisations it trusts. It is the reason a padlock appears next to a web address.

**The big picture**

For most of history, sharing a secret meant meeting first. You had to hand over a codebook, or agree a password face to face. Public-key cryptography broke that rule. Two strangers, with no earlier contact, can build a shared secret in plain view, because some jobs are easy to do and nearly impossible to reverse.

So, the next time you buy something online, remember what happens in the blink before the page loads. Your device and a distant computer each pick a private number, trade a pair of public ones, and, by the quiet commutative magic of multiplication, land on a secret that no listener could have worked out. Mixing paint is easy. Unmixing it never is.
