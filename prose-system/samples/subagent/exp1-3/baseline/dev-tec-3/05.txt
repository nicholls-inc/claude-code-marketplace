Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV). The cell behaves like a nonlinear voltage source (the open-circuit voltage, or OCV, which rises with state of charge) in series with an internal resistance. The charger can control either current or terminal voltage, and it uses each in turn.

Stage 1, constant current (CC). The charger forces a fixed current, typically 0.5C to 1C. (1C is the current that would fill the nominal capacity in one hour.) Terminal voltage rises as the cell charges, because the OCV climbs and the current drop across the internal resistance adds to it: V = OCV(SoC) + I*R. This stage ends when the terminal voltage reaches the cell's upper limit, about 4.2 V for a typical graphite/cobalt-oxide cell (4.1 to 4.35 V depending on chemistry). Roughly 70 to 80 percent of the capacity is delivered by this point.

Stage 2, constant voltage (CV). The charger now holds the terminal voltage at the limit and lets the current fall on its own. The limit cannot be exceeded. Above it, electrolyte oxidation accelerates, and at the anode lithium plating becomes likely (lithium deposits as metal instead of intercalating into the graphite). Both degrade the cell and are safety hazards. Charging ends when the current decays to a cutoff, commonly C/20 to C/10.

Why the current decays. While the terminal voltage V is clamped, the current is I = (V - OCV)/R. As charge accumulates, OCV rises toward V, so the voltage headroom shrinks and the current falls roughly exponentially. The cell's own state is throttling the charger. Using the full ceiling voltage is the only way to approach 100 percent charge, because OCV is a steep function of state of charge only at the top: the last few percent need the highest voltage.

The internal resistance is not purely ohmic. It includes charge-transfer resistance at the electrode surfaces and a diffusion term. Lithium must diffuse through the solid particles. At the end of charge the particle surfaces are nearly full while the cores are still filling, and the concentration gradient inside each particle relaxes slowly, with a time constant of order r^2/D. This diffusion limit sets a floor on how fast the final capacity can be added, even if the voltage headroom were larger. The effective resistance also grows as the anode fills, since fewer vacant sites remain for incoming lithium.

Practical consequences. Charging from 80 to 100 percent can take as long as 0 to 80 percent. Fast-charge systems therefore often stop near 80 percent, or taper current in steps instead of following a pure CV curve. Cold temperatures raise resistance and slow diffusion, so chargers cut the current to avoid plating. A battery management system monitors cell voltage, temperature and current throughout, and terminates charging if any limit is exceeded.
