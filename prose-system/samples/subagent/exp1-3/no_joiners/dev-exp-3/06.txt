Public key cryptography lets two strangers agree on a secret over a line that others can listen to. It does this by using a calculation that is easy to do in one direction and very hard to undo.

**The problem**

Suppose Alice and Bob want to chat privately, but they have never met and share no secret. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Whatever they say, an eavesdropper called Eve will hear every word.

For most of history, secret messages needed a shared key. This might be a codebook or a rule for scrambling letters, and both people had to hold the same one. That works if you can meet in advance. It fails if you cannot, because sending the key over the open line hands it straight to Eve.

It seems impossible. How can two people create a shared secret when every word of their conversation is public? The answer, found in the 1970s, is a clever use of maths that everyone learns at school.

**The paint picture**

Imagine that Alice and Bob each have a private colour of paint, and that they also agree publicly on a common starting colour, say yellow. Eve knows the yellow too.

Alice mixes her private colour with the yellow and sends the mixture to Bob. Bob does the same with his private colour and sends his mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures but cannot unmix them, because separating paint back into its ingredients is practically impossible.

Now Alice takes the mixture she got from Bob and adds her own private colour. Bob takes the mixture he got from Alice and adds his own. Both end up with the same final blend, which contains yellow plus Alice's colour plus Bob's colour. The order of mixing does not change the result.

Eve has only the yellow and the two half mixtures. She would need one of the private colours to finish the job, and she has neither. Alice and Bob now share a colour nobody else can make.

Mixing paint is easy and unmixing it is hopeless. Maths has a version of this idea.

**Clock arithmetic**

The maths version uses a kind of calculation that works like a clock. On a twelve hour clock, if it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, you get three o'clock. You counted past twelve and wrapped around to the start. Mathematicians call this working with remainders. You divide by the size of the clock and keep only what is left over.

We will use a clock with 23 positions instead of 12. When a number goes past 23, we divide by 23 and keep the remainder. So 25 becomes 2, and 46 becomes 0. This is just division with remainder, which you met in primary school.

We also need powers. Five to the power of three means five times five times five, which is 125. On our clock, 125 divided by 23 gives 5 with a remainder of 10, so 125 lands on position 10.

Here is the key fact. If I tell you that the answer is 10, and that I started with 5 and used some power, you cannot easily work out which power I used. With tiny numbers you could try every option, but the numbers in real life have hundreds of digits. The forward direction takes a computer a split second. The backward direction would take longer than the age of the universe.

**The exchange, step by step**

Alice and Bob agree in public on two numbers. They choose a starting number, which will be 5, and the clock size, which will be 23. Eve hears both, and that is fine.

Alice picks a private number and keeps it secret. She chooses 6. She calculates 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625. Dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8. She announces 8 in public.

Bob picks his own private number, 15, and tells nobody. He calculates 5 to the power 15 on the 23 clock and gets 19. He announces 19 in public.

Now each of them uses the other person's public number. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power of 6. The answer on the clock is 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private power of 15. The answer on his clock is also 2.

They have the same number, 2, and neither ever said it aloud. This is the shared secret.

**Why it works**

Both calculations produce the same result because of a rule about powers. Raising 5 to the power 6 and then raising that to the power 15 gives 5 to the power of 6 times 15. Doing it the other way round gives 5 to the power of 15 times 6. Since 6 times 15 equals 15 times 6, both routes arrive at the same place. This is the paint mixing again, where the order does not matter.

Now think about Eve. She knows the clock size 23, the starting number 5, and the public numbers 8 and 19. To find the secret, she must work out one of the private numbers. That means asking which power of 5 gives 8 on the clock. With a clock of 23 she could find it in seconds by trying everything. But real systems use a clock with a number hundreds of digits long. Then there are far too many possibilities to try, and no known shortcut that works for everyone.

So the security does not come from hiding the method. The method is published in textbooks and anyone can read it. Security comes from the fact that the private numbers are never sent and cannot practically be recovered from the public ones.

**From a shared number to private chat**

The shared secret is just a number, but that is all Alice and Bob need. They use it as the key for an ordinary scrambling method that both of them know. Because they now hold the same secret and Eve does not, they can send messages that look like gibberish to anyone listening. Your phone and the websites you visit do exactly this in the blink of an eye every time a small padlock appears in your browser.

**The other famous method**

A second approach, usually called RSA, uses a different one way calculation. Multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, even when each prime has hundreds of digits. Taking the answer and finding the two primes that made it is brutally hard. In this scheme a person publishes a public key, which is the product, and keeps the primes private. Anyone can use the public key to lock a message, but only the holder of the primes can unlock it. That is why this style is called public key cryptography. There are two keys, one that everyone may see and one that stays hidden.

**What to remember**

The whole idea rests on three simple points. First, some calculations are easy to do forwards and nearly impossible to reverse. Second, the order of certain steps does not change the outcome, so two people can reach the same answer by different routes. Third, each person keeps one ingredient completely private, so the listener never gets the full recipe.

Two strangers can therefore create a shared secret in public. Nobody needs to meet first, and nobody needs to trust the line.
