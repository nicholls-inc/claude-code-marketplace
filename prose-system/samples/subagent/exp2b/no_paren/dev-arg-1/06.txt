Free the Curb: Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet law shaping the ground beneath your feet. Before a single apartment, restaurant, or clinic can be built, the planning code says how many parking spaces must come with it. A restaurant might need one space for every hundred square feet of dining room. An apartment building might need two spaces per unit, whether or not the tenants own cars. These minimum parking requirements are among the most consequential and least debated rules in urban policy. Cities should repeal them.

Start with the basic problem. Parking requirements force a private business to supply a product, storage for cars, that the market might not call for. If a developer believes that tenants want two spaces per unit, the developer will build them. The rule matters only when the developer would build fewer. In those cases the city overrides a decision made by people who have money on the line and replaces it with a number drawn from a manual. Those manuals are often built on thin evidence. The standard reference tables rely on surveys of suburban sites with ample free parking, sometimes with a handful of samples. Cities then copy one another's numbers, and an arbitrary figure hardens into law.

The costs are large and they land on everyone. Structured parking can cost tens of thousands of dollars per space to build, and underground spaces cost far more. That expense does not vanish. It is folded into rents, home prices, and menu prices. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the garage, because the cost is bundled into the rent. Researchers who study this have found that requirements push up housing costs and reduce the number of homes built at all. Some small projects become impossible, since a narrow lot cannot fit the required spaces and the required building both. The housing shortage in many cities is not only a story of zoning for density. It is also a story of lots that must be sacrificed to asphalt.

Then consider what the land could otherwise do. Surface lots and garages consume valuable ground in the places where land is most scarce. Downtowns that should hum with shops and homes are instead pocked with empty lots that sit idle outside of peak hours. Buildings are pushed apart so that cars can fit between them, which makes walking less pleasant and transit harder to serve. Cities then wonder why their centers feel hollow and why their tax base is thin. A parcel covered by a parking lot generates a small fraction of the revenue an equivalent parcel generates when it holds a mixed-use building. Parking mandates quietly subsidize the least productive use of urban land.

The most common objection is that without mandates, developers will build too little parking and cars will flood the streets. This fear misunderstands what repeal does. Repealing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it, and in most places they will build plenty, because lenders, tenants, and customers want it. Cities that have removed their requirements, including Buffalo, which dropped them citywide in 2017, and Minneapolis and Anchorage more recently, have seen developers continue to include parking where it makes sense and trim it where it does not. The result is a better match between supply and demand, not a parking apocalypse.

Where spillover does occur, cities have better tools than a blunt mandate. Pricing on-street parking so that a space or two is usually open makes it far easier to find a spot, and it turns a free resource into revenue that can be spent on the neighborhood. Residential permit districts can protect blocks that are genuinely stressed. These approaches address the actual problem, which is a scarce public curb given away for nothing, rather than loading the cost onto every new building. Free parking is not free. It is paid for by someone, usually by people who never use it.

There is also a fairness argument. Parking requirements treat every household as though it drives, and they penalize those who do not. Many older adults, young people, and lower income families live without a car or with one, and they end up subsidizing spaces for others. A rule that raises housing costs for the people with the least to spare is hard to defend as neutral. And the environmental case reinforces the point. Abundant guaranteed parking is an invitation to drive everywhere, which means more congestion, more emissions, and streets designed for vehicles first. Cities that want to meet climate and safety goals cannot keep mandating the infrastructure of car dependence.

Some will say that a middle path is wiser, such as reducing requirements near transit while keeping them elsewhere. Reform of that kind is better than nothing, but it keeps the underlying mistake intact. It assumes that planners know the right number and that the number simply varies by location. A full repeal is cleaner. It lets the people who actually finance and use buildings decide, and it saves the city from the endless work of updating tables that were never grounded in much to begin with.

The strongest case for repeal is modesty. Nobody can know in advance how much parking each lot in a living city needs, and that need changes as habits, technology, and transit change. A mandate freezes a guess into law. Repeal admits the limits of the guess and lets reality answer. If drivers want spaces, there will be spaces, and they will be priced honestly. If they do not, the land can hold homes, shops, trees, or jobs instead. Cities have spent seventy years requiring parking. It is time to let them choose.
