Primary schools in England are expected to teach joined-up handwriting, and I think they should keep doing so. The national curriculum introduced in 2014 says that pupils in Year 2 should start using the diagonal and horizontal strokes needed to join letters and should learn which letters are best left unjoined. At the end of that year, when children are six or seven, teachers assess the size and spacing of their letters, and those judged to be working at greater depth are expected to join them. This is a modest requirement, and nobody is asking for copperplate. The real question is whether even this much is worth classroom time when so much adult writing is done on a keyboard or a phone. I think it is, and my reasons are mostly practical.

It helps to begin with what the evidence does not show. Claims that joined writing makes children cleverer, or better at spelling, go well beyond anything the research supports. Studies comparing children taught to print with children taught to join have generally found little difference in how legible or how quick their writing becomes. The better-supported findings concern handwriting as a whole. In 2012 Karin James and Laura Engelhardt scanned the brains of a small group of four- and five-year-olds who could not yet read, and found that those who practised letters by hand showed more activity in reading-related areas than those who typed them. In 2024 Audrey van der Meer and Ruud van der Weel recorded brain activity in 36 university students and found wider connectivity when they wrote in cursive with a pen than when they typed. A third study, by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer in 2014, reported that students who took lecture notes by hand answered conceptual questions better than those who typed, although later attempts to repeat it found a smaller effect, so I hold that result loosely. These studies are small, and I would not build a curriculum on any of them. They do suggest that forming letters with a pen is more than a slow way of getting words onto a page.

My first argument is about fluency. A child has to write automatically before attention can go to what is being said, because a child who must think about every letter has less left over for sentences and ideas. Research by Steve Graham and Virginia Berninger has linked handwriting fluency in primary pupils to the length and quality of what they write. That finding holds for any fluent script, so it does not settle the choice between print and joined. What joining adds is that a whole word can be written in one movement. The pen leaves the paper at the end of the word instead of after every letter, and the gaps between words are easier to see because they are the only gaps. In my view this is why joined writing tends to be faster once it is learned, and speed matters most when children are asked to write at length.

The second argument is that children will be asked to write by hand for a long time yet. GCSE and A-level examinations in England are sat on paper, and some papers last two or three hours. A pupil who writes slowly or illegibly at sixteen has a handicap that is hard to mend then, because the habit was set years earlier. Markers are also influenced by presentation. In experiments where the same essay was copied out in a neat hand and in an untidy one, the neat copy has tended to receive higher marks. I would prefer markers not to behave this way, but they do, and a school that ignores it does the child no favours. Pupils given a laptop on grounds of need can still use one. The aim is that everyone else leaves school able to write at a reasonable pace in a form others can read.

The third argument concerns cost. Joined writing does not need much time. Schools that teach it well usually use sessions of ten to fifteen minutes a few times a week over two years or so. At three sessions of fifteen minutes a week that comes to about 28 hours in a year of 38 teaching weeks, and some of that practice doubles as spelling work. The usual alternative is typing, which is worth teaching but slow to learn properly. A seven-year-old has small hands, and touch typing at a useful speed takes months of regular practice. A school can do both, and I think the best ones do. Finland dropped compulsory cursive from its national curriculum in 2016 and asked schools to teach typing alongside print. Some teachers there welcomed the change, and it has also set off argument about whether children now get enough handwriting practice. That worry seems to me a better reason to keep a clear expectation in the curriculum than to remove one.

It also helps to be clear about what good teaching looks like, since the case depends on it. In Reception children learn to form letters in print, with attention to where each one starts and which way it turns. Around the end of Year 1 or the start of Year 2, they learn a few joins at a time, usually beginning with the diagonal join from letters such as i, u and t, and then the horizontal join from o, r, v and w. They practise on words, not on isolated letters, so that the join is learned as part of a real spelling. The teacher models the movement at the board, and the child's own writing is checked against it. None of this is hard to run in a class of thirty, and it does not need a special programme, although several are sold. It does need the adults to be consistent, because a child who sees three different letter shapes on three different walls learns none of them well.

The American experience points the same way. The Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out, and a number of states followed by dropping it. Since then more than twenty states have passed laws to bring it back, California among them in 2023, often after parents complained that children could not read a handwritten note or sign their own names. Legislators are not always good judges of teaching, and some of these laws owe a lot to nostalgia. Still, I know of no evidence that anything better filled the gap where cursive was removed.

There is a smaller point about reading. Much of the handwriting a child meets is joined, such as a card from a grandparent, a teacher's comment or a letter in an archive. A child who writes in a joined hand can read these without difficulty, and one who does not may find a page of cursive as hard as a foreign alphabet. Reading and writing are separate skills, but the two are easiest to learn together. A signature, which adults still need for some purposes, is often a joined one.

The strongest objection is that joining is harder for some children. Those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties can find the continuous movement tiring, and forcing them through a single prescribed style can do harm. I accept that, and the curriculum already allows for it by telling teachers to decide which letters are best left unjoined. Some schools teach a print style with small exit strokes, which gives children a half step towards joining and costs slower writers very little. Left-handed children, who make up roughly one in ten, may need a different paper angle and pen, because the hand tends to cover what has just been written. Some specialists in dyslexia argue that a joined script helps because it makes reversals and wrong letter order less likely, although others prefer print for the same children. I take that as a reason to teach joined handwriting as the default and stay flexible about individual pupils, not as a reason to drop it. Most adults end up with a personal mixture of print and joins anyway, and a sound early training gives them something to mix.

A second objection is that this is tradition defended for its own sake. I do not think so. I would drop joined writing as soon as a better use of those hours became clear, and for now I cannot see one with more evidence behind it. Handwriting is still how children are examined. The research on handwriting, though not on joining as such, points to benefits for early literacy. Joining makes writing faster for most children at little cost. A school that teaches it carefully, expects modest results from Year 2 and leaves room for the child who needs another route is doing something sensible. None of this makes joined handwriting the most important thing a primary school does. It is a small skill with a modest payoff, and the sensible position is to teach it for a short time each week and then move on.
