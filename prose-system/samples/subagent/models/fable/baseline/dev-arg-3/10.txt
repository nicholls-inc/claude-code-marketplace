Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years someone proposes that joined-up handwriting should go the way of the slide rule. Children type, the argument runs, adults type, and the hours a seven-year-old spends looping an f into an l could be spent on coding, or on reading, or on anything with a future. Finland made cursive optional in 2016. The American Common Core standards left it out in 2010. The case sounds modern and unsentimental, and I think it is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching children to join their letters. The reason is not that joined writing is beautiful or traditional. The reason is that a fluent hand is still one of the cheapest and most useful tools a child can be given, and that joining is how a hand becomes fluent.

Start with the sentimental case, because it should be set aside. Defenders of cursive often say that children who cannot write it will be unable to read their grandmother's letters or the original Declaration of Independence. This is a weak argument. Reading joined script is a far smaller skill than writing it and can be picked up in an afternoon. Nor is there good evidence for the grander claims: that cursive cures dyslexia, or that it uniquely wires the brain. Where researchers have directly compared children taught to join with children taught to print, the differences have been small and inconsistent. Anyone arguing for joined-up writing has to concede this, and I do.

What the evidence does support, strongly, is the value of handwriting itself, and of handwriting that has become automatic. Writing letters by hand appears to help young children learn to recognise them. Brain-imaging work by Karin James and colleagues found that pre-literate children who had drawn letters freehand showed activity in reading-related regions when they later looked at those letters, while children who had typed or traced them did not. Forming a letter stroke by stroke seems to teach the child what the letter is in a way that pressing a key cannot, since every key feels the same.

The second finding matters more for older primary children. Writing is a juggling act: the writer has to hold an idea, choose words, spell them, punctuate them and physically produce them, all at once, with a working memory that is small at seven and not much bigger at ten. Whatever the hand demands is taken from the mind. Decades of work by Virginia Berninger, Steve Graham and others have shown that children whose handwriting is slow and effortful write shorter, poorer compositions, and that when handwriting is explicitly taught, the quality of what they compose improves along with its legibility. A child who has to think about how to form a k is a child who has less attention left for the sentence. Handwriting instruction turns out to be composition instruction by another route.

This is where joining comes in. The goal is automaticity: letters produced quickly, legibly and without thought. Print script, in which the pen lifts after every letter, is a fine place to begin and a poor place to stop. Each lift costs time and each letter has to be placed afresh, which is why the spacing of a slow printer wanders. Joining is simply what a hand does when it speeds up. Watch any adult who writes a great deal and you will see joins, whether they were taught them or not; fast writers almost all end up with a mixed hand, joining where it is easy and lifting where it is not. The studies of writing speed bear this out. The fastest writers tend to use neither a pure print nor a pure cursive but a blend of the two.

That finding is sometimes used against teaching joins. It supports the opposite conclusion. A child can only blend what she knows. If she has been taught the handful of diagonal and horizontal strokes that carry one letter into the next, she can keep the joins that serve her and drop the ones that do not. If she has never been taught them, she will invent her own under time pressure, at eleven or fourteen, and invented joins are often the awkward kind that produce illegible script. The sensible aim for schools is not an ornate copperplate with every letter linked and every capital flourished. It is a plain, efficient joined hand, taught properly once and then allowed to become personal. England's national curriculum already describes roughly this: children begin learning the strokes needed to join in Year 2 and are expected to choose which letters to leave unjoined as they get older. That is the right model, and it is a modest one.

The strongest objection is opportunity cost. Time in a primary classroom is finite, and typing is a real skill that children also need. I agree with both halves of that and deny the conclusion. Teaching joined writing well takes little time: short, regular sessions of ten or fifteen minutes over two or three years, much of it folded into spelling practice, since writing a common letter string as one movement helps to fix it in memory. It needs no devices, no licences and no technician. Touch typing should be taught as well, and it competes for curriculum time with many things besides handwriting. The choice between pen and keyboard is a false one. Children need both, in the way they need both mental arithmetic and a calculator.

There is also a practical fact that reformers tend to pass over. In Britain, and in most countries, the examinations that decide a young person's options are still largely written by hand, for hours at a time. A sixteen-year-old sitting a history paper has to get an argument onto the page against the clock. If her hand is slow, she writes less and she scores lower, whatever she knows. Perhaps exams will all move on screen eventually. They have been about to do so for twenty years, and the logistics of secure devices for every candidate in every hall remain difficult. A school that stops teaching fluent handwriting today is betting an eight-year-old's exam results on a reform that may not arrive in time for her.

The stakes here are not evenly spread. Children from homes with books, pens and attentive adults will pick up a serviceable hand whether or not school teaches it. The children who depend on explicit instruction are the ones who get little practice elsewhere, and children with motor or learning difficulties, for whom fluent letter formation will not come by itself and has to be taught step by step. Some of those children will do better with a keyboard in the end, and they should have one. But for many, the continuous movement of a joined hand is easier than print: there are fewer decisions about where to start each letter and where to put the pen down, and letters that begin from the same point are harder to reverse. Abandoning the teaching does not free these children from handwriting. It leaves them to struggle with it untaught.

Beyond school, handwriting remains more useful than its critics allow. Adults still annotate, sketch out plans, scribble in margins, fill in forms and write cards. There is some evidence, disputed in its details, that taking notes by hand encourages students to summarise where typists transcribe. I would not rest the case on that study. I would rest it on something simpler: a pen and paper is the one writing technology that needs no power, no login and no update, and it lets a person think on a page with nothing else competing for attention. A child who can write quickly and legibly owns that tool for life. A child who cannot is dependent on a screen being available and on resisting everything else the screen offers.

None of this requires nostalgia. The handwriting lesson I am defending is brief, plain and aimed at speed and legibility, with no prizes for elegance. It sits alongside typing, and it should be judged by whether children leave primary school able to write a page without their hand getting in the way of their thoughts. Countries that dropped the teaching have had second thoughts on these grounds; a string of American states, California among them, have put cursive back into their requirements after a decade without it.

The question was whether primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. They should, because fluent handwriting frees a child's attention for thinking, because joining is the ordinary route to fluency, because the children with least support gain most from being taught it, and because the cost is a few minutes a day. Dropping it would save very little time and would cost many children a good deal.
