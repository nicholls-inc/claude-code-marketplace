Free the Curb: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find more space set aside for parked cars than for the people who own them. Surface lots wrap around strip malls. Garages swallow the ground floors of apartment buildings. Much of this was not the free choice of builders or shoppers. It was the law. Minimum parking requirements tell developers how many spaces they must build, whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these rules entirely, and the sooner the better.

Start with how the rules came about. Planners in the mid twentieth century worried about traffic and spillover parking, so they borrowed rough estimates from engineering manuals. Those figures were based on a handful of suburban sites, often counted at their busiest hour. A restaurant must provide one space for every hundred square feet. An apartment must provide two spaces per unit. These numbers were guesses dressed up as science, and they hardened into code across the country.

The trouble is that a guess written into law becomes a mandate. Every required space costs real money. A surface space can cost several thousand dollars to build, while a space in a structured garage often runs past thirty thousand, and underground spaces cost more still. Developers do not absorb that cost out of generosity. It lands on tenants, buyers, shoppers and small business owners. A family that owns no car still pays for parking through higher rent. Researchers who study this call it a hidden tax on housing, and it falls hardest on people with the least money.

Housing is where the damage is most obvious. Cities everywhere complain that homes are too expensive, yet many of them demand that builders devote a large share of every site to storing vehicles. On a small lot, the parking requirement can decide whether a project is possible at all. A developer who could fit ten homes ends up with seven, or with none. Entire categories of cheap, modest building, such as small apartment blocks and conversions of old shops into flats, are quietly outlawed by arithmetic.

Consider also what parking does to the look and feel of a place. Older neighborhoods that were built before these rules, with buildings pressed up against the sidewalk and shops on the corner, are the ones people pay a premium to visit and live in. Newer districts, designed around mandated lots, tend to be sparse and hostile to anyone on foot. We have legislated away the very qualities that make cities worth living in, then wondered why downtowns feel empty.

The usual objection is that without requirements, developers will build too little parking and cars will flood the streets. This fear deserves a direct answer. Removing a minimum does not ban parking. It simply lets the builder decide how much to provide. Developers have every reason to supply parking when customers want it. A lender will not finance an apartment building that tenants cannot live in, and a shop owner knows which customers drive. Where parking is truly scarce, the market will price it, and that is a feature rather than a flaw.

The evidence from cities that have tried reform supports this. Buffalo scrapped its citywide minimums in 2017, and developers there have kept building parking where they judged it necessary while dropping it where they did not. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing list of other places have followed. Neighborhoods did not descend into chaos. What changed was that projects once blocked by arithmetic began to pencil out, and builders could respond to what their buyers actually wanted rather than a formula from a manual.

A second objection is about fairness. Some argue that residents who park on the street will lose out when new buildings bring more cars. But the answer to crowded curbs is to manage the curb, not to force every building to hide a parking lot inside it. Cities can charge fair prices for street parking, issue permits for residents, and return the revenue to the neighborhood. A price that rises when a block fills up keeps a space or two open at all times. That solves the real problem of scarcity far better than blanket mandates that oversupply parking in one place and ignore it in another.

There is also the matter of climate and public health. Required parking is a standing subsidy for driving. It guarantees that every trip by car ends in a free, convenient space, which makes driving look cheaper than it really is and nudges people away from transit, cycling, and walking. A city serious about emissions cannot keep a rulebook that insists on a place to park for every possible driver while it asks residents to drive less.

None of this means a city must be hostile to cars. Rural areas, suburbs, and many working places will still need plenty of parking, and builders will provide it. The point is that the decision belongs to the people who bear the cost and see the demand, not to a formula written decades ago. Cities that want more homes, livelier streets, and a fairer deal for those who cannot afford a car already have the tool in hand. They need only strike a few lines from the zoning code.

The land is there. The money is there. The demand for places to live is overwhelming. What stands in the way is a rule that no longer deserves to exist. Repeal it.
