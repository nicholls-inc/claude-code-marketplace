**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet mandate shaping what gets built. Before a developer can open a restaurant, a apartment building, or a clinic, the zoning code tells them how many parking spaces they must provide. A restaurant might need one space per 100 square feet. An apartment block might need two per unit. These minimum parking requirements look like technical details, but they are among the most consequential rules in city planning. Cities should remove them.

Start with the basic problem: the numbers are guesses. Parking minimums trace back to mid-century manuals that estimated peak demand at suburban sites with free parking, then turned those estimates into law. Nobody measured what the right amount of parking was for a given downtown corner. Studies of actual use keep finding that required lots sit mostly empty. A building owner who knew a tenant would pay for more spaces would build them without being told to. The rule only binds when it forces more parking than the market would supply, which means it only ever does one thing: make people build something they did not want.

That costs real money, and the bill lands on everyone. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count aisles and ramps. A space in a structured garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, menu prices, and the price of goods. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the garage beneath their building. Housing is where this stings most. Requiring two spaces per unit can make small, affordable apartments financially impossible, so what gets built is larger, pricier, and fewer. In a housing shortage, a rule that taxes every new home is hard to defend.

Parking rules also eat the land itself. In a growing city, land near jobs and transit is the scarcest resource there is, and minimums hand huge portions of it to idle cars. Lots that could hold homes, shops, or parks hold asphalt. Buildings get pushed apart, which makes streets less walkable, which makes driving more necessary, which seems to justify more parking. The rule creates the dependence it claims to serve. Older neighborhoods built before minimums, with their tight rows of shops and flats, are often the ones people love most and the ones that would be illegal to build today.

Defenders raise a fair worry: if we drop the requirements, will streets overflow with parked cars and spill into residential blocks? It is a real concern, but it conflates two questions. Removing minimums does not ban parking. It lets builders and lenders decide how much to provide, and most will keep providing plenty where demand is high, because tenants and customers want it. A suburban grocery store will not open without a lot. What changes is the cases where the mandate is clearly wrong: the small apartment near a train station, the corner cafe in an old building, the church that only needs spaces on Sundays. Flexibility lets those projects exist.

Where street spillover is a genuine risk, there are better tools than a blanket mandate. Cities can price on-street parking so that spaces turn over and always a few are free. They can issue neighborhood permits, or share the revenue with local residents. These measures manage the actual scarce thing, curb space, directly. A minimum requirement is a blunt instrument that tries to solve a pricing problem by pouring concrete.

The evidence from places that have tried this is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated citywide minimums in 2017, and developers there have since built projects with less parking when it suited them, while still building plenty where it did not. Cities such as Minneapolis and Anchorage have followed, and a growing list of states and municipalities have moved the same way. The sky has not fallen. What has happened is that builders gained room to fit a project to its site, and some previously unbuildable lots became viable.

There is also an issue of fairness. Roughly one in ten households in many cities has no car, and the share is far higher among lower-income residents, the elderly, and the young. Parking minimums make these households subsidize the storage of other people's vehicles. A policy that quietly transfers money from the carless to the car-owning, and from renters to landowners, would be hard to pass if proposed openly. It survives because it sits in the fine print.

Finally, think about what cities say they want. Almost every city claims to want more housing, lower carbon emissions, a stronger tax base, and lively streets. Parking minimums work against each one. They raise building costs, encourage driving, spread development thin so it yields less revenue per acre, and deaden the street with blank garage frontage. It is rare for a single reform to push in the right direction on so many fronts at once.

None of this requires hostility to cars. Drivers will keep driving, and developers will keep building parking wherever people will pay for it. The point is to stop pretending a planner can set the right number for every building in town. Let property owners, lenders, and neighbors work that out with real prices and real information. Cities that repeal their minimums are not abandoning planning. They are admitting that on this question the old formulas were wrong, and that it is time to let people build what the city actually needs.
