In the summer of 1854 the district of Soho in London was one of the most crowded places on earth. Families shared rooms, rubbish piled in the courtyards, and beneath the cobbled streets lay old cesspools that had never been properly emptied. In the last days of August a terrible illness began to spread through the streets around Broad Street. Its name was cholera.

Cholera was a feared disease. A healthy person could be struck down in the morning and be dead by nightfall. The victim suffered violent vomiting and diarrhea and lost so much water that the skin turned blue and shrivelled. In the space of about ten days, roughly five hundred people in that small neighbourhood died. Many families fled. Shops closed. Those who stayed behind buried their neighbours and waited.

Most learned people of the day believed they knew the cause. They thought cholera spread through bad air, which they called miasma, a foul vapour rising from rotting matter and filthy ground. It seemed sensible. The poorest districts smelled terrible and also had the most sickness.

A local physician named John Snow disagreed. Snow was forty one years old, the son of a labourer from York, and he had risen through hard work to become one of the finest doctors in London. He was famous for his skill with anaesthetics, and he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. Years earlier, during an earlier cholera epidemic, he had noticed something that bothered him. The illness attacked the stomach and bowels first. If people were breathing in poisoned air, he reasoned, the lungs should suffer first. Snow became convinced that people were swallowing something invisible, and that it was carried in water. He had said so in print, but almost nobody had listened.

Now the outbreak was happening a few streets from his own home, and Snow set out to prove his idea. He did not have a microscope that could reveal the true cause, because nobody yet knew that tiny living organisms could make people sick. What he had was patience, a notebook and a talent for asking sensible questions.

He began by obtaining the list of the dead from the local register office. Then he walked the streets, knocked on doors and asked grieving families where they got their drinking water. The answers pointed again and again to the same place, a public pump on Broad Street. Snow marked each death on a map of the neighbourhood with a small black bar. The bars gathered around the pump like iron filings around a magnet.

The pattern had odd exceptions, and Snow treated them as the most valuable clues. Near the pump stood a workhouse housing more than five hundred people. Remarkably few of them died. Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well and never touched the Broad Street water. A brewery on the same street had no deaths at all among its workers. The men were given free beer, and they also drank from the brewery well, so they had little reason to visit the pump.

Then came a stranger case. A widow living far away in Hampstead died of cholera, and so did her niece. The woman had once lived in Soho and loved the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her every day. Snow could hardly have hoped for a clearer example.

On the evening of 7 September he went before the local Board of Guardians and laid out his evidence. The officials were not wholly convinced, but they agreed to act. The handle of the Broad Street pump was removed so that nobody could draw water from it. The outbreak was already fading by then, because so many residents had fled or died, and sceptics have used that fact ever since to question how much the handle really mattered. Still, the gesture became legendary.

The investigation was not finished. A local clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who knew the neighbourhood well, had at first doubted Snow. Whitehead went on to question residents himself and traced the outbreak to its likely beginning, a baby girl at a house on Broad Street who had fallen ill with the disease just before the epidemic began. Her family had washed her soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool that lay only a few feet from the well. The brickwork was cracked, and the filth had been seeping in.

Official bodies at first rejected Snow's conclusion. The authorities did not want to believe that their water supply was to blame, and the old theory of foul air lingered for years. Snow died in 1858, at the age of forty five, without seeing his work accepted. In the decades that followed, scientists identified the germ that causes cholera, and cities built proper sewers and clean water systems. Snow came to be remembered as a founder of modern public health, the man who showed that a map and a notebook could save lives.
