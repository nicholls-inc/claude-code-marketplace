A heat pump does not make heat by burning fuel or by passing current through a resistor. It moves heat that already exists outside the house and releases it indoors. Air at 0°C still holds a great deal of heat, because the scale only reaches zero heat at about minus 273°C. Even on a frosty night the air, the ground or a body of water contains energy, and the machine's job is to collect some of it.

It does this with a loop of refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature. Propane, which many newer units use, boils at about minus 42°C, and R32, another common refrigerant, boils at about minus 52°C. In the outdoor unit the cold liquid runs through a coil while a fan blows outside air across it. The air is warmer than the refrigerant, so heat flows into the refrigerant and it boils into a gas, even when the air is at minus 5°C.

A compressor then squeezes the gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature, and this one leaves the compressor at roughly 70 to 90°C, which is well above the temperature of your rooms. The hot gas goes to a second heat exchanger indoors, where it passes its heat to the water in your heating circuit and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then lowers the pressure, the liquid turns cold again and the loop restarts. A fridge uses the same cycle to move heat from its inside to your kitchen.

The electricity goes to the compressor and the fans, and it is a small share of the heat delivered. The ratio of heat out to electricity in is called the coefficient of performance. A typical air-source unit reaches about 4 when the air is at 7°C and about 2.5 to 3 when it is at minus 7°C. Over a full year a well-installed system commonly averages between 3 and 3.5, which means 1 kWh of electricity yields 3 to 3.5 kWh of heat. A modern condensing gas boiler turns about 90 per cent of the energy in the gas into heat, so it can never deliver more heat than the energy it takes in.

Running costs depend on the price of each fuel. If gas costs 6p per kWh and the boiler is 90 per cent efficient, heat costs about 6.7p per kWh. If electricity costs 25p per kWh and the heat pump averages 3, heat costs about 8.3p per kWh. On those prices the heat pump is slightly dearer to run. Tariffs change, and some homes can use cheaper night or time-of-use rates, so your own bills are the first thing to check.

The second thing to check is the house. A heat pump is most efficient when it sends water at 35 to 45°C round the radiators, while a boiler usually sends 60 to 70°C. A pump can reach higher temperatures, but its coefficient of performance falls as it does. A well-insulated house with large radiators or underfloor heating suits a heat pump. A draughty house with small radiators may need loft insulation, draught-proofing or bigger radiators first, and that adds to the cost. An installer should calculate the heat loss room by room and tell you which radiators need replacing. You will also need space outside for a unit about the size of a large suitcase, which makes roughly 40 to 50 decibels at a metre, and usually a hot water cylinder indoors. In England and Wales the Boiler Upgrade Scheme currently offers £7,500 towards installation.

Units from the main manufacturers are rated to run down to about minus 20°C, and they are widely used in Norway and Finland. In very cold, damp weather ice forms on the outdoor coil, and the unit briefly reverses to melt it, which costs a little energy.

My view is that the case is strongest if the house is already reasonably insulated, the boiler is near the end of its life and you can get a grant or a lower electricity tariff. If the house is poorly insulated and the boiler is only a few years old, keeping the boiler while you improve the insulation is a sensible first step.
