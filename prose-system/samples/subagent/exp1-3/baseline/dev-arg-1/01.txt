**Scrap the Parking Mandate**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet form of central planning that few residents have ever voted on: the minimum parking requirement. It tells a developer that a restaurant must supply a set number of spaces per thousand square feet, that an apartment building must provide one or two spots per unit, and that a medical office needs more than a restaurant does. These numbers feel like common sense. They are in fact guesses, often borrowed from other cities, and they do real damage. Cities should remove them.

Start with what the rules do to the cost of building. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you include the aisles and ramps. A structured space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, home prices, and menu prices, and they are paid by everyone, including the households that own no car. Researchers who study this, most famously Donald Shoup, have estimated that mandated parking adds a meaningful share to the cost of new housing. When a city is short of homes and wants more of them, forcing each one to carry a built-in garage is a strange way to go about it.

The mandates also kill projects before they begin. Many small lots, such as the corner parcel that could hold a four-unit building or a neighborhood cafe, cannot physically fit the required spaces alongside the building. The result is no building at all. A lot that could have housed families becomes a vacant gravel pad, or the existing structure stays frozen because any change in use triggers a parking review. Older downtowns, with their narrow lots and mixed uses, were built before these rules existed. Today they would be illegal to build.

Consider what the requirements do to the shape of a place. Every mandated space pushes buildings apart, so destinations spread out and walking becomes impractical. Once walking is impractical, people drive for every errand, which creates the demand for parking that the rule claimed merely to meet. It is a loop that feeds itself. The land consumed is huge. In many American downtowns, more area is devoted to storing cars than to housing people or employing them. That land pays little in property tax, and the city must still maintain the roads and pipes that serve it.

The usual defense is that without mandates, developers will skimp and the neighborhood will drown in spillover cars. This worry deserves a direct answer. First, developers are not in the business of building places nobody can reach. Lenders and tenants care about parking, so most projects will include it where it is valuable. What changes is that the amount is set by the market and the specific site, not a formula. A building near a train station can offer fewer spaces, and a suburban office park can offer many. Second, where street parking really is scarce, the remedy is to price it. Charging for curb spaces, and returning the revenue to the neighborhood through a parking benefit district, keeps spaces available without forcing every builder to overbuild.

The evidence from cities that have tried this is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and others, including Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing number of California cities following state reforms, have since done the same. The sky did not fall. Developers still build parking when they need it, and they now have the option to build less, which opens the door to more homes, more small businesses, and the reuse of old buildings. Early reports also suggest that projects pencil out in places where they previously could not.

Critics also raise equity. Lower-income people, they note, are more likely to depend on cars in places with weak transit. That is true, and it is an argument for better transit and safer streets, not for forcing the cost of parking onto renters. Low-income households are also the ones hurt most by a parking mandate, because they pay for spaces they may not use. A tenant who owns no car subsidizes the neighbor who owns two. Removing the mandate lets housing be offered with or without parking at different prices, which widens choices for the people with the least money.

Nothing here requires a city to ban parking. Repealing a minimum is not the same as imposing a maximum. Owners remain free to build as many spaces as they like. The reform simply moves the decision from a zoning table written decades ago to the person who knows the site, the customers, and the cost. That is a modest idea, and a conservative one in the old sense: let those with the knowledge and the money at stake decide.

Cities spend enormous energy arguing about density, affordability, and climate. Parking minimums quietly work against all three, and they do so while pretending to be neutral technical standards. They are not neutral. They are a subsidy for driving, paid for by everyone and hidden in the price of everything. Removing them is one of the cheapest, fastest, and least controversial housing reforms available. A city that is serious about growing should start there.
