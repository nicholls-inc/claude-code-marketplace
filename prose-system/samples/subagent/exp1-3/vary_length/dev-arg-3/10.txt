**Keep the Joined Line: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Cursive**

Every few years, a headline announces the death of handwriting. Children type now, the argument goes, so why spend precious classroom hours teaching them to loop an "l" into an "e"? Keyboards are everywhere. Tablets are in every satchel. Surely the joined-up script of our grandparents belongs in a museum, alongside the abacus and the inkwell.

I think that argument is wrong. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it properly. Not as nostalgia, and not as a test of neatness, but because it does real work for children that nothing else in the curriculum does quite as well.

Start with what handwriting actually is. It is not simply a way of getting words onto paper. It is a motor skill that sits at the junction of language, memory and movement, and it is one of the first complex fine-motor tasks a child masters. When a seven-year-old writes the word "island", her hand has to hold the whole word in mind, sequence the letters, control pressure and direction, and keep going without lifting off. That is a lot of cognitive traffic for a small brain, and the practice builds pathways that tapping a key does not.

The research here is suggestive rather than conclusive, and I will not pretend otherwise. But the direction of travel is consistent. Studies using brain imaging have found that writing by hand activates the regions linked to reading and memory more strongly than typing does. Experiments with young children learning letters show that those who form letters by hand often recognise them faster and more reliably than those who only press keys. Work on note-taking in older students points the same way: those who write by hand tend to process ideas rather than transcribe them, and they remember more. None of this proves that cursive in particular is magic. It does suggest that the hand matters, and that removing it from early education would be a gamble with children as the stake.

So why joined-up, rather than the print-style letters children learn first? This is the fair question, and the honest answer is that cursive offers a few specific advantages that print does not.

The first is flow. In printed writing, every letter is a separate act: lift, place, form, lift again. In a joined script, a whole word becomes a single movement. The pen stays on the page. Speed comes naturally, because the hand is not constantly stopping and restarting. For a child who will spend years taking notes, writing exams and drafting stories, that fluency is not a luxury. It is the difference between a hand that keeps up with the mind and a hand that drags behind it.

The second is spelling. Because a joined word is learned as one continuous motion, the letter sequence becomes embedded in muscle memory. Teachers of children with spelling difficulties have long observed this. Writing "ing" or "tion" as a single flowing unit helps those patterns stick in a way that assembling them letter by letter often does not. It is hard to measure, but it is a recurring theme in the classroom, and it is especially relevant for children who struggle with literacy.

The third is a particular benefit for children with dyslexia and related difficulties. Many specialists recommend cursive precisely because it reduces reversals. A printed "b" and "d" are mirror images of each other, which is a trap for a child who already finds letter orientation confusing. In cursive, the two letters begin and move differently, so they feel different under the hand. That is a small thing until it is your child who keeps getting it wrong, and then it is everything.

Now consider the strongest objection, which is that time is finite. A primary timetable is already crowded. If we add handwriting practice, something else has to give, and surely it should be something that matters more than curly letters.

But this overstates the cost. Handwriting does not need an hour a day. Ten or fifteen minutes of focused, well-taught practice, a few times a week, is enough for most children to become fluent by the end of key stage two. The skill is not demanding in hours. It is demanding in good teaching, and that is a different problem. Schools that treat handwriting as an afterthought, handing out photocopied worksheets and hoping for the best, will get poor results and then conclude that cursive does not work. Schools that teach it as they would teach any other skill, with modelling, feedback and patience, tend to see children who write confidently and legibly. The method is not the failure. The neglect is.

There is also an equity argument, and it deserves more weight than it usually gets. Not every child has a laptop at home. Not every classroom has enough devices, or reliable ones, or the funds to replace them. Pen and paper are cheap and universal. If we decide that handwriting is optional, we create a world where some children get fluent typing and others get neither skill properly. Those who already have advantages will be fine; they will learn to type at home and write by hand at a private tutor. Those who do not will fall between the two. Teaching handwriting in school is one of the few ways to make sure every child leaves with a basic, durable tool for communicating.

And consider the exams. Whatever we think of them, most written assessments in primary and secondary school are still handwritten, and will be for the foreseeable future. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly is penalised, however good her ideas. Examiners cannot reward what they cannot read. Slow, effortful, cramped printing costs marks, and it costs them quietly, without anyone ever naming the cause. Fluent joined writing is not a guarantee of success, but it removes one obstacle that should never have been there.

I should say what I am not arguing. I am not arguing for the rigid, fussy cursive of old, with its mandatory slant and its marks for tidiness. Handwriting should not be a moral test. Nor am I against typing; children should learn to use a keyboard, and learn it well. The two skills are not rivals. They are complementary, and a well-educated adult needs both. The question is not whether to teach one or the other. It is whether we are willing to let one quietly disappear.

There is, too, a quieter point about what we lose. Handwriting is personal in a way that typing cannot be. A letter written by hand carries the writer's pace, her hesitation, her character. Anyone who has found an old birthday card in a parent's looping script, or a recipe annotated in a grandmother's cramped margin notes, knows this. We should not build a curriculum around sentiment, and I am not suggesting we do. But a society that can no longer read its own handwritten past, whose children cannot decipher a letter from a great-grandparent or a note in an archive, has lost something real. Teaching cursive is, in part, teaching children to read the documents that came before them.

Some will say that this is all very well, but the world has moved on. Perhaps. Yet the same was said about teaching children to read maps, to do mental arithmetic, to learn poetry by heart. Each time, the sceptics pointed to a tool that could do the job for us. Each time, we discovered that the tool replaced the output but not the benefit. A calculator gives you the answer; it does not give you number sense. A keyboard gives you the text; it does not give you the same relationship between hand, eye and thought.

So what should schools do? Teach print first, as most already do, so children can form letters clearly. Introduce joins early, ideally by seven, once letter shapes are secure, rather than leaving them as a rushed afterthought. Give handwriting regular, short, well-supported practice. Support children who struggle rather than abandoning cursive for them, and allow flexibility for the small number who genuinely cannot manage it. And teach typing alongside, not instead.

That is a modest programme. It asks for a few minutes a day and a teacher who knows what good handwriting looks like. In return, children get a fluent, personal, durable skill, one that helps with spelling, supports the children who most need support, keeps pace with their thinking, and costs almost nothing to provide.

The case against cursive rests largely on the idea that it is obsolete. But obsolete things do not usually keep turning out to be useful. Joined-up handwriting is not a relic. It is a tool that still works, still helps, and still belongs in the primary classroom. We should keep teaching it.
