Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). The charger is a controlled source, and the cell's own chemistry determines how much current it can usefully accept at each point.

Stage one is constant current. The charger forces a fixed current, commonly 0.5C to 1C (a 1C rate would fill the nominal capacity in one hour). Current drives lithium ions out of the cathode, across the electrolyte, and into the graphite anode, while electrons travel through the external circuit. As the anode fills, the terminal voltage rises steadily from about 3.0 V toward the upper limit, typically 4.2 V for a cobalt-based cell. Most of the charge, often 70 to 80 percent, goes in during this stage.

Stage two begins when the terminal voltage reaches the limit. The charger then holds the voltage constant and lets the current fall on its own. Charging ends when the current decays to a cutoff, usually about 0.05C. Holding voltage rather than current matters because pushing a fixed current into a nearly full cell would drive the voltage above the safe limit, causing electrolyte decomposition, lithium metal plating on the anode, and heat.

The slowdown has a simple electrical explanation. The terminal voltage you measure is not the cell's equilibrium voltage. It is the open-circuit voltage (OCV), which rises with state of charge, plus the current times the cell's internal resistance. Treat the cell as a voltage source in series with a resistor. During CC, the I times R term sits on top of the OCV. As OCV climbs, the cell hits the voltage ceiling while still partly empty. In CV, the allowed drop across the resistance is the ceiling minus the OCV, so the permitted current is (V_limit - OCV) divided by R. As the cell fills, OCV approaches the limit and that difference shrinks toward zero, and the current decays roughly exponentially.

Physical effects reinforce this. Lithium must diffuse into the graphite particles, and a concentration gradient builds up between the surface and the core. Near full, the surface is saturated while the interior is still filling, so the surface cannot accept more without exceeding its limit. The solid-state diffusion time constant is long, which makes the tail of the charge slow. Charge transfer at the interface and ion transport in the electrolyte add resistance that is worse when cold.

The practical consequence is a tradeoff. Fast chargers spend their effort in the CC stage and often stop early, which is why "80 percent in 30 minutes" is common while the last 20 percent takes much longer. Lower temperature or an aged cell raises R, shortening the CC stage and lengthening the CV tail.
