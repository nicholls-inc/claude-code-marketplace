Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and who can only talk to each other by shouting across a crowded square. Anyone nearby can hear every word. They want to end up sharing a secret, such as a number that only the two of them know, so that they can then lock their later messages with it. At first sight this looks impossible, because anything they say to agree on the secret is overheard by everyone. Yet this is exactly what happens every time your phone connects securely to a website, and the trick behind it is one of the neatest ideas in modern mathematics.

The key idea is that some calculations are easy to do in one direction and extraordinarily hard to undo. A familiar example is mixing paint. If you stir yellow and blue together you get green in a few seconds, but if someone hands you a pot of green and asks you to separate it back into the exact yellow and blue you started with, you would not know where to begin. Public-key cryptography uses the same lopsidedness, but with numbers instead of paint. The number version of the story goes like this. Alice and Bob first agree, out loud, on a shared starting colour that everybody is allowed to know. Each of them then secretly adds a private colour of their own and sends the mixture across the square. Then each of them adds their own private colour again to the mixture they received from the other person. Because the order of mixing does not matter, both end up holding the same final shade, while a listener who saw only the public colour and the two mixtures cannot recreate it without unmixing paint.

To turn this into real arithmetic we need an operation that behaves like mixing, and for that we borrow the idea of a clock. On a twelve-hour clock, if it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, it is three o'clock, because the numbers wrap around after twelve. Mathematicians call this clock arithmetic, and you can use any size of clock you like. If we choose a clock with twenty-three hours on it, then any number we calculate is replaced by the remainder left after dividing by twenty-three. Thirty becomes seven, because twenty-three goes into thirty once with seven left over, and forty-six becomes zero, because it divides exactly. This wrapping is what makes the calculation so hard to reverse.

Now we need the easy-to-do, hard-to-undo step, and the one we use is repeated multiplication. Raising a number to a power just means multiplying it by itself that many times, so five to the power of three is five times five times five, which is one hundred and twenty-five. In clock arithmetic on our twenty-three-hour clock we then wrap the answer around. Working forwards is simple, since you just multiply and reduce. Working backwards is the problem. If I tell you that five raised to some secret power gives eight on this clock, you cannot simply divide or take a root, because the wrapping has scrambled the pattern. With a tiny clock you could try every power in turn and find the answer, but if the clock has hundreds of digits, there are more possibilities than there are atoms in the world, and trying them all is hopeless.

Here is how Alice and Bob use this, with the small numbers so that you can follow every step. First, in front of everyone, they agree on two public numbers: the clock size, which is twenty-three, and a starting number, which is five. Everybody in the square hears these, and that is fine. Next, Alice privately picks a secret number, say six, and tells nobody. Bob privately picks his own secret number, say fifteen, and also tells nobody. These private numbers are never spoken aloud, and this is the heart of the whole scheme.

Alice now computes five raised to her secret power of six on the twenty-three-hour clock. Five to the sixth power is fifteen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, and when we divide by twenty-three and keep the remainder we get eight. She shouts the number eight across the square. Bob does the same with his secret: five raised to the power of fifteen, wrapped around the clock, comes to nineteen, so he shouts nineteen. Everyone in the square now knows twenty-three, five, eight and nineteen. What nobody knows is six or fifteen.

The remarkable step comes next. Alice takes the number she heard from Bob, nineteen, and raises it to her own secret power of six, wrapping around the clock as before. Bob takes the number he heard from Alice, eight, and raises it to his secret power of fifteen. Alice's calculation gives two, and Bob's calculation also gives two. They have arrived at the same number without ever saying it aloud. The reason is that both of them have really computed five raised to the power of six times fifteen, which is ninety, just reached by two different routes. Alice took the power of six first and then fifteen, while Bob took fifteen first and then six, and multiplying in a different order gives the same total. That shared number, two, is their secret.

Meanwhile the eavesdropper is stuck. To copy the result she would need to know either Alice's six or Bob's fifteen, and the only way to get either is to work backwards from eight or from nineteen, which is the hard unmixing problem. With a clock of twenty-three hours, anyone can do this by trial and error in seconds, which is why the example is only a toy. Real systems use clocks with numbers more than six hundred digits long, and then the best known methods would take longer than the age of the universe on every computer on Earth.

You may wonder why this is called public-key cryptography when we have described agreeing on a shared secret. The name comes from a closely related family of methods in which each person owns a pair of linked numbers, one made public and one kept private. Anyone can use your public number to lock a message so that only your private number can open it, rather like a padlock that anyone can snap shut but only you hold the key to. The best known version, called RSA, rests on a different hard problem: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but taking the answer and finding the two primes it came from is not. The agreement trick we walked through above was invented earlier and is named after its inventors, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman. In practice, the two ideas are often combined, with the slow public-key step used only to agree on a secret, and a much faster ordinary lock then used to protect the bulk of the conversation.

There is one important weakness that the mathematics alone does not solve. Our eavesdropper could do more than listen. She could pretend to be Bob when Alice calls, and pretend to be Alice when Bob calls, agreeing on one secret with each of them and passing messages between them while reading everything. For this reason, real systems also need a way for Alice to be sure that the number she receives really came from Bob. This is done with digital certificates, which are statements vouched for by trusted organisations that link a public number to a particular website or person. So the secret-sharing trick protects against people who merely listen, and certificates protect against people who impersonate.

What makes the whole idea so striking is how little the two strangers need. They share no password and no codebook beforehand, and they never have to meet. All they need is a calculation that is quick in one direction and hopeless in the other, and the patience to do a little arithmetic on a clock.
