**Apollo 13: A Journey Home**

Apollo 13 left Florida on April 11, 1970, with no reason for anyone to feel uneasy. It was NASA's third planned landing on the Moon. The first two had gone so smoothly that the public had begun to treat lunar flight as routine. Television networks had stopped broadcasting from the spacecraft. The crew were Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights, Fred Haise, and Jack Swigert. Swigert was a late replacement for Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles days before launch and was grounded. The crew rode in a command module named Odyssey, attached to a service module that held their engine, power and oxygen. Trailing behind them was a lunar module named Aquarius, the spidery landing craft meant to carry Lovell and Haise to the Fra Mauro highlands.

For two days the flight was uneventful. On the evening of April 13, the spacecraft was about 200,000 miles from Earth and the crew had just finished a short television broadcast. Mission Control then asked Swigert to flip a switch that would stir the supercold liquid oxygen stored in the service module's tanks. Stirring kept the contents from stratifying so the gauges would read accurately. It was a chore the crew had done many times.

Seconds after Swigert threw the switch, there was a bang. The spacecraft shuddered. Warning lights lit up, and the voltage on the electrical systems began to sag. Swigert reported, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, a little more formally: "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause was buried in the history of Oxygen Tank 2. Years earlier, during preparation on the ground, the tank had been dropped slightly while being moved, which may have damaged its internal fittings. Later, a ground test had run the tank's heaters at a higher voltage than they were built for, and that cooked the insulation on the wiring inside. Nobody noticed. When Swigert stirred the tank, a spark jumped across the bare wires and ignited the insulation in the pure oxygen. Pressure surged, the tank failed, and the blast tore a panel off the side of the service module. The second oxygen tank was damaged and began leaking. Within a couple of hours, essentially all the oxygen was gone, and with it the fuel cells that made the spacecraft's electricity and drinking water.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers spent the first minutes trying to save the command module, but the numbers kept falling. The conclusion was blunt. Odyssey was dying, and the Moon was off the table. The only question was whether the crew could get home alive.

The answer was the lunar module. Aquarius had its own oxygen, its own batteries and its own small engine, all intended to support two men for about two days on the Moon's surface. Now it would have to keep three men alive for roughly four days and serve as a lifeboat. The crew moved into it and shut down Odyssey almost completely, so as to save its limited battery power for the final return to Earth.

The next decision was the trajectory. The spacecraft was on a "free return" path that would swing around the Moon and, if nothing were done, loop back toward Earth, but it was not aimed to land in the ocean safely, and it would take too long. Controllers decided to fire the lunar module's descent engine, which had been meant for landing on the Moon, after the craft passed behind the far side. That burn, about four hours after the accident, sped up the return and aimed it better. On April 14 the crew passed behind the Moon, farther from Earth than any humans have ever been, nearly 250,000 miles. Lovell later remembered that they had little time to admire the view.

Then came the long, cold part. Without power for heat, the lunar module's cabin dropped to around 40 degrees Fahrenheit, and condensation dripped down the walls. The crew drank little, because water was in short supply and the lunar module's cooling depended on it. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. Sleep was almost impossible. Meanwhile, the water system and electronics had to be rationed to the last watt.

A deadlier problem arrived quietly. Every breath the crew took added carbon dioxide to the cabin air, and the lunar module's filters, canisters of lithium hydroxide, were designed for two people over a short stay. They filled up, and the gas rose toward dangerous levels. The command module carried plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's system needed round ones. On the ground, engineers gathered the same sorts of items the crew had aboard: cardboard, plastic bags, a hose, and duct tape. They assembled an adapter that would let a square canister work in a round socket, then read the instructions up to the crew step by step. Lovell, Haise and Swigert built it themselves in orbit. The carbon dioxide level dropped.

There was another worry. Odyssey had to be powered back up before reentry, after days of sitting cold and dead, and nobody had ever written a procedure for waking the command module with such limited battery power. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded by the measles, worked in a simulator with engineers for days to write one. The sequence he and his colleagues developed was read up to the crew and checked in real time.

The trajectory also drifted. A second engine burn was needed, and this one had to be done by hand, because the crew had shut down the guidance platform to save power. Lovell lined up the spacecraft by looking at the Earth, using the sunlit curve of the planet as his reference. Another burn followed later to fine-tune the angle of reentry. Too shallow, and the craft would skip off the atmosphere into space. Too steep, and it would burn up.

On April 17, with Earth close, the crew separated the damaged service module so that the heat shield on the command module would be free to do its work. As the module drifted away, Lovell saw what the explosion had done: an entire side panel was missing and the interior was scorched and tangled. They photographed it, and those images would help investigators. Next, Odyssey's systems came to life as planned, and the crew climbed back in and released Aquarius, the little lifeboat that had saved them. It burned up behind them.

Reentry brought one final stretch of silence. Spacecraft normally lose radio contact for about three minutes as superheated air wraps around them. This time, the blackout dragged on longer than expected. Controllers watched the clock and said little. Then, at last, Swigert's voice came through, and in the South Pacific, three parachutes opened above Odyssey. It splashed down within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, after nearly six days in space.

The investigation that followed, led by Edgar Cortright, traced the failure to that tank's history of small errors and overlooked warnings. The tanks were redesigned, and a third oxygen tank was added to the next mission, Apollo 14. Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, but it is remembered as one of NASA's finest hours. Teamwork, improvisation and calm judgment, from a handful of people in orbit and thousands on the ground, brought three men home.
