A leap second is a single extra second added to the world's official time so that clocks stay in step with the rotation of the Earth. Since 1972 it has been added 27 times, most recently at the end of 31 December 2016, when the clocks showed 23:59:60 before moving on to the new year.

The need for them comes from the fact that there are two ways of measuring time. One is astronomical. A day is the time the Earth takes to turn once, and the sun is overhead at about the same moment each noon. The other is atomic. Since 1967 the second has been defined by the vibrations of caesium atoms, at 9,192,631,770 cycles per second, and atomic clocks keep this beat far more steadily than the planet does. The Earth's spin is slowed a little by the tides and shifted by movements in its core and oceans, so a day is not exactly the same length from one year to the next. Over the long run the planet has tended to fall behind the atomic clocks.

Coordinated Universal Time, the basis of civil time around the world, was set up to follow the atomic clocks while staying within 0.9 seconds of the Earth's rotation. When the gap approached that limit, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service announced a leap second, usually six months ahead.

The trouble is that computers do not cope well with a minute that has 61 seconds. Most software assumes every minute has 60. Leap seconds have been linked to faults in Linux systems and websites in 2012 and to a Cloudflare outage in 2017. Some large technology companies avoid the problem by stretching the extra second over a whole day, but this means that their clocks differ slightly from others for a time. As more of the financial system, telecoms and satellite navigation depends on precise timestamps, the risk of mistakes has grown.

In November 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, which brings together national governments, resolved to stop using leap seconds by or before 2035. The difference between atomic time and the Earth's rotation will then be allowed to grow beyond one second. Its size has not been agreed, and the figure of about a minute has been discussed. A further reason for acting is that the Earth has recently spun slightly faster. This could one day require a negative leap second, which would remove a second, and no such step has ever been tried.

For ordinary readers, little will change. A difference of a minute or so between the clock and the sun would take many decades to appear, and it is smaller than the variation in sunrise across a single time zone.
