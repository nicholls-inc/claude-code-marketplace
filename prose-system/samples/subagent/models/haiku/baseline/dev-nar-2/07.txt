# Apollo 13: The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 13, 1970, the crew of Apollo 13 was nearly two days into a trip to the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, had already flown two Gemini missions and had circled the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was on his first spaceflight. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only days before launch, after Ken Mattingly was pulled from the flight because he had been exposed to German measles. The plan looked simple on paper: fly to the Moon, land in the Fra Mauro highlands, spend two days exploring, and come home.

At 9:07 p.m. Eastern time, roughly fifty-five hours after liftoff and nearly 200,000 miles from Earth, Swigert radioed Mission Control. "Okay, Houston," he said, "we've had a problem here." Moments earlier the crew had felt the spacecraft shudder and heard a loud bang. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space from the service module, the cylinder-shaped section that held the main engine, the fuel cells, and most of the oxygen for the trip.

The cause was an oxygen tank. A stirring fan inside tank 2 was designed to run on a 28-volt supply, but during a ground test the tank's heaters had been powered at 65 volts. The overload damaged the insulation on the fan's wiring. Weeks later, in flight, a short circuit ignited the insulation inside the tank. The fire drove the pressure up until the tank ruptured, and the blast damaged tank 1 and several panels on the service module.

In Houston, flight controllers worked to understand what they were seeing. Within minutes it was clear that the service module had lost its oxygen supply. Its three fuel cells produced electricity and drinking water by combining oxygen and hydrogen. Two were already dead, and the third was failing. The command module, the small capsule that would carry the crew home, now had only its batteries, and those were needed for re-entry.

The crew's lifeboat was the lunar module, Aquarius. It was built to support two men on the Moon for about two days, not to carry three men back to Earth. The crew moved into it, sealed the hatch between the two craft, and began a hard accounting of air and power. The most immediate problem was carbon dioxide. Exhaled air had to pass through lithium hydroxide canisters that absorbed the gas. The lunar module's canister openings were round, but the spare canisters carried from the command module were square. Engineers in Houston, led by a team under Ed Smylie, built a workaround from what was on board: cardboard, plastic bags, duct tape, and hose from the spacesuits. They wrote the procedure and read it to the crew, who assembled the adapter and fitted it into place. It worked, and the carbon dioxide levels stayed manageable.

Power was the next crisis. The crew shut down almost everything to stretch the remaining electricity. Heaters went off, and the cabin cooled until it was near freezing. Water condensed on the walls and dripped onto the crew. Drinking water was cut to a bare minimum, and the men grew thirsty, tired, and cold. Haise fell ill with a painful condition, later believed to be a kidney infection, and spent part of the journey in considerable discomfort.

Navigation was a constant concern. A bad trajectory could leave the spacecraft flying past Earth into deep space, or send it into the atmosphere at the wrong angle. Mission Control planned a short burn of the lunar module's descent engine to set the spacecraft on a free-return path, a route that would swing it around the Moon and back toward Earth without needing a large braking maneuver. The crew carried out the burn and later made smaller corrections, using star sightings to confirm their position. The engine had never been meant for this job, and every firing had to be calculated with care.

On April 15 the spacecraft passed behind the Moon, about 254 kilometers above the surface. Radio contact was lost while they were on the far side, and the crew were alone with their instruments, their breath fogging in the cold. When they came back into view of Earth, the mission was still in trouble, but the path home was set.

The final challenge was the command module itself. Its systems had been switched off for days, and it had to be brought back to life with little margin for error. The crew transferred into it, jettisoned the lunar module, and powered up the capsule. Before re-entry, the service module was cut loose, and the crew saw its damaged side for the first time. Re-entry was delicate. The spacecraft had to enter the atmosphere within a narrow range of angles. Too steep and the deceleration would crush the crew; too shallow and the capsule would skip off the atmosphere and bounce back into space.

On April 17, 1970, the command module Odyssey plunged through the atmosphere and parachuted into the South Pacific just after noon Eastern time, 142 hours into the flight. Recovery teams reached the capsule quickly and brought the three men aboard the aircraft carrier USS Iwo Jima. They were tired, thin, and dehydrated, but alive.

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon, and it is often called a "successful failure." The phrase captures something real. The mission failed its objective but succeeded in its most important job, which was bringing the crew back alive. The investigation that followed led to changes in oxygen tank design, wiring, and the procedures used in ground testing. Apollo 14 flew to Fra Mauro in February 1971 and completed the landing that Apollo 13 had been unable to make.

The story is also one of teamwork under pressure. Flight controllers, engineers, and astronauts worked through a chain of problems that had no textbook answers, and they did it with limited data and little time. The famous line "Failure is not an option" comes from the 1995 film *Apollo 13* and was not, as far as the record shows, spoken in Mission Control during the flight. The real lesson of the mission is less dramatic. Careful people, working with what they had, got a damaged spacecraft and its crew home across nearly 250,000 miles of space.
