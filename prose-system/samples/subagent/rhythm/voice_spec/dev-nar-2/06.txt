Apollo 13 left the Kennedy Space Center on 11 April 1970, at 2.13 in the afternoon, Florida time. It was the third mission planned to land men on the Moon. The commander was James Lovell, who had already flown to the Moon on Apollo 8. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot and was on his first spaceflight. Jack Swigert had joined the crew only days before. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles, and the flight surgeons would not clear him. The command module was named Odyssey and the lunar module Aquarius. The crew were to land in the Fra Mauro highlands.

The launch was mostly normal. The centre engine of the second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the other four engines burned longer to make up the speed. The crew reached orbit and then left it for the Moon. For two days the flight went so smoothly that the television broadcast Lovell and his crew made on the evening of 13 April was not carried by the major American networks.

The cause of what followed had been put in place years earlier. One of the two oxygen tanks in the service module had been dropped about five centimetres while being removed from an earlier spacecraft in 1968. It was checked and installed in Apollo 13 anyway. Before launch it would not empty fully in a ground test, so technicians used its internal heaters to boil the oxygen off. The heater switches were rated for 28 volts, but the ground equipment supplied 65, and the switches fused shut. The tank got far hotter than anyone intended, and the Teflon insulation on the wires inside it was damaged. Nobody knew this at the time.

At about 55 hours and 54 minutes into the flight, the crew were roughly 320,000 kilometres from Earth. Mission control asked Swigert to stir the oxygen tanks, a routine step that mixes the contents so the gauges read correctly. About two minutes later, current ran through the bare wires in tank 2 and started a fire. The pressure rose until the tank failed, and the blast blew a panel off the side of the service module. Swigert reported, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, as "Houston, we've had a problem."

The first signs in Houston were electrical faults and a loss of pressure in tank 2. Tank 1 was also damaged and was leaking. The service module's three fuel cells needed oxygen to make electricity and water, and as the oxygen drained the cells began to fail. Lovell looked out of a window and saw gas venting into space. Within about three hours the oxygen supply in the service module was gone. The command module could run for a few hours on its own batteries, but it would need them for re-entry, so the crew could not stay in it.

The flight director on duty was Gene Kranz, and the decision taken in Houston was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had been built to support two men for about 45 hours on the lunar surface. It now had to keep three men alive for about 90 hours. The crew powered it up, copied the alignment data from Odyssey, and shut the command module down.

The next problem was the route home. Apollo 13 was on a path that would have taken it round the Moon and back out into space, and a return to Earth would have needed a burn that the damaged service module could not be trusted to make. About five hours after the explosion, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine to put the spacecraft back on a path that would loop around the Moon and return to Earth. Later, after they had passed the Moon, a second burn of about four and a half minutes at the descent engine's full thrust shortened the trip and moved the splashdown to the Pacific Ocean, where the recovery ships were waiting. On 14 April, at about 254 kilometres above the far side of the Moon, the spacecraft was 400,171 kilometres from Earth, which remains the greatest distance from Earth reached by any crewed flight.

The trip home was uncomfortable and sometimes dangerous. The lunar module drew very little power to save its batteries, and the cabin temperature fell to about 3 degrees Celsius. Water was rationed to roughly 200 millilitres per man each day. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. The crew slept little, and the combined weight loss across the three of them was around 14 kilograms.

A more urgent problem was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which absorb the gas from the cabin air, were filling up. The command module held spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets took round ones. Engineers on the ground built an adapter from items that were known to be aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a suit hose and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the crew built the device and fitted it. The carbon dioxide level dropped.

Two further tasks remained. One was a mid-course correction, carried out about 105 hours into the flight. With the guidance systems off to save power, Lovell lined up the spacecraft by looking at the Earth's sunlit edge through the window and then fired the engine by hand for 14 seconds. The other was to restart the command module, which had been shut down and was by now cold and damp. Mattingly and engineers in the simulators in Houston worked out a start-up procedure that kept within the small amount of battery power available. They wrote it in a few days, and the crew carried it out.

Before re-entry the crew released the service module, which was no longer needed. This let them see the damage for the first time. One whole side panel was missing, and the equipment behind it was exposed and scorched. The crew photographed it, and the images later helped investigators. They then moved back into Odyssey and released Aquarius, which burned up in the atmosphere.

Re-entry on 17 April was expected to produce a radio blackout of three minutes. It lasted longer, and mission control waited about a minute beyond the expected time before the crew's voices returned. The command module's parachutes opened and Odyssey landed in the South Pacific, near Samoa, at 1.07 in the afternoon, Houston time. It came down about six kilometres from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted 142 hours and 54 minutes. All three men were in reasonable condition, though tired and thin.

A review board chaired by Edgar Cortright investigated the accident and traced it to the tank and the heater switches. NASA changed the design of the oxygen tanks and added a third tank to the service module. Apollo 14 flew in January 1971, and Apollo 15, 16 and 17 later landed on the Moon. Lovell never made a lunar landing. In the years since, the flight has often been described as a successful failure, which is a judgement and not a fact. The facts are that the mission did not reach the Moon's surface and that three men came home alive.
