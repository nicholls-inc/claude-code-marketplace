**Scrap the Parking Mandates**

Every city has a rule that most residents have never heard of and all of them live with: minimum parking requirements. These rules say that before anyone can build an apartment, a restaurant or a dentist's office, they must also build a fixed number of parking spaces, typically one or two per home, or one for every few hundred square feet of commercial space. Cities should repeal them. Not tweak them, not reduce them by a quarter, but remove them and let the people who actually pay for parking decide how much to build.

Start with where the numbers come from. The standard source is a manual of parking generation rates, built from surveys of suburban sites with free parking and almost no transit. Planners then convert those figures into law, treating a statistical observation about peak demand at a few isolated locations as a requirement for every lot in town. The results are often absurd. Cities have required the same number of spaces for a downtown cafe as for one on a highway, and have forced churches, bars and bakeries to build lots sized for a rare peak that occurs a few hours a year.

The cost lands on housing. A surface space takes up roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles, and a structured one can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, with underground spaces costing far more. Those costs are not absorbed by the developer out of generosity. They show up in rents and sale prices, and they are paid by everyone in the building, including households with no car. Mandates also eat land. On a small urban lot, the parking requirement can decide whether a project fits at all, so what gets built is fewer homes, bigger and pricier ones, or nothing. In a housing shortage, requiring developers to spend their scarce land and capital on car storage is a strange priority.

Defenders respond that without mandates, new buildings will push cars onto neighbouring streets. This worry deserves a serious answer, and the answer is that the street is the problem, not the building. Curb parking is usually free or nearly so, which guarantees it is overused. Charge a sensible price, or issue residential permits where neighbours want them, and the spillover concern shrinks. Repealing a mandate does not ban parking. It simply stops forcing it. Developers still build plenty, because lenders and buyers want it, and many projects in car-dependent areas will look much the same as before. The difference is that in places where the demand is lower, such as near a train station, in a walkable core or in a building aimed at seniors or students, the market can respond to that reality.

We now have evidence. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and later research found that developers did not abandon parking altogether; they built less where it made sense and more housing. Minneapolis, Anchorage, Edmonton and Auckland have done the same, with Auckland doing so across an entire country's largest metro. Many other cities have abolished mandates downtown or near transit. None has reported the catastrophe that critics predicted. Parking did not vanish, and neighbourhoods did not seize up. What changed was that projects that were once illegal or unviable became possible.

There is also a fairness argument that rarely gets made. Mandates are a subsidy to drivers, paid by non-drivers. A young person who cannot afford a car, an older resident who has stopped driving, and a family sharing one vehicle are all charged for spaces they do not use. Bundling parking into rent is a hidden tax, and it hits lower-income households hardest, because they own fewer cars yet live in the housing that is most squeezed by the rules.

Then there is the shape of the city itself. Parking mandates spread buildings apart, because every structure needs a lot beside it. Distances grow, walking becomes unpleasant, transit loses riders, and driving becomes the only sensible choice, which in turn seems to justify more parking. It is a loop the rules create and then cite as proof of need. Breaking it does not require banning cars or forcing anyone to give them up. It requires only that we stop making one way of living compulsory by law.

The sensible objection is about transition: what about places with truly poor transit, or neighbourhoods already stressed? A cautious city can pair repeal with tools it should have anyway, such as priced curb parking, permit districts, and the option for parking maximums in dense areas. None of that requires keeping a blanket minimum. If a developer wants to include parking, nothing in repeal stops them. Rules should step in only when there is a demonstrable public harm, and the harm of a building with fewer spaces than a manual predicts is not one.

Cities spend enormous effort worrying about housing costs, congestion and climate goals, while keeping a rule that worsens all three. Repealing parking minimums is cheap, requires no new spending, and takes no one's choices away. It simply returns a decision to the people with the strongest reason to get it right. Cities should do it, and soon.
