Keep the Joins: Why Primary Schools Should Still Teach Joined-Up Handwriting

Every few years someone proposes that joined-up handwriting should go the way of the slate and the inkwell. The case sounds like common sense. Children will spend their working lives at keyboards and touchscreens, the school day is already overfull, and hours spent practising the diagonal join from an "a" to an "n" could go to coding, or reading, or simply more maths. Finland removed the requirement to teach cursive in 2016. The American Common Core standards of 2010 left it out altogether. Why should a British seven-year-old still be labouring over loops?

I think primary schools should keep teaching it. The reason is not nostalgia, and it is not that joined writing is beautiful, though it can be. It is that fluent handwriting remains one of the cheapest and most reliable supports for learning to write well, and that joining letters is, for most children, the natural route to that fluency. The argument for dropping it rests on a mistake about what handwriting is for.

Start with what is not in dispute. Writing by hand matters in the early years in a way that typing does not replace. When young children form letters themselves, rather than selecting them from a keyboard, they learn to recognise those letters better. Brain-imaging work by Karin James and colleagues found that pre-literate children who had practised drawing letters freehand showed reading-related brain activity when they later saw those letters, while children who had typed or traced them did not. The explanation is intuitive. To write a "b" you have to build it, stroke by stroke, and the building teaches you what makes it a "b" and not a "d". Pressing a key teaches you where the key is.

The second established point concerns what happens when handwriting is slow. Composing a sentence is a juggling act. A child has to hold an idea in mind, choose words, spell them, punctuate them and physically get them onto the page. Working memory is limited, and whatever is not automatic takes a share of it. Decades of research by Virginia Berninger, Steve Graham and others have shown that children whose handwriting is laboured write less and write worse, and that the problem is not a lack of ideas. Their attention is being spent on the letters. When handwriting instruction makes transcription automatic, the quality of composition improves. Handwriting fluency is a bottleneck, and widening it releases everything queued up behind.

So the real question is not whether children should write by hand. Almost nobody seriously argues that five-year-olds should learn their letters exclusively on tablets. The question is whether, having learned to print, they should then be taught to join. Here the evidence is thinner, and honesty requires saying so. Studies comparing print and cursive directly are few, and they do not show cursive to be a cognitive miracle. Claims that joined writing uniquely wires the brain, or cures dyslexia, run well ahead of the data.

But the modest case is strong enough. The purpose of joining is speed with legibility. Lifting the pen between every letter costs time, and a child who prints each letter as a separate object tends either to stay slow or to speed up into a scrawl. Joins are simply what efficient handwriting looks like: the pen travels to where the next letter starts, and it may as well leave a line on the way. This is why nearly every adult who writes much by hand ends up joining at least some letters, whether they were taught to or not. One well-known study by Graham, Berninger and Weintraub found that the fastest writers among older pupils were those using a mixed style, joining where it helped and lifting where it did not. That finding is sometimes cited against teaching cursive. It is actually an argument for it. You cannot develop an efficient personal hand that uses joins selectively if nobody has ever shown you how to join. The children who mix styles well are drawing on a repertoire that someone taught them.

There is a secondary benefit worth noting. Joined writing treats the word as a unit. The pen moves through "said" as one continuous gesture, and many teachers find that this helps spelling, because the hand learns common letter strings as movements. The research base here is suggestive, not conclusive, but it fits what we know about motor memory, and it costs nothing extra once joins are being taught anyway.

Now consider the objections.

The first is that the keyboard has made all this irrelevant. It has not, and least of all in schools. Pupils in England still sit almost all their public examinations by hand: several hours of continuous writing per paper, repeated across a dozen or more papers. A sixteen-year-old who writes slowly or illegibly is at a real disadvantage that has nothing to do with what they know. Perhaps exams should move on screen, and perhaps one day they will, but primary schools must prepare children for the system that exists. Beyond exams, handwriting persists for note-taking, annotation, planning, forms, cards and the countless moments when a pen is nearer than a device. The research on whether handwritten notes beat typed ones is contested, and I would not lean on it heavily, but even the sceptical reading is that handwriting is no worse for learning, and a pupil who can do both has more options than one who can do only one.

The second objection is opportunity cost. Time is finite, so every minute on handwriting is a minute not spent on something else. This is true and should be taken seriously, but look at the actual sums. Effective handwriting instruction is short and frequent: ten or fifteen minutes a day for a couple of years, tapering off once the hand is automatic. In exchange, every subsequent writing task in every subject gets easier. Few things on the timetable offer a better return. It is also a false choice to set handwriting against typing. Children should be taught to touch-type as well, properly, and most schools do this badly or not at all. The answer to that is to teach typing, probably in the later primary years, not to abandon the pen.

The third objection is that joined writing is hard on some children, particularly those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties, and that insisting on it causes misery for little gain. This objection has force against how joined writing is sometimes taught and assessed, and less against teaching it at all. Some children find joining easier than printing, because a continuous movement removes the repeated problem of where to put the pen down next, and because joined words are clearly separated from one another. Others do find it harder, and for them the sensible response is flexibility: a clear print hand, or a keyboard, with no penalty. Teaching something to everyone does not require demanding identical results from everyone.

That points to where the critics are right. In England, the statutory assessment framework has at times made joined handwriting a condition of reaching the higher standard in writing at age eleven, so that a child with a vivid, well-argued piece of prose could be marked down for unjoined letters. That confuses the means with the end. Handwriting serves composition and should not be allowed to gatekeep it. Equally misguided are school policies that insist on elaborate looped scripts with lead-in strokes from the first day of Reception, before children can reliably form the letters at all. Schools have many scripts to choose from, and they should favour a plain one whose joined forms differ little from its printed forms, since children can pick that up quickly and it stays legible when they write fast.

So the position I am defending is specific. Teach children to print first, and teach it well. Once letter formation is secure, usually around age six or seven, teach a simple joined style in short daily sessions. Aim for speed and legibility, not uniformity or ornament. Let children's hands settle into personal styles as they mature, and excuse those for whom joining is a real obstacle. Teach typing alongside.

The cultural arguments, that children should be able to read their grandmother's letters or sign their names with a flourish, are pleasant but secondary. The central argument is practical. Writing is thinking made visible, and the child whose hand keeps pace with their thoughts can attend to the thoughts. Joined-up handwriting is a small, old technology for achieving that. It takes a few minutes a day for a few years, requires nothing but paper and a pencil, and goes on paying for itself until the last exam is handed in, and often long after. Schools that drop it will save very little, and their pupils will pay for the saving in slower, more effortful writing for years afterwards.
