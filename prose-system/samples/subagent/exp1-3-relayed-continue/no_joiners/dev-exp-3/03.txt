How Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

The puzzle

Imagine you and a stranger want to share a secret number, but every message between you passes through a crowded room where anyone can listen. You cannot meet first to whisper it. You cannot post a key in advance. If the number is said aloud, everyone hears it, so the task sounds impossible. Yet every time you shop online or send a private message, your devices solve exactly this problem in a fraction of a second. This article explains how, using nothing harder than the maths you met at school.

The central idea is a kind of job that is easy to do in one direction and brutally hard to undo. Once you see how such a job works, the rest follows naturally.

Easy one way, hard the other way

Think about mixing paint. Stir yellow and blue together and you get green in seconds. Now hand someone a pot of green and ask exactly which yellow and which blue went in. They are stuck, because mixing is easy and unmixing is hopeless.

Maths has its own versions of this. Multiplying two large prime numbers is quick. Take 101 and 103 and you get 10,403 with a little effort. But give a stranger 10,403 and ask for the two primes behind it, and they must hunt through possibilities one at a time. With numbers several hundred digits long, every computer on Earth working together would not finish in the lifetime of the universe.

The method described below uses a different one way job, built from powers and a special kind of counting.

Counting on a clock

Picture a clock face with 23 positions numbered 0 to 22. When you count past 22 you wrap around to 0, just as 13 o'clock wraps around to 1 on an ordinary clock. So 23 becomes 0, 25 becomes 2, and 30 becomes 7. In other words, you divide by 23 and keep only the remainder. Mathematicians call this working modulo 23.

Next, recall powers. Five to the power of 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. On our clock, 125 is five full laps of 23, which is 115, plus 10 left over. So the result is 10.

Here is the key observation. If I tell you the starting number is 5, the power is 3 and the clock has 23 positions, you can find 10 easily. But suppose I only tell you that 5 raised to some mystery power landed on 8 and ask for the power. Because the clock keeps wrapping around, the answers jump about with no visible pattern. You cannot nudge your guess up or down and see whether you are getting warmer. You have to try powers one after another. With a 23 position clock that takes moments. With a clock of several hundred digits, it can never be done in practice.

The paint mixing story

Now we can build the secret sharing trick. Call the two people Alice and Bob, and call the listener in the crowded room Eve, short for eavesdropper.

First, Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers. They are the starting number 5 and the clock size 23. Eve hears both and that is fine, because these are like a shared tin of base paint that everyone can see.

Second, each person privately picks a secret number and never says it aloud. Alice picks 6. Bob picks 15.

Third, each person raises the starting number to the power of their own secret, using the clock. Alice works out 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625. Dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8, so her result is 8. Bob works out 5 to the power 15 on the clock and gets 19. This step is the mixing of their private colour into the shared paint.

Fourth, they swap results in public. Alice announces 8 and Bob announces 19. Eve hears both.

Fifth, each person takes the other's announced number and raises it to the power of their own secret. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to the power 6. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to the power 15. Remarkably, both land on the same answer, which is 2. That number is now their shared secret, and it never travelled across the room.

Why both people get the same answer

This is not a coincidence, and the reason is pleasantly simple. Alice effectively computes 5 to the power of 15 and then raises that to the power of 6. Bob computes 5 to the power of 6 and then raises that to the power of 15. Remember the school rule that raising a power to another power means multiplying the exponents. Either way, the final result is 5 to the power of 6 times 15, which is 5 to the power of 90, on the clock. Multiplication does not care about order, so both arrive at the same place.

In paint terms, both pots end up holding the same three colours, base plus Alice plus Bob.

Why Eve cannot copy them

Eve knows the starting number 5, the clock size 23, and the two announced numbers 8 and 19. What she lacks is either private secret. To get the shared answer she would need to find Alice's 6 or Bob's 15, which means solving the hard unmixing problem from earlier. With our toy clock she could just try every power and succeed in minutes. That is why real systems never use a clock this small.

Real versions use clocks with 600 or more digits, and private numbers of similar size. Alice and Bob's devices still calculate quickly thanks to clever shortcuts, while Eve has no shortcut for unmixing.

Locks and padlocks, the second big idea

The method above lets two people agree on a secret. A close cousin of the idea lets people send messages directly, and it is the reason the field is called public key cryptography.

Imagine Bob manufactures thousands of open padlocks and leaves them on a public table, while keeping the only key to himself. Anyone, including a stranger like Alice, can drop a message in a box and snap one of his padlocks shut. Nobody but Bob can open it, not even Alice who locked it. The padlock is the public key. The matching key in Bob's pocket is the private key. In real systems the padlock is a pair of large numbers linked by the hard factoring job from earlier, which makes shutting the lock easy and opening it without the key hopeless.

One remaining danger

There is a catch worth knowing. If Eve can do more than listen, and can intercept and replace messages, she could pretend to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, agreeing a separate secret with each. Public numbers alone cannot prevent this. To solve it, websites carry digital certificates, which are like passports stamped by trusted authorities, so your device can check it is really talking to the right party before it begins.

The big picture

Two strangers can agree on a secret in public because they never send the secret. They send only a mixture that is easy to make and impossible to unmix, and each combines the other's mixture with a private ingredient only they know. Because the order of multiplication does not matter, both arrive at the same answer while the listener is left holding an unsolvable puzzle. That small piece of clock arithmetic quietly protects most of what you do online.
