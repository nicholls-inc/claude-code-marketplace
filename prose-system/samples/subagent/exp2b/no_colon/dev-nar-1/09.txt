In late August 1854, a terrible sickness fell upon Soho, a crowded district of London. The disease was cholera, which causes violent vomiting and diarrhea, drains the body of fluid, and can kill within a day or two. Within about ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few streets of one another had died. Many families fled, and shops stood empty.

At the time, almost everyone of standing believed cholera spread through "miasma," meaning foul air rising from rotting waste and filthy ground. The idea was reasonable on its face, since London stank, and the worst outbreaks did seem to strike the dirtiest neighborhoods. A physician named John Snow was not convinced.

Snow, then in his early forties, was a respected doctor who had pioneered the use of anesthesia, and he had attended the birth of a royal child with chloroform. He had also been thinking about cholera for years. During an earlier epidemic in 1848 and 1849, he had noticed something that miasma could not easily explain. Cholera began with symptoms of the gut, not the lungs. If the poison were breathed in, he reasoned, the illness ought to start in the chest or throat. Instead, he suspected that people swallowed something, and that the cause passed from the sick to the healthy through contaminated water or food. He had published a short pamphlet saying so, and most of his colleagues had ignored it.

Now the outbreak was happening almost on his doorstep. Snow lived and worked nearby, and he set out to test his idea. He did not have a microscope powerful enough to see the real culprit, because bacteria were not yet known to cause disease. What he had was a method. He would find out exactly where the dead had lived and what they had in common.

He obtained the death records from the local registrar and began walking the streets, knocking on doors and asking questions. As the names and addresses piled up, a pattern emerged. The deaths clustered tightly around the water pump on Broad Street. Most of the households where people had died were closer to that pump than to any other.

The exceptions were just as revealing. A workhouse nearby housed hundreds of poor residents, yet very few of them died. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street, whose workers fell sick in remarkably small numbers, also had its own supply. The brewers, it turned out, mostly drank the beer. In the other direction, Snow heard of a woman living far away in Hampstead who died of cholera after drinking Broad Street water. She had liked its taste so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her every day. Her niece, who drank the same water, died as well.

By the first week of September, Snow was confident. On the evening of the seventh, he went before the parish board of guardians, the local officials in charge of the neighborhood, and urged them to act. His argument was simple and practical. Take the handle off the pump, so nobody could draw water from it. The officials were skeptical, but they agreed, and the handle was removed the next day.

It is tempting to say the epidemic ended at that moment, but the truth is messier. The outbreak had already been fading, since many residents had fled and others had already died. Skeptics later pointed to this as evidence that the pump handle mattered little. Even so, Snow's detective work did not rest on the handle alone.

He kept investigating, and the Reverend Henry Whitehead, a local clergyman who began as a doubter, became his ally. Whitehead knew the neighborhood and its people intimately, and he tracked down details Snow could not. Together they uncovered what is probably the origin of the outbreak. A baby girl living at 40 Broad Street had died of a diarrheal illness just before the epidemic began. Her mother had washed the infant's soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool near the house. The cesspool's lining was decayed, and it leaked toward the well beneath the pump. When workers dug up the area, they found the brickwork broken, with waste seeping through.

Snow also drew a map. He marked each death with a small black bar stacked at the address where it occurred, and the bars piled up thickly around the pump. The map is now one of the most famous images in the history of public health, though its real power lay in the careful reasoning behind it.

The establishment did not immediately yield. An official inquiry concluded that the evidence did not support Snow's theory, and the miasma idea survived for years. Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, without seeing his view accepted. Only later, as germ theory took hold and cholera bacteria were identified, was he fully vindicated. Today he is remembered as a founder of modern epidemiology, the science of how diseases spread through populations, because he showed that careful counting and honest questioning can reveal what theory alone cannot.
