Apollo 13: The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 13, 1970, Apollo 13 was nearly 56 hours into its flight and about 200,000 miles from Earth. The mission was meant to be the third lunar landing. Its commander, Jim Lovell, was making his fourth spaceflight; Fred Haise was to walk on the Moon with him, and Jack Swigert, a late substitute for Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles, was to wait in orbit aboard the command module, Odyssey. The crew had just finished a television broadcast that none of the major American networks carried. Moon flights had already begun to seem routine.

Minutes later, mission control asked Swigert to perform a housekeeping chore: switch on the fans that stirred the supercold oxygen in the service module's tanks. Inside oxygen tank 2, wiring whose insulation had been damaged weeks before, during ground tests, sparked. The tank burst. The crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights came on across the instrument panel.

"Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Lovell repeated it a moment later: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first, controllers in Houston suspected faulty instruments, because the readings made no sense together. Then Lovell looked out a window and reported that the ship was venting something into space. It was oxygen. The explosion had destroyed tank 2 and damaged tank 1, which was now leaking away. That oxygen was not only what the crew breathed. It fed the three fuel cells that produced the command module's electricity and water. Two of the fuel cells were already dead, and the third would die when the oxygen ran out. Within a couple of hours, Odyssey would be a dark, powerless shell.

The landing was off. The question now was whether three men could be brought back alive.

The answer lay in the lunar module, Aquarius, still docked to the nose of the command module. It had its own oxygen, power, and engine, and it had been built to keep two men alive on the Moon for about two days. It would now have to keep three men alive for four. As Odyssey's last power drained away, Lovell and Haise hurried to bring Aquarius to life, while Swigert shut down the command module, saving its small reentry batteries for the final hours of the flight. The guidance platform's alignment had to be copied by hand from one ship's computer to the other, with Houston checking Lovell's arithmetic.

In mission control, flight director Gene Kranz and his team weighed how to bring the crew home. Turning around immediately would require the service module's main engine, and no one knew whether the explosion had damaged it. They chose the slower, safer path: continue around the Moon and let its gravity sling the spacecraft back toward Earth, using the lunar module's descent engine for any maneuvers. About five hours after the explosion, a short burn put the ship back on a path that would return it to Earth. The crew passed behind the Moon, traveling farther from Earth than any humans before them, and looked down at the surface they would not touch. Two hours later, a second, longer burn sped them up, cutting about ten hours from the trip and moving the splashdown to the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships were waiting.

Then came the hard arithmetic of survival. Power was the first limit. To stretch the lunar module's batteries, nearly everything was turned off, including the guidance computer and the cabin heaters. Water was the second. The lunar module used it to cool its electronics, so the astronauts cut their drinking to about six ounces a day each, a fraction of what they needed. All three became dehydrated, and Haise developed a urinary tract infection that left him feverish.

The cold was constant. With the electronics off, the cabin temperature fell to around 38 degrees Fahrenheit. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men had no cold-weather clothing and slept poorly, in short stretches, in the dark and clammy command module they called the refrigerator.

The most urgent threat was the air itself. Each breath the crew exhaled added carbon dioxide to the cabin, and the lunar module's chemical scrubbers, canisters of lithium hydroxide, were sized for two men for two days. The command module had plenty of fresh canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. As the carbon dioxide level climbed toward the danger point, engineers in Houston worked out a fix using only items they knew were on board: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight plan cover, a spacesuit hose, and duct tape. They read the assembly instructions up to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the contraption, which they called the mailbox, and the carbon dioxide level began to drop.

Navigation posed its own trouble. The spacecraft was drifting off course, pushed slowly by venting from the lunar module's cooling system. A correction burn was needed, but the guidance computer was powered down. So the crew steered by eye. Lovell kept the Earth centered in his window, using the line between its day and night sides as a reference, while Haise watched for sideways drift and Swigert timed the 14-second burn with his wristwatch. It worked.

Meanwhile, in Houston, controllers faced a problem no one had planned for: how to wake up a command module that had been shut down cold in flight, using only the limited power in its reentry batteries. Ken Mattingly, the astronaut bumped from the crew, who never did come down with measles, spent hours in a simulator with engineers working out the sequence of switches. The procedure they produced ran to many pages, and Swigert copied it down by hand as it was read up to him. No one knew whether the condensation soaking the instrument panels would cause short circuits when the power came on. It did not. Odyssey woke up.

On the morning of April 17, as Earth filled the windows, the crew cast off the damaged service module and saw it for the first time. An entire panel had been blown off its side, from the engine bell to the base of the command module. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. The sight raised a new fear: the heat shield, which sat right against the service module, might have been cracked by the blast. There was nothing anyone could do about it.

The crew then said goodbye to the lifeboat that had saved them. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," mission control radioed as the lunar module drifted away.

Reentry always brings a radio blackout, as superheated gas surrounds the capsule. It normally lasted about four minutes. This time, the silence stretched more than a minute longer, while controllers and a worldwide audience waited. Then Swigert's voice came through, and television cameras picked up the capsule under its three parachutes. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific, near the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, almost six days after launch. All three men were alive, exhausted and thinner, and Haise was ill, but they were safe.

NASA called Apollo 13 a "successful failure." It never reached the Moon, but the crew came home.
