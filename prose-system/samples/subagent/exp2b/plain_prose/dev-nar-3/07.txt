On January 15, 1919, a mild winter afternoon in Boston's crowded North End turned into one of the strangest disasters in American history. Towering above the neighborhood, near the waterfront at Commercial Street, stood a steel tank fifty feet high and nearly ninety feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses, a thick syrup brought up from the Caribbean and destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions during the war.

The tank had been built quickly in 1915, and it had never been treated with much care. The company's officials had not hired an engineer to review the design, and the steel plates were thinner than they should have been. Residents had long noticed that the tank groaned and leaked, with brown syrup oozing down its sides. Rather than repair it, the company is said to have painted the tank brown to hide the stains. Children from the neighborhood sometimes collected the dribbling molasses in tin cans to take home.

The day was unusually warm for January, after a bitterly cold spell, and a fresh shipment of warmer molasses had recently been pumped into the tank. Around half past noon, as workers took their lunch and families went about their errands, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a rumbling sound and a series of sharp cracks like machine-gun fire, as rivets shot out of the steel. Then the whole structure burst, and a wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen feet high at its crest, swept through the streets at tremendous speed.

The wave tore a firehouse from its foundations, crushed wagons and horses, and flung debris in every direction. It bent the supports of the elevated railway, nearly toppling a train. Buildings splintered. People caught in the flood found themselves unable to move, pinned in a sticky mass that grew heavier as it cooled in the winter air. Twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty more were injured. Rescuers waded in to reach survivors, among them soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship, and Red Cross workers who set up a makeshift hospital. The search for victims continued for days, and the harbor stayed brown for months afterward.

The aftermath turned into a landmark legal fight. The company insisted that anarchists had blown up the tank, a theory that played on the fears of the era. But a lawsuit led by attorney Hugh Ogden, with a court-appointed auditor hearing years of testimony, exposed the shoddy construction and the lack of proper testing. In 1925 the company was found responsible, and it ultimately paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements. The case helped push states toward requiring that engineers and architects approve building plans, and it became a quiet milestone in the development of modern safety regulation.

Locals say that on hot summer days, the North End still carries a faint, sweet smell of molasses.
