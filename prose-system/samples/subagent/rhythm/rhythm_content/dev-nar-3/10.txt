On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unseasonably mild, with temperatures near forty degrees after a bitter cold snap, and the neighborhood's mostly Italian and Irish working families were going about a midday that included lunch, errands, and children playing near the waterfront. Looming over Commercial Street stood a steel tank fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and holding about 2.3 million gallons of molasses that had arrived by ship from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a business made more urgent by the approach of Prohibition and by wartime demand for munitions. The tank had been built hastily in 1915, its walls thinner than any careful engineer would have specified, and nobody had properly tested it; when it was first filled it groaned and leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the syrup in cups, and the company's answer was to paint the tank brown so the stains would not show. At about 12:30 the tank failed with a sound witnesses compared to a machine gun, as rivets shot out of the steel, and then the whole structure split, releasing a wave of molasses that by most accounts rose to around fifteen feet at its crest and moved at perhaps thirty-five miles an hour, a speed that nobody on the street could have outrun and that the cold temperature of the day, which should have thickened the syrup, did not slow nearly enough. The wave crushed buildings, lifted a train off its elevated tracks, tore the supports from the structure that carried them, and swept up horses, wagons, and people alike, killing twenty-one and injuring about a hundred and fifty more; among the dead were children, a number of dock workers, and firemen whose station house was knocked from its foundation and collapsed on the men inside. Rescuers arrived from the nearby Navy ship and from the Red Cross, wading chest-deep into a sticky mass that cooled and stiffened as it sat, trapping survivors and making the work of finding the living painfully slow, and the recovery of bodies went on for days, with some victims so thoroughly coated that they could be identified only with difficulty. Cleanup crews used saltwater pumped from the harbor, and sand spread over the streets, and the harbor itself ran brown until summer, while residents claimed that the neighborhood smelled of molasses for decades afterward on warm days. The legal aftermath was among the most consequential parts of the story, because the company insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank, an assertion that echoed the fears of a nation shaken by recent labor unrest, yet a lawsuit that became one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts, heard by an auditor named Hugh Ogden over several years and drawing on testimony from some three thousand witnesses, concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, that the company had cut corners, and that no outside sabotage was required to explain the collapse. The company paid roughly 600,000 dollars in settlements to victims and families, an amount that worked out to a small sum per death, but the ruling pushed Massachusetts and eventually other states to require that architects and engineers formally certify their plans, and so a day of sweetness turned to disaster became a quiet turning point in how American buildings and industrial structures were designed and approved.
