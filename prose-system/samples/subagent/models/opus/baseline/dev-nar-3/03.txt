The Great Molasses Flood

January 15, 1919, was an unusually mild day in Boston. After a bitter cold spell, the temperature had climbed past 40 degrees. In the North End, a crowded waterfront neighborhood of Italian and Irish immigrants, people were outside enjoying it. Children collected firewood, laborers ate lunch near the docks, and freight wagons rattled along Commercial Street.

Above it all stood a huge steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It was about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The molasses was fermented into industrial alcohol, which was used to make munitions, among other things. Neighbors had long complained that the tank leaked. Brown syrup oozed down its seams, and some families collected the drips to take home. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown.

Shortly after half past noon, witnesses heard a deep rumble, followed by sharp cracks that some compared to machine-gun fire as rivets burst from the steel. Then the tank split open.

A wave of molasses, by some accounts 25 feet high, rolled into the streets at roughly 35 miles an hour. It was far heavier than water, and it crushed whatever it hit. It knocked buildings off their foundations and snapped the steel supports of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue. A firehouse was shoved toward the harbor. Horses, wagons, and people were swept away. A train on the elevated line stopped just short of a collapsed section of track.

The wave was only the start of the disaster. As the molasses spread out and cooled in the winter air, it grew thicker. People caught in it could barely move, and the more they struggled, the deeper they sank. Rescuers waded in waist-deep, among them sailors from a training ship docked nearby, as well as police, firefighters, and Red Cross volunteers. Many victims were so coated in syrup that it was hard to recognize them. Some had suffocated before anyone could reach them.

Twenty-one people died, including several children, and about 150 were injured. Rescuers kept searching for days. Some bodies were not found until months later, when they surfaced in the harbor. The cleanup took weeks. Crews used salt water from fireboats to break up the hardened syrup and sand to soak it up. The harbor stayed brown well into the summer, and longtime residents said that for decades the neighborhood smelled of molasses on hot days.

United States Industrial Alcohol at first blamed anarchists, claiming that a bomb had destroyed the tank. Survivors and the city did not accept that explanation. More than a hundred lawsuits were combined into one of the first class-action cases in Massachusetts history. After years of testimony, a court-appointed auditor concluded in 1925 that the tank had been built in a hurry, had never been properly tested, and was too weak for the load it carried. The man in charge of building it had no engineering training. The company paid damages of about $628,000.

The ruling helped push Massachusetts and other states to require that engineers and architects sign off on their designs. Today a small plaque in a waterfront park marks the spot where an ordinary sweetener once flooded a neighborhood.
