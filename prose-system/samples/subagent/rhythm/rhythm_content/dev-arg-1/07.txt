Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon, because the rules force every new building to subsidize car storage whether or not anyone who will live or work there owns a car. A parking minimum is a number written into the zoning code, say one and a half spaces per apartment or one for every three hundred square feet of retail, and a developer who cannot meet it cannot build. The number is usually borrowed from a manual of suburban traffic counts taken at sites with free parking and no alternatives, which means the formula was calibrated to the most car-dependent places in the country and then applied to downtowns, transit corridors and old neighborhoods where the same assumptions do not hold.

The first cost is financial and it lands on housing. A single structured parking space commonly costs tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground space can cost far more, so a requirement of one space per unit adds a sum to every apartment that rivals a good used car. Developers do not absorb that sum out of goodwill. They pass it into rents, they shrink the number of homes a lot can hold, or they abandon marginal projects entirely, and the households who pay are disproportionately those who own fewer cars and would happily have skipped the garage. A tenant who gets by on a bus pass and a bicycle is paying for the spot that a neighbor with two cars requires, and the law has arranged this without anyone ever deciding it was fair.

The second cost is spatial. Surface lots and garages consume land that could hold homes, shops and workplaces, and when a city mandates that much of it, the buildings spread out, the distances between them grow, and walking becomes less pleasant and less useful. Anyone who has crossed a wide lot to reach a store set back behind it has felt the effect. Each added space pushes destinations farther apart, which makes driving more necessary, which seems to justify the next round of requirements. The rule manufactures the dependence it claims merely to accommodate.

The standard defense is that without minimums, new buildings will flood the street with parked cars and neighbors will suffer. This deserves a serious answer, because spillover is real in some places and at some times. But the response to a scarce public resource is to price it, not to require every private building to oversupply its own. Cities that charge for on-street parking, set the price to keep a space or two open on each block, and return some of the revenue to the neighborhood remove most of the conflict, since the curb stops being a free-for-all and begins to behave like any other thing people value. Residential permit programs can protect existing residents while still letting new buildings choose how much to provide. These tools exist and many cities already use them, and they address the actual problem, which is a free good being overused, rather than the invented one of too few mandated spaces.

It also helps to be clear about what repeal does not mean. Removing a minimum does not ban parking, and it does not instruct anyone to build without it. Lenders, tenants, grocery chains and suburban office tenants will continue to ask for parking where they believe customers drive, and developers will continue to supply it, often in generous amounts. What changes is that the decision moves from a legislative formula to the people who bear the cost and know the site. A builder near a rail station can offer fewer spaces and cheaper rents, a builder on a highway frontage can offer many, and the market can learn which mix works. The evidence from places that have tried it is encouraging: Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, Minneapolis and Seattle followed with major reforms, and cities from Edmonton to Auckland have since done the same. Developers in those places have mostly kept building parking where it pays and skipped it where it does not, and the sky has stayed where it was.

There is a fairness argument too, one that is easy to miss because the status quo looks like neutral infrastructure. A minimum is a decision that cars are the default and every other way of living is an exception that must apply for a variance. Older neighborhoods built before these rules, with narrow lots, corner stores and apartments above shops, are often the places people most want to live, yet in many cities it is now illegal to build them again. Parking reform is one of the cheapest ways to make them legal, because it costs the treasury nothing and asks only that the city stop prohibiting what it should be welcoming.

The remaining objection is about transition, and it is fair to worry that a sudden change could strand a few neighborhoods without a plan for the curb. That is a reason to pair repeal with pricing and with good transit, not a reason to keep the rule. Cities can phase in changes, begin with areas well served by buses and trains, and watch the results. But the direction is clear enough. A requirement that raises rents, wastes land, entrenches driving and rests on decades-old guesses has no claim to permanence. Let cities strike the numbers from the code, price the curb honestly, and trust builders and residents to decide how many spaces a building actually needs.
