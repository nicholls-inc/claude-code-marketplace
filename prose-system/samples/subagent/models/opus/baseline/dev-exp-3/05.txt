How two strangers can share a secret while everyone listens

Imagine you want to send a private message to someone you have never met. You can only talk to them by shouting across a crowded room, and anyone in the room can hear every word. You have no chance to meet first and whisper a password. This seems impossible. Whatever you say to agree on a secret, the eavesdroppers hear it too, so how can it stay secret?

For most of history people thought it was impossible. Every secret code relied on the two people already sharing something, such as a codebook, a key word or a setting on a cipher machine, which had to be handed over in person or by a trusted messenger. In the 1970s, mathematicians found a way around this. The idea is called public-key cryptography, and it protects almost everything you do online today: shopping, banking and messaging.

The trick rests on one simple observation: some things are easy to do but very hard to undo.

**Easy one way, hard the other**

Think about mixing paint. If I give you a pot of yellow and a pot of blue, you can stir them into green in seconds. But if I give you a pot of green and ask you to separate it back into exactly the yellow and blue that went in, you cannot. The mixing is easy and the unmixing is, in practice, impossible.

Mathematicians look for calculations that behave like this. They are quick to work out forwards and hopelessly slow to work backwards. Public-key cryptography is built on them.

**The paint version of the trick**

Here is the whole idea with paint, before any numbers. Call the two people Alice and Bob, and call the eavesdropper Eve.

1. Alice and Bob agree, out loud, on a common starting colour, say yellow. Eve hears this. That is fine.
2. Alice secretly picks her own private colour, say red, and tells nobody. Bob secretly picks his own, say blue.
3. Alice mixes her red into some yellow and gets orange. Bob mixes his blue into some yellow and gets green.
4. They swap these mixtures in public. Alice sends orange to Bob, and Bob sends green to Alice. Eve sees both pots.
5. Alice takes Bob's green and stirs in her secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange and stirs in his secret blue.

Now look at what each person ends up with. Alice has yellow + blue + red. Bob has yellow + red + blue. They contain the same ingredients, so they are the same colour, a muddy brown. That brown is their shared secret.

What does Eve have? She has yellow, orange and green. To make the brown she would need either Alice's red or Bob's blue on their own, and those were never sent. She could try mixing orange and green together, but that gives yellow + red + yellow + blue, which has too much yellow and comes out a different shade. To get the right brown she would have to unmix the orange to pull the red out, and unmixing is the thing nobody can do.

That is the entire idea. Each person keeps one ingredient private, sends out a mixture that hides it, and adds their private ingredient at the end. Both arrive at the same result, and the listener cannot.

**Swapping paint for numbers**

Computers do not use paint, so we need a numerical version of easy-to-mix, hard-to-unmix. The one most often used is called *clock arithmetic*.

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 9 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 2 o'clock, not 14. When you go past 12 you wrap around and start again. In maths terms, you take the remainder after dividing by 12: 14 divided by 12 is 1 remainder 2, so the answer is 2.

We can do the same with any size of clock. Let us use a clock with 23 hours, numbered 0 to 22. To find where a number lands, divide it by 23 and keep the remainder. For example, 625 divided by 23 is 27 remainder 4, so 625 lands on 4.

The mixing step is this: take a starting number and multiply it by itself a secret number of times, that is, raise it to a power, then see where it lands on the clock.

**A worked example**

Alice and Bob agree in public on the clock size, 23, and a starting number, 5. These play the part of the yellow paint. Eve knows them.

Alice secretly picks 4. Bob secretly picks 3. These are their private colours.

Alice works out 5 to the power 4, which is 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 625. On the 23-hour clock, 625 lands on **4**. She sends 4 to Bob.

Bob works out 5 to the power 3, which is 125. Divided by 23 that is 5 remainder 10, so it lands on **10**. He sends 10 to Alice.

Eve has now heard 23, 5, 4 and 10.

Next, Alice takes Bob's 10 and raises it to her secret power 4: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. On the clock, 10,000 lands on **18**.

Bob takes Alice's 4 and raises it to his secret power 3: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. On the clock, 64 lands on **18**.

They both get 18, and neither of them ever said it out loud. That is their shared secret.

Why do they match? Alice effectively calculated 5 raised to 3, then raised to 4. Bob calculated 5 raised to 4, then raised to 3. Either way, 5 has been multiplied by itself 12 times. As with the paint, the same ingredients went in, just in a different order. (Wrapping around the clock part-way through does not change the final position, just as with a real clock.)

**Why Eve is stuck**

Eve knows that 5 to some secret power lands on 4 on a 23-hour clock. To find the shared secret she has to work out what that power was. This is the unmixing step.

With a clock of only 23 positions she could simply try every power until one works, which would take seconds. The protection comes from size. In real use the clock is not 23 but a number around 600 digits long, and the secret powers are just as enormous. Going forwards is still fast for a computer, because there are clever shortcuts for raising a number to a big power and wrapping it round a clock. Going backwards has no known shortcut. Wrapping around the clock scrambles the result so thoroughly that the answer gives almost no hint of the power that produced it. Trying every possibility would take every computer on Earth far longer than the age of the universe.

So the listener hears everything, and it does her no good.

**What happens next**

Once Alice and Bob share a secret number, they use it as the key for an ordinary lock-and-key code, the kind where the same key both scrambles and unscrambles a message. Those codes are very fast and very strong. Their only weakness was always getting the key to both people safely, and the method above solves exactly that.

The method described here was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, and it is known as Diffie-Hellman key exchange. A close cousin, called RSA, works like a padlock. You can hand out open copies of your padlock to anyone, and they can snap a message shut inside a box, but only you hold the key that opens it. Today's systems often use a variant based on curves rather than clocks, which does the same job with smaller numbers.

**One important catch**

Everything above assumes Alice really is talking to Bob. Suppose Eve does more than listen. If she can intercept the messages and reply herself, she can pretend to be Bob when talking to Alice and pretend to be Alice when talking to Bob, setting up a separate secret with each. The maths would still work, but with the wrong person.

To block this, the internet adds a system of digital signatures and certificates. These work like a trusted notary vouching that a particular public number really belongs to your bank and not an impostor. That small padlock symbol in your web browser means both parts have happened: the identity was checked, and then a secret was agreed in the open that nobody listening can work out.

**In one sentence**

Two people can agree on a secret in full public view because each keeps one private ingredient, shares only a mixture that hides it, and relies on a calculation that is easy to do and practically impossible to undo.
