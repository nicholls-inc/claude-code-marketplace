Cities should remove their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon.

Start with what a parking mandate actually is. It is a rule, written decades ago by traffic engineers working from thin data, that tells a developer how many spaces to build for every apartment, restaurant seat, or square foot of retail. The numbers often came from surveys of suburban sites with free parking and no alternatives, then hardened into law everywhere. The result is that city governments, which have no particular expertise in how many cars a given business needs, decide the answer for everyone and force the cost onto whoever builds.

That cost is large and mostly invisible. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles and ramps, which is land that could hold housing, a shop, or a park. A space in a structured garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and in an underground garage far more. Because the space must be built regardless of whether the future tenant owns a car, its cost is folded into the rent. Households without cars subsidize those with them. A person who bikes to work, takes the bus, or cannot afford a vehicle still pays for the concrete. Researchers who have studied this have found that mandates raise the price of housing and, in some places, make smaller and cheaper units impossible to build at all, because the parking eats the budget and the lot.

Mandates also quietly kill projects that would otherwise be good for the city. A small lot downtown might fit eight apartments, but not eight apartments plus the twelve spaces the code demands. So the lot stays a vacant patch or a single-story building. Old main streets are full of historic buildings that could never be built today, because no modern code would permit a structure covering its whole lot with no parking. Many cities have found their own beloved neighborhoods illegal. When a rule forbids what the city already loves, that is a hint the rule is the problem.

Defenders raise a fair worry: without mandates, won't people just crowd the streets with parked cars and spill into neighborhoods? This deserves a direct answer. First, removing a requirement is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it where customers want it, because a building with no parking in a car-dependent area will struggle to find tenants and lenders. The difference is that the decision moves to the people who bear the cost and know their market. Many suburban shopping centers and apartment complexes would keep building about as much parking as they do now. What changes is that the corner lot, the converted warehouse, and the infill project get to make a different choice.

Second, the spillover problem is real but solvable by pricing rather than mandate. The reason curbs overflow is that street parking is usually free or nearly so, and anything free is overused. Charge a sensible price for curb space, set so that a spot or two is usually open, and the scramble goes away. Cities can return the revenue to the neighborhood through benefit districts that pay for sidewalks, trees, and lighting, which turns a nuisance into something residents want. Residential permit programs can protect existing blocks. These tools address the actual issue, which is managing a scarce public resource, without forcing every builder to guess wrong in advance.

Cities that have gone first offer evidence. Buffalo became the first large American city to eliminate its citywide mandates in 2017, and its housing and commercial development did not collapse into a sea of unparked cars. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and many others have since scrapped or sharply reduced theirs. Where developers still wanted parking, they built it. Where they did not, they could finally build something modest and affordable that the old rules had blocked. California, among other states, has stepped in to bar mandates near transit, a sign that the old consensus has broken.

There is also a climate and fiscal argument that should appeal across the usual lines. Cars are the largest source of transportation emissions, and a city that guarantees abundant parking guarantees abundant driving. Meanwhile, parking lots produce almost no tax revenue per acre compared with buildings, so a downtown given over to asphalt is a downtown running a deficit. Dense, mixed development pays for its own streets and pipes in a way sprawl does not.

None of this requires hostility to drivers. People will keep driving, and many neighborhoods will keep wanting plenty of spaces. The point is that the number should come from the market and from good curb management, not from a formula copied out of a manual written for a different era. Removing minimum parking requirements lowers the cost of housing, frees land for better uses, legalizes the kind of places people actually like to visit, and lets cities respond as conditions change. A rule that makes everything more expensive while protecting almost no one is worth repealing.
