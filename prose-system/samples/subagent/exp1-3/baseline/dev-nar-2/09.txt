Apollo 13: The Accident and the Return

On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Cape Kennedy at 2:13 in the afternoon, Houston time. It was NASA's third planned mission to land men on the Moon, and by then the public had begun to treat such flights as routine. Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three spaceflights, was joined by Fred Haise, who would pilot the lunar module, and Jack Swigert. Swigert was a late replacement: Ken Mattingly had been grounded just days before launch after exposure to German measles, and Swigert, the backup, took his place. Television networks did not bother to carry the crew's broadcasts live. That would change within two days.

The first fifty-five hours were uneventful. The spacecraft, a conical command module named Odyssey joined to a service module and carrying the spindly lunar lander Aquarius, coasted toward the Moon. On the evening of April 13, the crew finished a short television broadcast that few networks aired, and Houston asked Swigert to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine step that agitated the supercold contents so the gauges would read accurately.

At 9:08 p.m. Houston time, Swigert flipped the switch. A little over ninety seconds later, a bang shook the ship. Lovell, floating in the lower equipment bay, felt it through the structure. Swigert reported the words that became famous in a slightly altered form: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, adding that they had a main bus B undervolt.

What had happened was rooted in a chain of small errors made years earlier. The oxygen tank had been dropped about two inches during handling at the factory, likely damaging an internal fill tube. Later, during ground testing, the tank's heaters had been run for hours at a higher voltage than their thermostatic switches were built for, fusing the switches shut and damaging the wire insulation. No one noticed. In flight, when Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark from the bare wiring ignited the insulation inside a tank full of liquid oxygen. Pressure surged, the tank ruptured, and the explosion blew off a panel of the service module and crippled the second tank beside it.

In Houston, flight controllers watched their screens fall apart. Oxygen pressure was dropping. Power was sagging. Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas venting into space and said so. The crew soon understood that they were losing the oxygen that fed their fuel cells, and with it their electricity and their drinking water. The command module's own batteries held only a few hours of power, and those were needed for reentry. Gene Kranz, leading the shift, and his team made a decision that ended any hope of landing on the Moon: the crew would move into the lunar module, a craft meant to hold two men for two days, and use it as a lifeboat.

The next question was how to get home. The spacecraft was on a path that would loop around the Moon and return to Earth only if a course correction was made, and the main engine in the damaged service module could not be trusted. Instead, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine. After the spacecraft swung around the far side of the Moon, far beyond the reach of any radio, they fired it again to speed the return. Lovell, Haise and Swigert had traveled farther from Earth than any humans before them, nearly 250,000 miles out.

Then the problems turned practical and grim. Aquarius's life support was built for a short stay, and three men were breathing in it. Carbon dioxide began to build, and the lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrub it from the air, were filling up. The command module had spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's receptacles were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the same items the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual cover, suit hoses, and duct tape. They devised a way to fit a square canister into a round hole and talked the astronauts through building it. The adapter worked, and the carbon dioxide level fell.

Cold became the other enemy. To conserve power, the crew shut down nearly everything. The cabin temperature dropped to around 40 degrees Fahrenheit, water condensed on the walls, and the men slept little. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day each, and the crew grew dehydrated. Meanwhile, the ship drifted slightly off course and needed another correction, which Lovell carried out by hand, steering by holding the Earth in a window, since the guidance system had been shut down to save power.

On the ground, a parallel struggle was under way. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, worked in a simulator to devise a procedure for restarting the command module in the right order using almost no power, because Odyssey's systems had to be brought back to life before reentry, and no one had ever planned to restart it from a cold, drained state. The procedure was written, tested, and read up to the crew, who copied it down word for word.

Late on April 16 and into the 17th, the spacecraft neared home. About five hours before reentry, the crew jettisoned the service module, and for the first time saw the damage. A whole panel was gone, and the side of the ship was scorched and ragged. Lovell described it to Houston, and photographs were taken. Then the crew moved back into Odyssey, powered it up, and let go of Aquarius, the little lander that had saved them. It burned up in the atmosphere over the Pacific.

Reentry carried one more uncertainty. The heat shield was believed to be intact, but no one could be sure. The time of the radio blackout, normally about three minutes, stretched past that limit. In Houston, controllers sat silent. Then, at about four minutes and then past, Swigert's voice came through: "OK, Joe." The three parachutes bloomed on the television pictures from recovery aircraft. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific, close to the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, at 12:07 p.m. Houston time on April 17, 1970, nearly six days after launch.

The crew were thinner, exhausted, and Haise was ill, but all three were alive. Investigators later traced the explosion to the faulty tank and its wiring, and NASA redesigned the tank, removed the fans, and added a third oxygen tank and a backup battery in the service module for future flights. The Apollo program continued, and astronauts walked on the Moon again the next year.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, yet it became known as a "successful failure." It showed that the people on the ground and in the spacecraft could improvise under pressure, with limited tools and no precedent. Lovell later said the crew had been lucky: the explosion had happened on the way out, when they still had the lunar module as a lifeboat, not on the way back, when it would have been gone. Whatever the proportion of luck and skill, three men came home from a journey that, for most of four days, looked as though it might not end well.
