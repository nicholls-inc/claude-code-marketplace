A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current, constant voltage, usually shortened to CC-CV. It has two phases, and the second is where the slowdown comes from.

In the first phase the charger forces a fixed current into the cell, often sized as a fraction of capacity per hour. For a 3 Ah cell, a 1C rate means 3 A. The cell voltage rises steadily while lithium ions leave the positive electrode (the cathode, typically a metal oxide), travel through the liquid electrolyte, and insert between the layers of the graphite negative electrode. This insertion is called intercalation. Most of the charge, commonly 70 to 80 percent, goes in during this phase, and the cell is fast to charge while it is far from full.

The charger cannot raise the current or voltage without limit. Each chemistry has a maximum voltage, about 4.2 V for common cobalt-based cells. Above it the electrolyte starts to oxidize, the cathode degrades, and lithium metal can plate onto the anode, which is a safety hazard. So once the terminal voltage reaches the limit, the charger switches to the second phase and holds the voltage constant. Current is now the free variable, and it decays on its own, roughly exponentially. The charge ends when current falls to a small cutoff, often about 5 percent of the original rate.

The decay has a simple electrical explanation. The voltage measured at the terminals is the cell's open-circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance. Open-circuit voltage rises as the cell fills, because it reflects the chemical potential difference between the two electrodes. Holding terminal voltage fixed, any rise in open-circuit voltage leaves less voltage available to drive current through the resistance. The driving term shrinks, so current must fall. An engineer might picture a capacitor charging through a resistor from a fixed supply, though a cell is not linear and its open-circuit voltage depends on state of charge.

There is a transport explanation too. Near full, the graphite has few vacant sites left, so lithium arriving at the surface has fewer places to go. Diffusion inside solid particles is slow, and a concentration gradient builds up between the surface and the core. The surface looks full while the core is still filling. Pushing harder would only pile up lithium at the surface and raise plating risk, so the voltage clamp enforces a gentle pace.

Temperature matters as well. Cold cells have higher resistance and slower diffusion, so they reach the voltage limit earlier and spend longer in the slow phase. This is why fast chargers favor warm cells, and why the last 20 percent takes disproportionately long.
