Boston's molasses flood was a disaster in January 1919 that killed 21 people and injured about 150.

On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusual thaw. The day before, the temperature had hovered near two degrees Fahrenheit. Now it had climbed to about forty, and people who had been huddled indoors came out to enjoy the sun. Dock workers ate lunch near the harbor. Children played in the street. Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, which was as tall as a five story building and about ninety feet across.

The tank held more than two million gallons of molasses, shipped up from the Caribbean. The molasses would be turned into industrial alcohol, much of it needed for munitions. The tank had been built quickly in 1915, and it had never been tested by filling it with water. It leaked from the start. Neighbors collected the sticky drips in cans, and rather than fix the problem, the company painted the tank brown so the stains would not show.

Shortly after noon, residents heard a low rumble. Witnesses later said rivets shot from the tank like machine gun fire. Then the seams tore open. A wave of molasses roughly twenty five feet high burst out and raced through the streets at an estimated thirty five miles an hour.

The wave crushed everything in its way. It knocked buildings off their foundations and tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports. A firehouse was shoved aside, and men inside were trapped in the wreckage. Wagons, horses and people were swept off their feet. The cold air made the molasses thicken quickly, and victims found themselves stuck in a substance that grew heavier as they struggled. Rescuers waded in waist deep. Soldiers, sailors and Red Cross nurses rushed to the scene, but pulling people out was agonizing work. Some of the dead were not found for days.

Cleanup took weeks. Fireboats pumped salt water over the streets, and workers spread sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown well into the summer. For decades afterward, locals insisted that the area still smelled sweet on hot days.

The aftermath was legal as well as physical. The company blamed anarchists and claimed the tank had been bombed, a theory fed by the fears of that postwar era. Residents did not accept this, and they brought one of the first major class action suits in the state. A court appointed auditor spent years hearing testimony from hundreds of witnesses. Engineering experts showed that the steel was too thin and the design too weak to hold such a load. In 1925 the company was found liable and paid out damages to victims and their families.

The disaster helped change how Americans built things. Stricter rules followed, requiring engineers to review and sign their plans and demanding that large structures be tested before use. The molasses flood remains a vivid reminder of what can happen when speed and profit outrun safety.
