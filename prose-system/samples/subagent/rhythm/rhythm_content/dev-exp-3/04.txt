Imagine two people, Amira in Leeds and Bilal in Lagos, who have never met and share no code book, and who want to agree on a secret number that they can then use to scramble their messages, even though every word either of them says on the way to agreeing it will be overheard by anyone who cares to listen. At first this looks impossible, since a secret that has to be announced in public is no longer a secret, and for most of history the only way to share a code was to meet in person or send a trusted courier carrying it. Public-key cryptography, worked out in the 1970s, gets round the problem by exploiting a lopsided fact about arithmetic: some calculations are quick to do forwards and hopelessly slow to undo, and if each person does one such calculation privately and then swaps the results in the open, both can finish with the same answer while a listener who has seen everything cannot.

The easiest way to see the shape of the idea is with paint rather than numbers. Suppose Amira and Bilal agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow, and anyone may know it. Amira privately picks a colour of her own, red, and mixes it into a big tub of the yellow; Bilal privately picks blue and does the same with his own tub of yellow. They post the two tubs to each other, and the eavesdropper can see both mixtures in transit. Amira now adds her private red to the tub that arrived from Bilal, and Bilal adds his private blue to the tub that came from Amira, so each of them has ended up with yellow, red and blue mixed together, and since the order of mixing does not change the final shade, their two tubs are the same colour. The eavesdropper holds yellow, yellow-and-red and yellow-and-blue, and could add something to either tub, but would have no way to remove the red from one or the blue from the other, because unmixing paint is the hard direction. Everything depends on that asymmetry, and the real version simply replaces paint with a kind of arithmetic that is just as easy to mix and just as hard to unmix.

The arithmetic in question is clock arithmetic. On an ordinary clock you add hours and then discard whole laps, so that ten hours after eight o'clock it is six o'clock, because eighteen minus twelve leaves six, and the same trick works with any size of clock, not just twelve. Mathematicians call the leftover after discarding whole laps the remainder, and for the cleanest behaviour they use a clock whose size is a prime number, one that divides evenly by nothing except itself and one. Take a clock with 23 marks on it. Raising a number to a power just means multiplying it by itself that many times, so five to the power of three is five times five times five, which is 125, and on the 23-mark clock that comes out as 10, because 125 is 5 laps of 23, which is 115, with 10 left over. Working out such a remainder from a base and a power is quick even when the numbers are enormous, since you can square repeatedly instead of multiplying one step at a time, but going the other way, being told that five to some unknown power leaves a remainder of 10 and being asked what the power was, has no shortcut that anyone has found once the clock is big enough. With 23 marks you could simply try every power in a minute, but with a clock of several hundred digits the number of possibilities exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe.

Here is the whole exchange with the small clock, so that you can check each step with a calculator. Amira and Bilal publicly agree on the clock size 23 and the base number 5, and these are the yellow of the paint story. Amira privately chooses 6 and computes five to the power of six, which is 15625, whose remainder on the 23-mark clock is 8, so she announces 8. Bilal privately chooses 15 and computes five to the power of fifteen, whose remainder on the same clock is 19, and he announces 19. Neither private number has left its owner's head, yet each now holds something the other can use. Amira takes Bilal's announced 19, raises it to her private power of 6, and takes the remainder, which is 2. Bilal takes Amira's announced 8, raises it to his private power of 15, and takes the remainder, which is also 2. They land on the same number because both calculations amount to raising 5 to the power of 6 times 15, and multiplication of the powers does not care which factor came first, just as the paint did not care which colour went in first. The shared secret is 2, and it was never transmitted.

Now consider what the listener has actually gathered, which is the clock size 23, the base 5, and the two announcements 8 and 19. To reach the secret she would need either Amira's 6 or Bilal's 15, and to find one of them she would have to work out which power of 5 leaves a remainder of 8, or of 19, on the 23-mark clock. With such small numbers she can try the powers one at a time and succeed almost at once, which is why this example is only a demonstration and not a protection. Real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits and private numbers of similar size, and then the same hunt through powers cannot finish in any realistic time, even with every computer on Earth working on it, while Amira and Bilal's own calculations still take a fraction of a second because of the repeated-squaring shortcut. The security does not come from hiding the method, which is published and studied by thousands of people, but from the gap in effort between doing the calculation and reversing it.

The name public-key cryptography comes from a close cousin of this exchange in which each person owns a pair of linked numbers, one that is published and one that is never shown to anyone. Anyone may use the published number to lock a message, much as anyone may snap shut an open padlock that you have left lying around for the purpose, but only the owner of the matching private number can open what was locked. Amira can therefore publish her padlock in a directory, Bilal can fetch it and use it to lock a message to her, and nobody who intercepts the locked message can read it. The padlock is built from the same kind of one-way arithmetic, in the best-known version from the fact that multiplying two huge primes together is easy while splitting the product back into its two primes is not.

In practice the two ideas are combined, because the heavy arithmetic is slow and is kept for the short opening exchange, after which the shared secret becomes the key to a much faster ordinary scrambling method that handles the actual conversation. When your phone shows a small padlock beside a website's address, an exchange of just this kind has taken place in the first fraction of a second, and you did not have to do anything.

There is one weakness the mathematics alone does not cure, and it is worth being clear about. A listener who only watches is helpless, but a listener who can intercept and replace messages could pose as Bilal to Amira and as Amira to Bilal, running one exchange with each and relaying everything between them while reading it all. To defeat this, the numbers people publish must be vouched for by someone both sides already trust, and this is the job of certificates, which are in effect public numbers signed by an authority whose own number is built into your device before you ever use it. Public-key cryptography therefore does not remove trust altogether, but it shrinks what has to be trusted from a courier for every pair of strangers to a small number of well-known signatures, and that is why two people who have never met can still agree a secret across a line that the whole world can hear.
