A lithium-ion cell is charged by forcing current through it in the direction opposite to discharge, which pushes lithium ions out of the positive electrode, across the electrolyte, and into the graphite of the negative electrode. The cell behaves roughly like a voltage source whose open-circuit voltage rises with its state of charge, in series with an internal resistance. Because the charger must exceed the open-circuit voltage to drive current, the terminal voltage during charging is the open-circuit voltage plus the current times the internal resistance, along with some dynamic overpotentials. For a typical cobalt-based cell, the open-circuit voltage climbs from about 3.0 V when empty to about 4.2 V when full, and the upper limit is a hard safety boundary that the charger must not exceed.

Most chargers therefore use a two-stage protocol called constant-current, constant-voltage. In the first stage the charger holds the current fixed, commonly near the one-hour rate, and lets the terminal voltage rise as the cell fills. This stage delivers the bulk of the energy, often around 70 to 80 percent of capacity, and ends when the terminal voltage reaches the 4.2 V ceiling. In the second stage the charger holds the voltage at that ceiling and lets the current fall on its own. Charging is declared complete when the current has tapered to a small fraction of its starting value, typically about a twentieth of the one-hour rate.

The taper arises because the voltage ceiling is applied across both the open-circuit voltage and the internal resistance. As the cell fills, its open-circuit voltage approaches the ceiling, leaving a shrinking voltage difference available to drive current through the resistance. By Ohm's law the current must fall in proportion, so the charging rate decays roughly exponentially. The charger cannot simply raise the voltage to keep the current high, because the extra potential would overcharge the surface of the particles while their interior is still partly empty.

That last point reflects a second cause, which is transport inside the electrode. Lithium must diffuse into the solid graphite particles, and diffusion in solids is slow. During fast charging the surface of each particle fills before the interior, so the surface reads as nearly full while the core lags behind. Holding the voltage constant lets the concentration gradient inside the particles relax gradually, and the current falls as the gradient flattens. Near full charge the graphite also has fewer vacant sites, which raises the effective resistance to further insertion.

The taper also protects the cell. Pushing high current into a nearly full anode risks plating metallic lithium on its surface, which permanently loses capacity and can form dendrites that cause short circuits. Slowing down at the end of charge is thus both a physical consequence of the cell's electrochemistry and a deliberate safeguard.
