Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules. The case does not rest on a belief that cars are bad or that people should give them up. It rests on the narrower point that a council is poorly placed to decide how many parking spaces a given shop, flat or restaurant needs, and that the rule it uses in place of that knowledge has costs it seldom counts.

Minimum parking requirements say that a developer must build a fixed number of spaces for each unit of a building. A typical American rule asks for one or two spaces per home, around one space for every 300 square feet of retail, and a set number for each seat in a restaurant. The numbers usually come from a manual of parking counts taken at suburban sites with ample free parking, where the observed peak demand became the legal minimum. Donald Shoup, the planning professor who wrote The High Cost of Free Parking in 2005, pointed out that these figures describe what people do when parking is free, and then require every new building to supply enough of it to stay free.

Parking costs money to build. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once aisles are included, which is land that could hold something else. A space in a multi-storey garage commonly costs in the region of $25,000 to $50,000 to build in the United States, and an underground space costs more. Those costs do not disappear when the rule requires the space. They are added to the price of the flat or the rent of the shop, and everyone who lives or shops there pays them, including households with no car. About one in ten American households has no car, and the share is far higher in some large cities. For them the rule is a charge for a service they do not use.

The rule also shapes what gets built. On a small urban lot, the required spaces can take up so much of the site that a project no longer works. Corner shops, small apartment blocks and conversions of old buildings are often the first to fail, because they have the least room to spare. Cities then lose the kind of compact, varied building that many of them were full of before the rules arrived. Much of the older town centre in most cities could not be built legally today because it lacks the required parking.

Some places have already tried the alternative. Buffalo, in New York State, removed its minimum requirements across the whole city in 2017. Auckland did the same in 2016, and New Zealand extended the change nationally in 2020. London has set maximums rather than minimums for new homes since the early 2000s, and its central boroughs are among the busiest and most valuable places in Europe. Minneapolis and Austin have since dropped their minimums too. None of these cities has reported that parking vanished. Developers there still build spaces where buyers and tenants want them, because a block of flats with no parking is harder to sell in many places. The difference is that the amount is set by the person paying for it rather than by a table in the code.

The main objection is that people will fill the streets with their cars if buildings do not provide spaces. This is a real concern, but it is a problem with how the street is managed and not with the building. Kerbside parking that is free and unpriced will fill up near any busy place, whatever the buildings behind it provide. Cities can charge for it, issue permits to residents, and use the revenue on the same streets, as some already do. Such tools handle the question of who parks where far better than a minimum that fixes the supply at the moment a building is approved and cannot respond to what happens afterwards.

A second objection is that removing minimums hurts places where there are few alternatives to driving. That is a fair point about timing. A town with no buses and a single large employer will find that most developers still choose to build plenty of parking, and removing the rule will change little there. In my view that is an argument for repeal, not against it, since a rule that is not binding costs nothing to drop, and one that is binding is the one doing the harm.

Removing the requirement does not ban parking, and it does not stop a lender, a landlord or a supermarket from insisting on it. It only ends the situation where a government tells a private owner how many spaces to build regardless of the evidence on that site. A council could keep its power to set maximums near rail stations, to require disabled bays, and to manage the kerb. Those are decisions about particular places and can be made with the information to hand.

The simplest test is to ask who gains from the minimum. Developers say it adds cost, residents without cars pay for it, and the land it uses could often have held homes. Cities that have removed it have not seen the consequences its defenders predicted. That is a reasonable basis for other cities to do the same.
