Suppose Alice and Bob want to exchange private messages, but they have never met and have no shared code or password. Everything they say to each other travels along a line that a third person, Eve, can read word for word. For most of history this was an impossible situation, because a secret code needs a secret key, and the key had to be handed over in person or carried by a courier before the conversation started. Public-key cryptography removes that requirement. Alice and Bob can talk openly, Eve can record every word, and at the end Alice and Bob both hold a number that Eve does not know and cannot work out. The method rests on a kind of arithmetic that is easy to do in one direction and, for large numbers, extremely slow to undo.

The first idea is a calculation that is quick forwards and slow backwards. Multiplying 61 by 53 takes a few seconds and gives 3,233. If you are given 3,233 and asked for the two primes that multiply to make it, you have to try dividing by 2, 3, 5, 7 and so on until something fits. For a small number that is a short job, but for a number with six hundred digits there is no known way to do it in any sensible time, even with every computer in the world working on it. Several public-key systems use this fact. The one described here, called the Diffie-Hellman exchange after the two researchers who published it in 1976, uses a different one-way calculation, built from remainders and powers.

A remainder is what is left after dividing. Divide 25 by 23 and you get 1 with 2 left over, so the remainder is 2. Mathematicians call this working modulo 23, and it behaves like a clock with 23 hours on its face: once you pass the top you start again from zero. Every whole number maps to one of the numbers from 0 to 22. A power is a number multiplied by itself a set number of times, so 5 to the power 3 means 5 × 5 × 5, which is 125. Put the two together and take successive powers of 5, keeping only the remainder after dividing by 23. Five to the power 1 is 5. Five squared is 25, which leaves 2. Five cubed is 125, which leaves 10. Five to the power 4 is 625, which leaves 4. The results jump about with no obvious pattern, even though each one follows from the last by a simple rule.

Going forwards is easy. If someone tells you the power is 6, you work it out and get a single answer. Going backwards is the hard direction. If someone tells you the result was 8 and asks which power of 5 produced it, you have no shortcut and must try 1, 2, 3 and so on until you land on 8. With a clock of 23 hours that takes a few moments. With a clock of a very large size it becomes impractical, and that gap between the easy direction and the hard direction is what the whole method depends on.

Here is the exchange with small numbers. Alice and Bob first agree on two public numbers, and they can do this out loud, because nothing about them is secret. The numbers are 23, the size of the clock, and 5, the base to be raised to powers. Eve hears both.

Alice then chooses a private number and tells no one. She picks 6. She works out 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625, and takes the remainder after dividing by 23. Since 23 × 679 is 15,617, the remainder is 8. She sends the number 8 to Bob.

Bob chooses his own private number, 15. He works out 5 to the power 15, which is 30,517,578,125, and takes the remainder after dividing by 23. The remainder is 19. He sends 19 to Alice.

Now each of them uses what the other sent. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power of 6. That gives 47,045,881, and dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private power of 15. The full figure is 35,184,372,088,832, and dividing by 23 also leaves 2. They have reached the same number, 2, without either one having sent it.

The reason they match is a plain fact about powers. Alice effectively computed 5 to the power of 15, raised to the power of 6. Bob effectively computed 5 to the power of 6, raised to the power of 15. In both cases the exponents multiply, and 6 times 15 is the same as 15 times 6, so each ends with 5 to the power 90. The remainders also agree, because taking a remainder part way through the calculation does not change the final remainder. That is what lets each of them work with a smaller number and still arrive at the same result.

Consider what Eve has. She heard 23 and 5 at the start, then 8 from Alice, then 19 from Bob. She does not have 6 or 15, because those never left Alice's and Bob's heads. To get the shared number she needs one of them, which means finding the power of 5 that gives 8 on the 23-hour clock. With numbers this small she can simply try powers until 8 turns up, and she would be done within a minute. That is why the example is only an illustration and would offer no protection in practice.

Real systems use a clock of around 2,048 binary digits, which is a number with about 617 decimal digits, and private numbers of a similar size. The list of powers that Eve would need to search is then far longer than the number of atoms she could ever count, and the best methods published so far do not reduce the work to anything practical. Alice and Bob, meanwhile, can do their own calculations in a fraction of a second, because there are fast ways to compute huge powers by repeated squaring. The honest and the dishonest are doing different jobs, one easy and one close to impossible, and the method is built on that difference. Modern web browsers usually use a variant based on points on a curve, which is called elliptic-curve cryptography, but its structure is the same: two public starting values, two private choices, and one shared result.

The name public-key comes from this split. The number each person sends is the public part, and the number each person keeps is the private part. In other systems, such as RSA, the split is used in a different way, so that anyone can lock a message with a person's public key but only the holder of the private key can unlock it. In everyday use, though, the shared number from an exchange like the one above is typically turned into the key for a fast, ordinary cipher, and that cipher then protects the actual traffic. This is what happens in the moments before a page with a padlock symbol loads.

The method has limits that are worth stating. It does not tell Alice who she is really talking to. If Eve can sit in the middle of the line and answer each of them in turn, she can run one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, and then read and pass on everything. To prevent this, websites present certificates, which are statements signed by organisations that browsers have been set to trust, tying a public value to a named site. There is also no mathematical proof that a shortcut for the hard direction cannot exist, only a long record of people trying to find one and failing. It is generally accepted that a large enough quantum computer would run an algorithm, published by Peter Shor in 1994, that does break these particular problems, and new methods built on other kinds of mathematics are already being introduced for that reason.
