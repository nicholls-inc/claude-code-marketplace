A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to world clocks to keep them in step with the spinning Earth.

For most of history, time was set by the sky. A day was the time between one noon and the next, and a second was a tidy slice of that day. Then, in the 1950s, scientists built atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of atoms and keep time far more steadily than any planet. Since 1967 the official second has been defined by those vibrations, not by the Earth's turning.

The trouble is that the Earth is not a perfect timekeeper. Tides drag on its rotation, earthquakes and shifting ice change it slightly, and movements deep inside the planet nudge it too. Over the long run the Earth has tended to slow, so a day is a little longer than the atomic clocks say it should be. Left alone, atomic time would slowly drift away from the position of the sun in the sky.

To prevent that, international authorities introduced the leap second in 1972. Whenever the gap between atomic time and Earth time grew close to a second, one extra second was inserted at the end of June or December, so a minute briefly lasted 61 seconds. In all, 27 have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016.

Leap seconds sound harmless, but computers dislike them. Software assumes that every minute has 60 seconds and that time only moves forward. An unexpected extra second has caused servers to freeze, databases to disagree and websites to crash. Large technology companies developed their own workarounds, such as spreading the extra second across several hours, but those methods are not uniform, which creates new risks. Systems that depend on precise timing, including financial markets, telecommunications, satellite navigation and power grids, all have to handle the change.

There is another twist. In recent years the Earth has been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the possibility of a negative leap second, where a second would be removed. That has never been tried, and engineers fear it would be even more dangerous for software than an added one.

In 2022 the international body that sets the measurement standards voted to phase out leap seconds by 2035. The reasoning is that the benefit is tiny, since almost nobody notices a gap of under a second between clock time and the sun, while the cost and risk of the adjustment are real. After 2035, the gap will be allowed to grow for decades before any correction is considered, perhaps by adding a larger adjustment such as a minute at some point in the next century.
