In the late summer of 1854, a terrible illness struck the crowded streets of Soho, in London. Cholera, a disease that causes violent diarrhea and rapid dehydration, could kill a healthy adult within a day or two. In the space of about ten days, hundreds of people living around Broad Street died. A physician named John Snow set out to learn why, and his method changed how the world fights disease.

At the time, almost everyone of standing believed cholera spread through "miasma," a poisonous foul air that rose from rotting matter and filthy ground. London was a smelly city, so the theory seemed sensible. Snow, a respected doctor who had pioneered the use of anesthesia and had attended Queen Victoria during childbirth, disagreed. He had studied earlier cholera epidemics and become convinced that the illness entered the body through the mouth, carried in something people swallowed. Cholera, he reasoned, attacked the gut first, not the lungs, which one would expect if the cause were bad air. He suspected contaminated water.

Snow lived and worked a short walk from Broad Street, so the outbreak came to him. He knew the neighborhood. Homes there were packed tightly, and sewage was a constant problem, since many houses drained into cesspools beneath the floors or into the street. Most families had no running water and fetched what they needed from public pumps. One of these, on Broad Street, was known for its pleasant-tasting water.

Snow began with the simplest tool available: a list of the dead. He obtained records from the local register of deaths, which gave names and addresses. Then he did something unusual. He walked the district, knocked on doors, and asked questions. Where did you get your drinking water? Did the person who died use the Broad Street pump? He drew a map of the area and marked each death with a small black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars piled up in clusters, and the heaviest cluster surrounded the pump on Broad Street.

A map alone could be misleading, and Snow knew it. A skeptic might say the deaths clustered simply because that was where people lived, or because the air there was bad. So he looked for exceptions, the cases that did not fit, and those turned out to be the most persuasive evidence he had.

Near the pump stood a workhouse housing more than five hundred people, yet only a handful died. Snow learned the workhouse had its own well and its residents rarely touched the Broad Street water. A brewery on nearby Broadwick Street employed dozens of men, and none of them fell ill. The workers there received a daily allowance of beer, and the brewery also had its own well. The men simply did not drink from the pump.

The reverse cases were just as telling. A woman who lived far from Broad Street, in Hampstead, died of cholera after the illness struck her. Snow discovered that she had once lived in Soho and loved the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her every day. Her niece, who drank from the same bottle, also died. Nobody else in their neighborhood fell ill.

By the first week of September, Snow felt he had enough. He presented his findings to the Board of Guardians of St. James's Parish, the local authority. He asked them to remove the handle from the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. The officials were not convinced by his theory, but they agreed to try. The handle came off on September 8.

Here history grows a little murky, and honesty about it matters. The outbreak was already declining by the time the handle was removed. Many residents had fled the area, and many of the most vulnerable had already died. So the removal of the handle cannot be credited with ending the epidemic, and Snow himself acknowledged this. Still, the evidence he had gathered pointed strongly in one direction.

The final piece came from a local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, who had begun as a doubter of Snow's idea. Whitehead conducted his own investigation among his parishioners and uncovered something Snow had missed. The first known victim, an infant girl who lived at 40 Broad Street, had fallen ill a few days before the surge of deaths. Her mother had washed the child's soiled diapers in a bucket and emptied the water into a cesspool just a few feet from the pump's well. Inspectors later found that the brick lining of the cesspool had decayed, allowing waste to seep into the water supply.

Officials of the time still did not fully accept Snow's conclusions, and the miasma theory held on for years. Snow died in 1858 without seeing it overturned. But his careful counting, mapping, and questioning laid the foundation for modern epidemiology, the study of how diseases spread through populations. Later work by scientists who identified the cholera bacterium confirmed what he had argued. The pump handle became a lasting symbol of how patient evidence can defeat a popular but mistaken belief.
