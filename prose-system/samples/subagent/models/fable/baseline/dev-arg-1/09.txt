Stop Making Cities Build Parking Nobody Asked For

Somewhere in your city's zoning code is a table that few residents have ever read. It says that a restaurant must provide one parking space for every so many square feet of dining room, that an apartment needs one or two spaces per unit, that a bowling alley needs a set number per lane. The figures look scientific. Mostly they are not. They were copied from neighbouring towns, or from surveys of suburban sites with free parking and no bus service, and then written into law as though they described a fact of nature. Cities should delete the table.

Start with what the mandates cost. A surface space takes up roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles needed to reach it. A space in a parking structure costs tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one considerably more. That money does not vanish. It is folded into rents, into the price of a meal, into the lease a shopkeeper signs. Everyone pays it, including the tenant who does not own a car, the pensioner who gave up driving, and the teenager who walked to the cafe. A parking mandate is a transfer from people who drive less to people who drive more, and since car ownership rises with income, it tends to run from poorer households to richer ones.

Then consider what does not get built. A small apartment block on a narrow urban lot often cannot fit the required spaces without sacrificing a floor of homes or digging a garage the project cannot afford. So the developer builds fewer units, or builds luxury units that can carry the cost, or builds nothing. An old shopfront sits empty because a change of use from retail to restaurant triggers a parking requirement that the site, built before cars, can never meet. The buildings people love most in older neighbourhoods, the three-storey walk-ups and corner shops built to the pavement, are frequently illegal to replicate today, and parking rules are a large part of the reason.

The mandates also produce the very problem they claim to solve. When every building must surround itself with asphalt, destinations spread apart. When destinations spread apart, walking becomes impractical and transit becomes unviable, so everyone drives, and the demand for parking rises to meet the supply. Planners then observe full car parks and conclude the requirements were right all along. The reasoning is circular. Free, abundant parking is a subsidy to driving, and subsidised things get used more.

The standard objection is that without mandates, developers will build nothing and cars will flood the neighbouring streets. This misunderstands what repeal does. Removing a minimum is not imposing a maximum. Developers still build parking where their customers want it, because a flat that cannot be let and a shop that cannot be reached are bad investments. Lenders still ask about it. What changes is who decides the number: the person risking money on the building, with knowledge of the site and its likely occupants, instead of a decades-old formula that treats a flat above a tram stop the same as one beside a motorway.

The evidence from cities that have gone ahead supports this. Buffalo removed its minimums citywide in 2017, and subsequent projects provided a mix: some built less than the old code required, some built more, and several conversions of older buildings went ahead that would otherwise have stalled. Minneapolis, which eliminated its requirements as part of a broader set of reforms, has seen strong apartment construction and rents that have held up better than in comparable cities. Neither place ran out of parking. Developers kept building it, in quantities matched to the site.

Spillover onto residential streets is a real concern, but it is a problem of kerb management, and it has kerb-management solutions. Residents' permit zones, time limits, and meters priced so that a space or two is usually free on each block all address it directly. Using the zoning code to protect free street parking is a clumsy method: it forces private landowners to build an expensive product so that a public asset can continue to be given away. Price or ration the kerb properly and the spillover argument largely dissolves.

There are fair caveats. Accessible parking for disabled people should stay mandated, since the market undersupplies it. Places with little transit will see less change than dense centres, because builders there will keep providing plenty of spaces on their own. And repeal is no cure-all for housing costs; it removes one obstacle among several.

But that modesty is the strength of the case. Repeal costs the public purse nothing. It forbids nothing. It simply stops the city from requiring a particular, costly, land-hungry amenity regardless of whether anyone at that address wants it. We do not legislate the number of bedrooms per household or seats per cafe. Parking deserves no special treatment. Let the people who build and occupy buildings decide how much room to give their cars, and let cities get back to the work of making room for people.
