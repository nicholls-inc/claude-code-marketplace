On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unseasonably mild, the temperature having climbed above forty degrees after days of bitter cold, and the neighborhood's dockworkers, children and horse-drawn carts were going about a lunchtime that felt almost like spring. Beside Commercial Street stood a steel tank as tall as a five-story building and nearly as wide as a city block, owned by the United States Industrial Alcohol Company and holding about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, which had been shipped up from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a trade whose demand was swelling as Prohibition approached and as munitions makers wanted the product for the war. The tank had been built hastily in 1915 by a company whose president never bothered to consult an architect or engineer, its walls were thinner than they should have been, and it had been leaking so freely that neighborhood children collected the syrup in cups from the seams, while the company's answer was to paint the tank brown so the stains would not show.

At about 12:40 that afternoon, with a fresh shipment having been pumped in the day before and the sudden warmth thinning the contents and building pressure behind them, the tank failed with a roar that witnesses compared to a machine gun, as rivets shot out and the walls tore open. A wave of molasses perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its front rushed out at around thirty-five miles an hour, and it took with it everything in its path, crushing the nearby firehouse, knocking an elevated railway structure off its supports, and lifting buildings from their foundations. Men, women and children were caught in a substance that grew thicker and stickier as it cooled in the winter air, so that those who were not struck by debris were trapped in it, unable to pull themselves free, and rescuers who waded in to help found themselves stuck to the thighs. Soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship, along with Red Cross nurses and Boston police, formed the chain that tried to reach survivors, and they kept at it for days, though by then the dead numbered twenty-one, with about a hundred and fifty more injured, and horses lay among the wreckage as well.

The harbor ran brown for weeks, and the smell lingered in the neighborhood for decades, or so residents later claimed on hot summer days. The cleanup took months, and the company tried to blame anarchists, suggesting that a bomb had been planted, a theory that suited the postwar fear of radicals. A lawsuit followed, one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts, with an auditor named Hugh Ogden spending three years hearing testimony from some 3,000 witnesses and then concluding that the tank's construction was at fault, so that the company ended up paying out roughly $600,000 in settlements, a sum that for the families of the dead often came to about a few thousand dollars each. The case helped bring about stricter rules requiring engineers to sign off on construction plans, a change that shaped the building codes we now take for granted.
