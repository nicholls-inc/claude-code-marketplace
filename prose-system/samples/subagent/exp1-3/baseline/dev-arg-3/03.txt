**Keep Teaching Joined-Up Handwriting**

Every few years a new round of headlines announces that handwriting is finished. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, so primary schools should stop spending scarce hours on looped letters and join the digital age. The argument is tempting, and it is wrong. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. Not because nostalgia demands it, and not because a beautiful hand is a mark of virtue, but because a fluent, joined script is a practical tool that serves children's thinking, their exam performance and their independence as writers. The cost is modest and the alternatives are worse.

**What the case against gets right**

The sceptics deserve a fair hearing. Most adults write very little by hand. Shopping lists, a signature, a birthday card. Children will spend their working lives at keyboards and touchscreens, and typing is plainly a skill they need. Cursive, in particular, has a reputation for being a fussy hangover from the age of the dip pen, when joining letters kept the nib from lifting and spattering ink. Ballpoints solved that problem decades ago. Why, then, should seven-year-olds spend months learning exit strokes and diagonal joins?

There is also a fairness argument. Some children find joining letters genuinely hard, and being marked down for untidy writing can crush their confidence before they have a chance to show what they know. If the keyboard can free those children, why not let it?

These are serious points, and the answer to them is not to defend handwriting as an end in itself. The answer is to look at what handwriting does and ask whether the keyboard does it as well. On the evidence, it does not.

**Handwriting is a thinking tool, not just a transcription tool**

The strongest reason to keep teaching it is what the act of writing does for learning. A growing body of research compares note-taking by hand with note-taking on a laptop, and children's letter recognition after handwriting practice with recognition after typing. The findings are not uniform, and some of the early headline studies have been challenged on method. But the broad direction is consistent. Forming letters by hand appears to support letter learning in young children, and writing by hand slows a student down just enough that they must select and rephrase rather than transcribe. Typing is faster, which sounds like an advantage until you notice that speed encourages copying everything down without processing it.

For a six-year-old, the benefit is concrete. Each letter has a distinct motor pattern. A child who has physically produced a hundred lower-case b's, with the stem and then the bowl in a fixed sequence, has encoded the difference between b and d in a way that pressing two nearly identical keys does not. Anyone who has taught early reading knows how persistent that confusion can be. Handwriting practice attacks it from the side of the body as well as the eye.

Joined-up writing adds something specific on top of print. When letters flow together, the child learns to write words as units rather than as strings of separate marks. That is closer to how fluent writers actually think about spelling. Teachers often report that joining helps with common letter patterns such as the endings of verbs. A child who has joined the letters in "ing" a few hundred times has a motor memory for that sequence. It is a small effect, but it is real, and it comes almost free once the skill has been taught.

**Fluency frees the mind**

The second argument concerns what psychologists call cognitive load. A child struggling to form letters has less attention left for what they are trying to say. This is why a pupil who can speak a vivid paragraph produces three flat sentences on paper. The mechanics eat the working memory.

Fluent handwriting removes that tax. Once the movements are automatic, the writer can concentrate on vocabulary, sentence structure and ideas. Joined writing tends to be faster than print because the pen stays on the page and there is no stop-start between letters. A child who writes at a comfortable pace can keep up with the stream of their own thoughts. For a child who must handwrite a timed test, that speed is not a luxury. It decides how much they can show.

That matters because handwriting is still how nearly every child is assessed. Written exams, in England and in most countries, remain handwritten from the first spelling test to the last paper at eighteen. A pupil whose script is slow, cramped or illegible loses marks, sometimes because the examiner cannot read the answer, sometimes because the pupil ran out of time. Until the exam hall is full of keyboards, teaching children to write quickly and legibly by hand is not a cultural indulgence. It is basic preparation for the system they are actually in.

**Joined-up is not the same as ornate**

Much of the opposition rests on a caricature. Defenders of joined writing are imagined to demand the looping copperplate of a Victorian copybook. Nobody serious asks for that. Modern schemes, including the ones used in many English primary schools, teach a plain, mostly upright script with simple joins. The aim is legibility and speed, not elegance. Children are encouraged to develop their own style once the basics are secure. In effect, joined writing is taught the way sentence structure is taught: a sound foundation, then personal variation.

This also answers the worry about children who find it hard. The sensible position is not to force every child into a single flawless hand, but to teach the whole class the basics, give extra practice and support to those who struggle, and be flexible about what counts as acceptable. Some children with specific motor difficulties will always do better with a keyboard, and they should have one. But the existence of exceptions argues for support, not for abolishing the skill for everyone. We do not stop teaching reading because some children are dyslexic.

**The practical case for teaching both**

The choice is often framed as handwriting versus typing, as though a school day were a zero-sum contest. It is not. Typing can be taught well in a few dozen hours spread across upper primary and secondary school, and children pick it up quickly because they are surrounded by keyboards at home. Handwriting is different: it has to be taught deliberately, in the early years, while the motor habits are forming, and it is much harder to fix later. A child who reaches secondary school with a clumsy, laboured hand rarely develops a good one by accident.

That asymmetry is the heart of the matter. Typing is an easy skill to acquire late. Handwriting is not. If primary schools drop it, they are not making a neutral trade; they are closing a door that is difficult to reopen. The cost of keeping it is a few short sessions a week, which is small beside the time spent on almost any other part of the curriculum.

There is also resilience to consider. Screens run out of battery, devices break, networks fail, and plenty of schools and homes do not have a reliable device for every child. A pencil works everywhere. A population that can write quickly and clearly by hand has a skill that does not depend on infrastructure.

**What about the human side?**

It is easy to dismiss the personal side of handwriting as sentimental, but it is not trivial. A handwritten note carries something a typed one does not. Children who can write a legible thank-you card, a letter to a grandparent or a message to a friend are practising a form of communication that still matters to the people who receive it. Many adults treasure the handwriting of a parent or teacher precisely because it is individual. Something is lost if a whole generation never develops a hand of its own.

And there is the matter of ownership. A child who can write fluently by hand has a tool that is entirely theirs: no password, no software update, no one else's platform. They can scribble a draft, cross it out, annotate the margin, sketch a diagram in the middle of a paragraph. That freedom is part of what it means to be literate.

**Conclusion**

The case for dropping joined-up handwriting rests on the observation that adults type a lot. That is true and beside the point. Primary school is not a rehearsal for the average adult's day; it is where the foundations of literacy are laid, and handwriting is one of those foundations. It helps children learn the shapes of letters, lets them write fast enough to keep pace with their ideas, prepares them for the handwritten exams they will certainly face, and costs very little time when taught well.

Teach it plainly, teach it without fuss over neatness, support the children who find it hard, and teach typing alongside it. But do not abandon joined-up writing. The pen is not obsolete; it is still one of the most efficient tools a child will ever be given for thinking on paper.
