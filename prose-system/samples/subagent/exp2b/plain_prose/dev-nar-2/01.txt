On the evening of April 11, 1970, three men lifted off from Kennedy Space Center aboard a Saturn V rocket, bound for the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was making his fourth spaceflight and would become the first person to fly to the Moon twice. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only days earlier, replacing Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was on his first flight. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third lunar landing, set down in the Fra Mauro highlands. By then the public had begun to treat trips to the Moon as routine, and the networks had stopped broadcasting from the spacecraft.

The first two days went smoothly. The crew ejected the spent third stage, docked with the lunar module, which they had named Aquarius, and settled into the long coast outward. The command module was called Odyssey. On the night of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the flight, mission control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks. These tanks held supercooled liquid oxygen and hydrogen, which fed the fuel cells that produced the spacecraft's electricity and water. The crew had just finished a television broadcast, and Lovell was tidying up the cabin when Swigert flipped the switch for the stir.

Some moments later came a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Swigert reported the now-famous words, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated them, adding that it was a main B bus undervolt. Warning lights lit up. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas venting into space. The crew still believed this was a fault that could be corrected. They would soon learn it was not.

The cause lay in the oxygen tank. Years earlier, the tank had been dropped slightly during handling, damaging an internal fitting. During ground testing before launch, the tank would not drain properly, and technicians used heaters to boil off the oxygen. The heaters were designed for a lower voltage than the ground power supplied, and their thermostatic switches welded shut. The wiring inside the tank was damaged by the extreme heat, and no one knew it. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark ignited the Teflon insulation in a pure oxygen environment. The pressure rose until the tank ruptured and blew a panel off the service module, damaging the second oxygen tank as well.

Within minutes the situation was grim. The oxygen was draining away, and with it the fuel cells that powered Odyssey. Mission control could see the pressure falling, and the flight director, Gene Kranz, called his team to focus on the problem. The landing was lost. The goal now was to get the men home alive. Odyssey's batteries were needed for the final reentry, so the crew would have to abandon the command module and take shelter in the lunar module, a craft built for two men over two days, now asked to sustain three for four.

Lovell, Haise and Swigert moved into Aquarius and powered it up, while Odyssey was shut down to conserve what little power remained. The first question was the route home. The spacecraft was on a trajectory that would swing around the Moon and, without a correction, miss the Earth. Controllers chose to use the lunar module's descent engine, which was never meant for this job, to speed up the return. They first did a burn to put the ship back on a path that would bring it home, and later a second, larger burn after rounding the Moon, which shortened the trip by many hours.

The crew passed behind the far side of the Moon and came within about a hundred kilometers of its surface, farther from Earth than any humans had ever been. They had no chance to look at the landscape they had hoped to explore, though they did snap photographs. Lovell later described the Earth as a small, distant disc, and the thought of home was a heavy one.

The flight back brought its own troubles. To save power, nearly everything was switched off, and the cabin grew cold, falling to around forty degrees Fahrenheit. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and the crew grew dehydrated. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. They slept little. Condensation formed on the walls. All the while they were breathing air that was growing poisonous, because the lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrubbed carbon dioxide from the air, were filling up. The command module had canisters in plentiful supply, but they were square, and the lunar module's receptacles were round.

Engineers on the ground met this problem with a room full of materials the crew had aboard: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, duct tape, and a spacesuit hose. They devised a way to fit the square canister to the round opening, and read the instructions to the crew over the radio. The astronauts built the device, and the carbon dioxide level began to fall. The improvisation became one of the best-known episodes of the flight.

A further challenge was the reentry procedure. Odyssey had been shut down for days and had to be restarted with the little battery power left, using a sequence that nobody had written because nobody had imagined needing it. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded, worked in a simulator with engineers to find a procedure that would bring the ship back to life without exceeding the available power. The team wrote it out, tested it, and passed it up to the crew.

Another small error needed correcting too. The ship's alignment had drifted, and with the guidance platform off, the crew could not use the usual references. Lovell used the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, to hold the attitude for one burn. Another correction was done by hand, with Swigert timing the burn while Lovell and Haise steered. Each of these was a minor thing in itself, but a mistake in any one could have meant disaster.

On April 17, a few hours before reentry, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module. For the first time they could see the extent of the damage. An entire panel was gone, and the side of the module was scarred and torn. They took pictures and reported what they saw. Then they climbed back into Odyssey, powered it up, and let the lunar module go, thanking it as it drifted away.

Reentry required a communications blackout of about three and a half minutes. When the blackout stretched on past the expected time, the controllers waited in silence. Then, finally, there came Swigert's voice, and cheers broke out in Houston. Odyssey dropped under three parachutes into the South Pacific, near Samoa, and the crew was picked up by the USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted almost six days.

The investigation that followed, led by Edgar Cortright, traced the fault to the tank and its heaters, and NASA redesigned the oxygen system. Apollo flew onward, and four more crews would land on the Moon. Lovell, Haise and Swigert had never walked on its surface, but the rescue of their craft is regarded by many as one of NASA's finest hours. The accident was a failure of equipment and of oversight, and the return was a triumph of improvisation, calm, and teamwork, performed by people who refused to treat a lost Moon landing as a lost crew.
