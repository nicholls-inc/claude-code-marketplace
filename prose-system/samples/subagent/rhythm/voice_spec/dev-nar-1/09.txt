By 1854 cholera had reached London three times. It caused severe diarrhoea and vomiting, and a person could go from healthy to dead within a day. The epidemic of 1848 and 1849 alone killed about fourteen thousand Londoners. Most doctors of the time held that the disease was carried in bad air rising from rotting waste and damp ground. John Snow, a physician born in York in 1813, did not accept this. He was best known for giving chloroform to patients during surgery and childbirth, but he had also studied cholera since the 1830s. In 1849 he published a short book arguing that the illness attacked the gut, that it was passed on by something swallowed from the waste of the sick, and that polluted drinking water was the usual route. Few readers were convinced, and Snow had no proof he could show them.

The outbreak that gave him his chance began around 31 August 1854 in Soho, a crowded district of the West End. Within three days 127 people in the neighbourhood were dead. Within a week many families had left their homes, and some streets stood largely empty. Snow lived in Sackville Street, a short walk away, and he began going from house to house to ask what had happened.

He obtained the list of deaths from the General Register Office and marked the addresses. Nearly all of them lay close to the public water pump in Broad Street, which is now called Broadwick Street. Snow found only ten deaths in houses that were nearer to some other pump. In five of those cases, the families told him that they always sent to the Broad Street pump for their water, and in three more the dead were children who went to school near it. He therefore treated the pump as the common point linking the victims, and he looked for cases that did not fit.

Several turned up, and each one supported him. The workhouse in Poland Street held 535 inmates and recorded only five deaths. It had its own well, and it was surrounded by streets where many had died. The Lion Brewery on Broad Street employed about seventy men, and none of them died. The brewery also had a well of its own, and the men were given malt liquor to drink during the day. A widow who had moved to Hampstead, several miles away, died of cholera on 2 September, and so did her niece. Snow learned that the widow had once lived in Broad Street and had come to prefer the taste of its water. Her son sent her a bottle from the pump each day by cart, and she had drunk some shortly before she fell ill.

On 7 September Snow put his findings before the Board of Guardians of the parish of St James. The next day the board had the handle taken off the pump, so that nobody could draw water from it. The number of new cases was already falling by then, partly because so many residents had fled, and Snow said as much himself. Historians still disagree about how much the removal of the handle changed the course of the outbreak. My own view is that its main value was as an argument, since it showed that a plain action based on his theory was open to the authorities.

One question remained, which was how the water had become poisoned. Here Snow was helped by Henry Whitehead, a curate at the nearby church of St Luke's. Whitehead knew the district and its people, and he began by doubting Snow's theory. He spent weeks questioning residents and came to agree with Snow. His inquiries led him to a house at 40 Broad Street, where a baby girl had fallen ill with diarrhoea in late August and died on 2 September. Her mother had washed the child's soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool beneath the house. When the ground was opened up, the cesspool was found to lie about three feet from the well, and the brickwork of the well was cracked and leaking. Sewage had been seeping into the water that the neighbourhood drank.

In the end 616 people died in the outbreak. Snow drew a map for the second edition of his book, published in 1855, with a bar for each death stacked at the address where it happened. A committee of the Board of Health looked into the outbreak and rejected his conclusion. It said that nothing proved the water was the cause. Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, after a stroke, without seeing his view accepted. The organism that causes cholera had in fact been seen under the microscope in 1854 by an Italian anatomist, Filippo Pacini, but few took notice of his work. Robert Koch identified the bacterium in the 1880s, and by then the sewers of London had been rebuilt for different reasons and cholera no longer returned to the city. Snow's reputation grew after his death, and the Broad Street pump is now regarded as one of the foundations of the study of how disease spreads through a population.
