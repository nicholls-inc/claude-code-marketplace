A leap second is a single extra second added to the world's clocks so that they stay in step with the rotation of the Earth. Since 1967 the length of a second has been defined by atomic clocks, which count 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a caesium atom. These clocks keep time far more steadily than the planet does. The Earth's spin is uneven, and tides, the movement of the molten core and shifts in ice and water all change the length of a day by small amounts. Over the long run the tides have been slowing the rotation, so a day is now a little over two milliseconds longer than it was a century ago.

Coordinated Universal Time, the standard used by civil clocks, was set up in 1972 as a compromise between the two. It ticks at the steady atomic rate, but it is not allowed to drift more than 0.9 of a second from time based on the Earth's rotation. When the gap approached that limit, an international body in Paris, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service, announced a leap second. It was added at the end of 30 June or 31 December, so that the clock read 23:59:60 before moving on to midnight. There have been 27 of them, and the last was on 31 December 2016.

The practice has caused trouble for computers. Most software assumes that every minute has 60 seconds, and a minute with 61 can confuse it. A leap second in 2012 was followed by outages at several websites and an airline's booking system, and a bug in the Linux kernel was blamed for some of them. Large technology companies have handled the extra second by stretching it across the preceding hours, but each operator does this in its own way, and the methods do not always agree with one another. The Earth has also been spinning slightly faster in recent years. That raises the possibility of a negative leap second, in which a second would be removed. This has never been done, and engineers have little experience of how systems would react.

In November 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. After that date the difference between atomic time and Earth time will be allowed to grow well beyond a second, and the limit is to be set at a level that will not be reached for at least a century. Supporters say the change removes a regular risk for systems that depend on exact timing, such as telecommunications, financial trading and navigation. Astronomers, who still need to know the Earth's orientation, will have to correct for the difference themselves. Some form of larger adjustment, such as a leap minute, may be needed in the distant future, but no decision has been taken on that.
