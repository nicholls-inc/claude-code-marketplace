A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). It is easiest to picture the cell as a voltage source whose value depends on how full it is, in series with an internal resistance. Charging means forcing current through that combination so that lithium ions move from the cathode, through the electrolyte, and into the graphite anode.

In the first phase the charger holds the current fixed, commonly around 0.5 to 1 C, where 1 C is the current that would fill the cell in one hour. The cell terminal voltage rises steadily as the state of charge climbs. That voltage is the sum of two parts. One is the open-circuit voltage, which is set by the thermodynamics of how much lithium sits in each electrode. The other is the overpotential, which is the current multiplied by the internal resistance plus extra losses from reaction kinetics and ion transport. For a typical cell the open-circuit voltage runs from about 3.0 V empty to about 4.2 V full.

The cell cannot simply be pushed beyond its upper voltage limit. Above roughly 4.2 V the electrolyte oxidises at the cathode, and the anode surface can drop low enough in potential that lithium plates out as metal instead of intercalating. Plating reduces capacity, can grow dendrites, and is a safety hazard. So once the terminal voltage reaches the limit, the charger switches to the second phase and holds the voltage constant.

The slowdown happens here, and it has a simple cause. With the terminal voltage clamped, the current is whatever the difference between the clamp and the open-circuit voltage can drive through the resistance. As the cell fills, the open-circuit voltage creeps toward the clamp, so the available voltage difference shrinks and the current decays. In a first-order model this looks like an exponential tail, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor, although the real curve is stretched by diffusion effects.

Diffusion adds a second reason. Lithium must spread inward through solid particles, and this is slow compared with the electrical response. During the current phase, the particle surfaces fill first while the cores lag. The surface concentration therefore reaches its limit early, and the voltage hits the clamp before the interior is full. In the voltage phase the lithium gradually equalises inward, and the current falls as this gradient relaxes. Higher charge rates and lower temperatures worsen the gradient, because resistance rises and diffusion slows.

The charger ends the process when the current falls to a cutoff, typically 0.05 to 0.1 C. Continuing would add little charge but extra time at high voltage, which accelerates ageing. This is why the last 20 percent of charge takes disproportionately long, and why fast chargers often stop at about 80 percent.
