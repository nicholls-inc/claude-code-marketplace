Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). The charger first forces a fixed current into the cell, and then holds a fixed voltage while the current falls away.

**Stage 1: constant current.** The charger drives a steady current, typically 0.5C to 1C (a 1C rate would fill the nominal capacity in one hour). The cell's terminal voltage rises steadily as lithium ions move from the cathode, through the electrolyte, and into the graphite anode (intercalation). Terminal voltage is not just the cell's open-circuit voltage. It is that voltage plus a drop across the cell's internal resistance, V = OCV + I·R. For a typical cell with a 4.2 V upper limit, this stage ends when the terminal voltage reaches that limit, at roughly 70 to 80 percent state of charge.

**Stage 2: constant voltage.** The upper voltage limit is a hard safety and lifetime boundary. Pushing a cell above it accelerates electrolyte decomposition and, at the anode, can plate metallic lithium, which degrades the cell and can cause shorts. So the charger stops regulating current and regulates voltage instead, holding 4.2 V. The current now decays on its own, roughly exponentially. Charging ends when current falls to a cutoff, commonly 0.05C to 0.1C.

**Why it slows near full.** Think of the cell as an open-circuit voltage source in series with a resistance. With the terminal voltage clamped, the current is I = (V_limit − OCV) / R. As charge accumulates, OCV climbs toward V_limit, so the driving voltage shrinks and the current shrinks with it. The cell has a rising back-pressure, like filling a tank whose pressure increases as it fills.

Two further effects reinforce this. First, the OCV curve steepens near full charge, so small gains in stored charge produce relatively large voltage rises, leaving little headroom. Second, the internal resistance is not purely ohmic. It includes slow lithium diffusion within solid graphite particles. Near full, the particle surfaces are saturated while their interiors are still filling, so the remaining capacity is reached only as lithium diffuses inward, which is a slow, diffusion-limited process. The surface concentration, which sets the voltage, runs ahead of the bulk, so the controller sees "full" at the terminals while the particle cores still have room.

**Practical consequences.** The CV stage often takes as long as a half to a third of the whole charge, yet adds only 20 to 30 percent of the capacity. This is why fast chargers advertise "0 to 80 percent" times, and why many devices stop at a reduced limit or taper deliberately to protect lifespan. Temperature matters too. Cold cells have higher resistance and slower diffusion, and must be charged gently to avoid plating.
