Apollo 13 left Kennedy Space Center on April 11, 1970, carrying James Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon, in the hilly Fra Mauro region that geologists hoped would hold material older than anything yet brought home. Swigert was not the original command module pilot, since Ken Mattingly had been pulled from the crew a few days earlier after exposure to German measles, and Swigert, who had trained as his backup, took his place with little time to spare. The first two days passed with the kind of routine that the public had begun to find dull, and a television broadcast from the spacecraft on the evening of April 13 was not carried by the major networks, so that few Americans saw the crew show off their cabin, which was a few minutes before the trouble began.

The cause of that trouble had been built into the spacecraft years earlier. The service module carried two tanks of liquid oxygen, which fed the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity and also supplied air for the crew to breathe. Oxygen tank 2 had been dropped slightly while being lifted out of an earlier vehicle in 1968, which probably loosened a fill tube inside it, and during a countdown test in March 1970 technicians had trouble draining it and finally boiled the oxygen off with the tank's internal heaters, run from ground power at 65 volts when the thermostatic switches protecting those heaters were only rated for the spacecraft's 28. The switches welded shut, the heaters ran for hours, and the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank was baked and cracked, a fact no one noticed, so that the tank was cleared for flight with bare wire waiting inside a vessel full of pure oxygen.

About fifty-six hours into the mission, roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, mission control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that agitates the contents so the gauges read correctly. Swigert threw the switch, and a spark from the damaged wiring ignited the insulation in tank 2, the pressure climbed, and the tank ruptured with a bang that Lovell and Haise felt through the structure of the ship. Swigert said that they had had a problem, and Lovell repeated it for the ground a moment later. The explosion blew a panel off the service module and damaged the plumbing of tank 1, which began to leak, and the readings in Houston told a story that the flight controllers at first suspected was an instrument fault because it seemed too severe to be real. Within minutes two of the three fuel cells were dying, the oxygen pressure was falling, and Lovell, looking out a window, reported that something was venting into space, a gas that he could see streaming away from the spacecraft.

Once it was clear that the service module was lost, flight director Gene Kranz and his team had to turn their attention from a landing to getting three men home. The command module, Odyssey, had only its entry batteries, which had to be saved for the last hours before reentry, so the crew powered it down and moved into the lunar module, Aquarius, which had been designed to carry two men to the surface and back over about two days and which now had to sustain three for roughly four. The lunar module's descent engine, meant for landing, became the means of propulsion, and the first major decision was whether to turn around at once or to swing around the Moon on a path that would bring the spacecraft back toward Earth without any need for the damaged main engine. The planners chose the loop, because aborting immediately would have required using the service module engine, whose condition no one could vouch for, and about five hours after the explosion Lovell and Haise fired the descent engine to put the ship onto a free return trajectory. Later, after the spacecraft had passed behind the Moon and come out the other side, a second burn at the point of closest approach sped the return and moved the splashdown to the Pacific, where the recovery fleet could reach it, and in the course of that swing Apollo 13 passed about 250 miles above the lunar far side and took the crew farther from Earth than any humans have ever travelled, 248,655 miles, a record that stands.

The return trip was dominated by scarcity. To keep the lunar module's limited batteries and water alive the crew shut off nearly everything, including most of the guidance computer, and the cabin cooled until the temperature dropped to near freezing, so that condensation ran down the walls and the men, dressed in flight clothes and in some cases without their boots, slept poorly or not at all. Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection, water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and Lovell, who had no hope any longer of walking on the Moon, spent his time on navigation. A more immediate threat came from carbon dioxide, since the lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which absorb the gas, would be saturated long before the trip ended, while the command module held plenty of canisters of a different shape that did not fit the lunar module's sockets. On the ground, engineers assembled from the items available aboard, cardboard, plastic bags, suit hoses and tape, an adapter that would let the square canisters work in a round receptacle, and then read the instructions up to the crew, who built it and watched the carbon dioxide readings fall.

Navigation was its own problem, because the usual star sightings were impossible through the cloud of debris around the ship, and Lovell had to align the spacecraft by the edge of the sunlit Earth, then fire the engine by hand and by clock for a mid-course correction that kept them on target. Meanwhile Mattingly, still on the ground, worked in a simulator with other engineers on a procedure to wake the command module from its sleep using less power than the batteries would permit by the standard checklist, and the sequence he and the team produced was read up to the crew for the final day, when they transferred back into Odyssey. Before entry they jettisoned the service module, and the crew, seeing it for the first time, found that an entire side was missing and that the engine bell hung out beside a scorched, gutted bay, which Lovell reported with evident shock and which the photographs later showed clearly. They also let go the lunar module, which had kept them alive, and which would break up in the atmosphere over the Pacific.

On April 17 the command module hit the atmosphere, and the blackout, when the ionised air around the capsule cuts off radio, ran longer than the planners had expected, long enough that the silence in Houston became hard to bear, until Swigert's voice came through and the parachutes appeared on television. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa at about 1:07 in the afternoon Houston time, and the crew were picked up by the helicopters of the carrier Iwo Jima, tired, unshaven and thinner, but in good health apart from Haise's infection.

A review board under Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank's history of damage and the mismatch of its thermostat switches, and NASA redesigned the oxygen system, removing the stirring fans and their wiring from the tanks and adding a third tank, before flights resumed the following year. Lovell would often say afterward that the mission was a successful failure, the failure being that no one landed, and the success being that the men were brought home, which the people who built the adapter and the checklists, and the three who followed them, had accomplished largely by improvisation.
