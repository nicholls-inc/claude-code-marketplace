Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, at least in the form most of them practice it, which is a person naming a number of days or points for a task they have not yet done and a manager treating that number as a promise. The evidence against the practice is not subtle: decades of research on project overruns, from Kahneman's planning fallacy to large reviews of IT projects, find that the typical estimate is optimistic and that actual outcomes have a long right tail, so the tasks that go wrong go wrong by multiples rather than by percentages, and no amount of careful reasoning in a planning meeting reveals in advance which task that will be.

The cost of the practice is paid twice. Engineers spend hours in refinement sessions debating whether a ticket is a three or a five, and the result carries almost no information beyond what the ticket's title already told you, since the variance between nominally identical tickets dwarfs the difference between a three and a five. Then the number leaves the room and hardens into a commitment in a roadmap, a date given to a customer, a reason to ask why the work took longer than it should have, and the engineer learns to pad, or to cut the corners that never appear on a schedule, such as tests and the cleanup of a design that was only ever meant to be temporary.

What replaces it is less exotic than people fear. Cut the work into pieces small enough that nobody argues about their size, count how many pieces finish each week, and forecast from that history, as a range, with the honest statement that the next six weeks will probably deliver somewhere between this many and that many. Teams that do this find the forecast is no worse than the estimates it replaces, because the throughput of a stable team is remarkably steady even when individual tickets are not, and it costs a fraction of the time. The effort moves to the place where it was always valuable, which is deciding what to build first and what to drop when the range turns out to be wrong.

The strongest objection is that the business needs to plan, and that hiring, launches and contracts all depend on knowing roughly when something will be ready, which is true and is an argument for forecasting rather than estimating, since a probabilistic forecast built from actual delivery data answers the need more honestly than a sum of guesses ever could. It also gets the incentives right, because a manager handed a range learns to plan around uncertainty, whereas a manager handed a single date learns to be angry when it is missed. Where a deadline is fixed by something outside the team, such as a regulation or a trade show, the useful question is what can be cut to fit, and slicing the work finely answers that question where an estimate does not.

Teams will still need to say something about the future, and they should, but they should say it from evidence about what they have shipped, in ranges, and with the understanding that the plan is a bet that gets revised every week.
