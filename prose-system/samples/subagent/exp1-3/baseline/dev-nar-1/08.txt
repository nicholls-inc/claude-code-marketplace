In late August 1854, cholera struck Soho, a crowded district of London. Within ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few hundred yards of one another had died. The disease brings violent diarrhoea and vomiting, and it dehydrates the body so quickly that a healthy person can be dead within a day. Nobody knew what caused it. The dominant belief was "miasma": the idea that disease spread through foul-smelling air rising from rotting waste, and from the filthy streets and cesspools of the city.

John Snow, a physician who lived nearby, doubted this. He had seen the previous cholera epidemic, in 1848 and 1849, and he had already published a short pamphlet arguing something unfashionable. He thought cholera was swallowed, not breathed. The illness began in the gut, he reasoned, and not in the lungs. Whatever caused it must be passed along in something people ate or drank, probably water contaminated by the waste of the sick. The microscope had not yet shown anyone the germ, so Snow had no way to see his culprit. He had only logic and evidence.

When the Broad Street outbreak erupted, Snow set out to gather more of that evidence. He walked the neighbourhood and went door to door, asking about the dead. Where had they lived? What had they drunk? The deaths were clustered tightly, and he soon noticed that most of them centred on the public water pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street. Residents queued there to fill buckets and jugs, because most homes had no running water. Snow suspected the pump.

To test this, he obtained the official list of deaths from the Registrar General's office and began plotting them on a street map. Each death was marked with a small black bar at the address of the victim. As the marks accumulated, they piled up around the Broad Street pump like a dark stain. Households nearer other pumps had far fewer deaths. The map showed the pattern at a glance, and it later became one of the most famous images in the history of public health.

But a skilled investigator looks hardest at the cases that do not fit, and Snow did exactly that. A brewery stood on Broad Street, and its workers, though close to the pump, seemed to have escaped. Snow learned that the men were given free beer, and the brewery had its own well, so they never touched the pump water. A workhouse nearby, housing hundreds of poor residents, also had remarkably few deaths. It too had a private well. Meanwhile, a widow living far away in Hampstead had died of cholera. Snow discovered that she had loved the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her each day. A niece who drank the same water had died too. These exceptions, once explained, strengthened his case rather than weakening it.

Snow also checked the cases that appeared to contradict his theory. Some people who lived near the pump had survived. He found that many of them simply preferred water from other sources, or had been away.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow presented his findings to the local Board of Guardians, the parish officials responsible for the area. He asked them to remove the handle of the pump, which would put it out of use. They were doubtful, but they agreed. The next day the handle was taken off. The outbreak was already declining by then, since many residents had fled the district, so the removal of the handle cannot be credited with ending it alone. Even so, Snow's argument had been heard, and action had been taken based on evidence instead of smell.

The final proof came from a clergyman named Henry Whitehead. He had at first been sceptical of Snow's idea, but as a local resident he knew the community well and worked with Snow to investigate more thoroughly. Whitehead tracked down the first known victim, a baby girl who lived at 40 Broad Street and fell ill in late August. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies and thrown the water into a cesspool just a few feet from the pump's well. Inspection later showed that the brickwork of the well was cracked, and sewage was leaking into the water supply. The source was found.

Snow's theory was not widely accepted at once. Officials preferred to blame the air, and a committee that investigated the outbreak rejected his conclusion. He died in 1858, still largely unconvinced of his triumph by his critics. But later work on germs confirmed that he had been right, and cholera was eventually shown to be caused by a bacterium carried in water. Today Snow is remembered as a founder of modern epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations. His method was simple and powerful: count the cases, map them, test the exceptions, and follow the evidence wherever it leads.
