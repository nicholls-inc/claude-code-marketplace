A lithium-ion cell is charged with a protocol called constant current, constant voltage (CC-CV). It works like a regulated supply feeding a capacitor-like load whose open-circuit voltage rises as it fills.

In the first phase the charger forces a fixed current, commonly 0.5C to 1C, where 1C is the current that would fill the rated capacity in one hour. The cell terminal voltage rises steadily during this phase. Lithium ions leave the cathode (for example a nickel-manganese-cobalt oxide), travel through the electrolyte and the porous separator, and insert between the graphene layers of the graphite anode. The cell terminal voltage is the sum of two things. One is the open-circuit voltage (OCV), set by thermodynamics and by how full the electrodes are. The other is the voltage lost across the cell's internal resistance, which is the current multiplied by that resistance.

When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, typically 4.2 V for a common chemistry, the controller switches to the second phase. This limit exists because higher voltage accelerates electrolyte oxidation, cathode degradation and, at the anode, lithium plating, which is metallic lithium deposited on the graphite surface. Plating is both a lifetime problem and a safety hazard. So the charger now holds the voltage constant and lets the current fall on its own. Charging is declared complete when the current decays to a cutoff, often around 0.05C.

The slowdown has a simple electrical explanation. With terminal voltage clamped at the limit, the current is the difference between that limit and the OCV, divided by the internal resistance. Early in the CC phase the OCV is well below the limit, so the gap is large. As the cell fills, the OCV climbs toward the limit, the gap shrinks, and the current must shrink with it. The behaviour resembles a resistor-capacitor circuit charging toward a source voltage, with an exponential-like decay of current.

There is also a transport reason that acts alongside the electrical one. Near full charge the graphite surface is nearly saturated with lithium, so there are fewer vacant sites to accept more ions. Solid-state diffusion into the particle interior is slow, and concentration gradients build up inside each particle. The surface reaches its limit before the core does, so the cell reads full at the terminals while the interior is still equilibrating. Forcing more current at this stage would overshoot the surface potential and drive plating, which is exactly what the voltage clamp prevents.

The practical consequence is that the CC phase delivers roughly 70 to 80 percent of the capacity quickly, while the CV tail takes a disproportionate share of the total time. Fast-charging designs therefore try to reduce internal resistance and improve diffusion, through thinner electrodes, better electrolytes and warmer cell temperatures, to shorten that tail without crossing the plating threshold.
