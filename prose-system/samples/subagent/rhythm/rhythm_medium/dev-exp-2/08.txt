A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time that do not quite agree. One is astronomical: a day is the time the planet takes to rotate once, and for most of history the Sun overhead was the only clock anyone had. The other is atomic: since the 1950s, laboratories have counted time by the vibrations of atoms, and these clocks tick with a steadiness that no spinning planet can match. The Earth, it turns out, is a rather unreliable timekeeper, because tides, shifting ice and movements of molten rock inside it slow and speed its rotation by tiny amounts.

When Coordinated Universal Time, the basis of civil timekeeping, was set up in 1972, its designers wanted a clock that ran at the atomic rate but never drifted far from the Sun. Their solution was to insert an extra second when the two threatened to differ by more than 0.9 of a second. Since then, 27 leap seconds have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016. Each one was announced only months in advance by an international body that monitors the Earth's rotation, and on the chosen night the clocks ran from 23:59:59 to 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight.

For ordinary people this made little difference, but for the machines that now run modern life it has been a persistent headache. Computers generally assume that every minute has sixty seconds, and a sixty-first can confuse software, cause systems to freeze, or leave different networks disagreeing about the exact time. Several large internet outages have been blamed on leap seconds, and technology companies have invented a variety of workarounds, such as spreading the extra second thinly across a whole day, which only adds to the inconsistency. Financial markets, telecommunications, satellite navigation and power grids all depend on precise, shared timestamps, and none of them welcomes a surprise.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. The Earth has also recently been spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the unusual prospect of a negative leap second, a skipped second that would be even harder for software to handle. The plan is to let the gap between atomic and solar time grow for decades, and to decide later how to correct it, perhaps in larger and rarer steps. Sunrise will not noticeably move as a result.
