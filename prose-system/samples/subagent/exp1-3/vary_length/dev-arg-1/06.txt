**Let the Market Park the Cars**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet kind of waste. It sits behind strip malls, around apartment blocks and beside restaurants, and it is almost always empty. I mean the parking lot, and its size is not an accident. It is the product of minimum parking requirements, rules that force builders to supply a fixed number of spaces whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these rules entirely.

Start with how the numbers get made. Most requirements trace back to rough guidelines drafted decades ago, often from surveys of a handful of suburban sites. Planners took the peak demand observed at those sites, at the busiest hour of the busiest day, and wrote it into law as the floor for everyone. A restaurant must have one space per hundred square feet. An apartment must have two spaces per unit. Nobody asks whether the restaurant sits next to a train station or whether the tenants own cars at all.

The result is a mandate to oversupply. Parking that is full on the week before Christmas sits idle the other fifty-one weeks.

That oversupply has a price, and someone pays it. A surface space costs a few thousand dollars to build and takes up about three hundred square feet once you include the aisles. A space in a garage can cost tens of thousands. Developers do not absorb that bill. They pass it into rents, menu prices and the cost of everything sold in the building. Renters who own no car still pay for the spaces, because the cost is bundled into the rent. Researchers who study the subject, Donald Shoup most famously, have argued for years that this hidden subsidy makes driving look cheaper than it is and everything else look dearer.

Housing is where the damage is clearest. Every space a builder must provide is room and money taken from homes. On a small urban lot, the parking mandate can decide whether a project of twelve apartments is possible or whether only eight pencil out. Some sites simply cannot meet the requirement, so nothing gets built, and the old building stays as it is while the shortage grows. We say we want more affordable housing, then we pass rules that make it expensive and rare. That is a contradiction, and parking minimums are one of its clearest examples.

Now the objection. If cities drop the rules, won't streets fill with cars and neighborhoods fall into chaos?

I doubt it, and the evidence from cities that have tried suggests the fear is overblown. Buffalo scrapped its minimums citywide in 2017. Minneapolis and several other cities have followed in the years since. What happened was not a flood of spillover cars. Developers kept building parking, because tenants and customers still want it. They just built the amount they judged useful rather than the amount a formula demanded. Some projects built less. Some built more. The difference is that the choice now belongs to the people who know the site and bear the cost of getting it wrong.

That is the core of the argument. A developer who builds too little parking will lose tenants to a rival down the street, so there is already a strong incentive to get the number right. Lenders have the same incentive. They will not finance a building that cannot attract renters. The market is not perfect, but it has a feedback loop. A rule written in a council chamber in 1985 has none.

A fair critic will say that free street parking can still be overrun when nearby buildings provide none. That is true, and it is a real problem. But it is a problem with how we price curb space, not with how many off-street spaces we mandate. Charge a modest, demand-based fee for street parking in busy districts, and spillover becomes manageable. Better still, return some of that revenue to the neighborhood in the form of sidewalks and lighting. Residents then see a direct benefit from the meter instead of a nuisance.

There is also a matter of fairness. Minimums force every household to fund a habit that only some practice. Young people, the elderly, the poor and anyone who chooses to live without a car are all billed for the storage of other people's vehicles. A rule that quietly taxes the carless to benefit the car-owning is hard to defend once you see it clearly.

Repeal does not ban parking. This point gets lost constantly. A grocery store in a car-dependent suburb will still build a large lot, because its customers arrive by car. A downtown tower may build a garage. What changes is that the lot is a decision and no longer a decree. Cities can keep their power to set maximums in dense areas or to require accessible spaces for people with disabilities, which serve a clear public need.

We are left with a simple choice. We can keep telling builders how much concrete to pour for cars, at the expense of homes, shops and parks. Or we can let the people who pay for the space decide what it is worth. Cities that have taken the second path have not regretted it. The rest should follow, and soon.
