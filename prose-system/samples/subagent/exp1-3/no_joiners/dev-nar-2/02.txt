On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center in Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third crewed landing, and by then the public had grown comfortable with the idea. Two crews had already walked on the lunar surface, and the television networks had stopped broadcasting from the spacecraft. Nobody expected the next few days to become one of the great survival stories of the twentieth century.

The commander was Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights who was making his fourth trip into space. Fred Haise, a rookie, was to pilot the lunar module. The third seat was supposed to belong to Ken Mattingly, but a few days before launch doctors worried that he had been exposed to German measles, and he was replaced by a backup, Jack Swigert. Swigert had trained hard but had only days to settle into the crew. Mattingly, grounded and frustrated, would soon play a crucial part from the ground.

The first two days went smoothly. The astronauts named their command ship Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. They coasted outward, checked their systems and joked with Houston. On the evening of April 13, about fifty five hours into the flight, Mission Control asked them to stir the cryogenic tanks. These tanks held liquid oxygen and hydrogen that fed the fuel cells making the ship's electricity and drinking water. Stirring them prevented the contents from stratifying, and it was a routine chore.

Swigert flipped the switch. A few moments later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights spread across the panel. Swigert called down that they had a problem, and Lovell repeated it a moment later, a phrase that would become famous in a slightly altered form. On the ground, flight controllers watched their screens fill with strange readings. Pressure in one oxygen tank had dropped to nothing. The second tank was leaking away as well.

Investigators later pieced together what had happened. Years earlier, during ground testing, a heater inside oxygen tank two had been exposed to a voltage it was never designed to handle, and its protective insulation had been quietly damaged. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, bare wires sparked inside a tank full of pure oxygen. The tank burst, tore a panel from the side of the service module and crippled everything around it.

Within minutes it was clear that the Moon was out of the question. Without oxygen the fuel cells would die, and the command module would be left with only its batteries, which were needed for the final descent through the atmosphere. Lovell looked out the window and saw gas streaming into space. He told Houston that it looked like the ship was venting something, and everyone understood what it meant.

The solution was as unlikely as the accident. Aquarius, the lunar lander, was a fragile craft built to support two men for about two days on the Moon. Now it had to serve as a lifeboat for three men for four days. The crew crowded into it and shut down Odyssey to save its remaining power. Gene Kranz, the flight director, led his team through the long night of calculations that followed. His controllers split into groups, and each took one piece of the puzzle. They had to conserve electricity, conserve water, find a path home and keep the astronauts alive on the way.

The path home presented its own choices. The ship was on a trajectory that would loop around the Moon and return toward Earth, but it would miss a safe landing zone by a wide margin. Engineers decided to fire the lander's descent engine, never meant for this job, shortly after the ship passed around the far side of the Moon. The burn would speed up the return and bring the capsule to the Pacific Ocean. While the crew was behind the Moon, they were farther from home than any human beings had ever been, nearly four hundred thousand kilometers from Earth. Lovell later recalled the strange stillness of that moment, with Earth a small blue marble far away.

Then new problems appeared. Three men breathing in a cabin built for two produced carbon dioxide faster than the lander's filters could remove it. The command module carried plenty of spare filters, but they were square and the lander's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the same materials the astronauts had on board, which included cardboard, plastic bags, a spacesuit hose and a roll of duct tape. They worked out a way to join the pieces into an adapter and read the instructions to the crew over the radio. The astronauts built the contraption, and the carbon dioxide levels fell.

Cold became the next enemy. With the power cut, the cabin temperature dropped close to freezing. Water condensed on the walls, and the men slept little. They rationed their drinking water, and Haise developed a fever from an infection. Lovell and Swigert were exhausted and dehydrated, and all three lost weight. Yet they kept working, listening to the calm voices from Houston and following long lists of instructions.

A further course correction was needed on the way home, and it had to be done by hand. The guidance computer had been shut down to save energy, and the crew could not rely on the usual stars for navigation because the debris cloud from the accident glittered around the ship. Lovell used the Sun to align the craft, and the burn was carried out with the care of men who knew a small error could ruin everything.

Meanwhile Mattingly and a team of engineers wrote a new procedure for starting up Odyssey in the correct sequence with so little power that nothing could go wrong. He worked it through in a simulator for hours until the steps were right. It had never been tried in space, and the stakes could not have been higher.

As the ship approached Earth, the crew released the damaged service module. For the first time they could see the harm done by the explosion. An entire panel was gone, and equipment dangled from the wound. Lovell reported the sight to Houston and photographed it so the experts could study it. Then the crew moved back into Odyssey, turned on its systems one by one, and said goodbye to Aquarius, which had saved their lives. The lander was released and later burned up in the atmosphere.

On April 17 the capsule plunged into the air above the Pacific. The radio blackout that comes with reentry was expected to last about three minutes, but it stretched on. Everyone in Mission Control waited, and the silence seemed endless. Then Swigert's voice came through. Three parachutes opened, and television cameras showed the capsule drifting to a gentle landing in the sea near Samoa. The crew was picked up by helicopter and brought to the carrier Iwo Jima. They had been gone for nearly six days.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, yet it is remembered as a triumph. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tanks and the wiring, and the program continued, with later crews landing safely. The astronauts and the people on the ground had shown that careful thought and steady nerves could solve problems nobody had imagined. In the end, the mission was called a successful failure, which is exactly right.
