Joined-up handwriting should stay in the primary school curriculum

Every few years a headline announces the death of cursive. Keyboards are everywhere, the argument runs, so teaching small children to link their letters is nostalgia dressed up as education. Several American states dropped it after the Common Core reforms, and plenty of teachers elsewhere quietly let it slide. I think that is a mistake. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and teach it properly, because it serves children's thinking, their fluency, their access to the written world and their dignity as writers in ways that typing cannot replace.

Start with the strongest objection, which is practicality. Adults rarely write by hand for long stretches, and children will spend most of their working lives at screens. This is true, and it is why handwriting instruction should be short, focused and well taught rather than abandoned. But the practical argument cuts the other way too. Children still handwrite in class every day. They take notes, answer questions, fill in forms and sit exams, and in many countries examinations remain handwritten for the foreseeable future. A child who writes slowly and illegibly is penalised in all of these settings, however brilliant their ideas. Handwriting speed and legibility are not luxuries. They are a gateway skill, and the evidence suggests that fluent joined writing is faster than printing once it is automatic, because the pen stays on the page and the hand moves in a continuous rhythm instead of lifting after every letter.

That brings me to the question of cognitive load. A child who must think hard about how to form each letter has less attention left for what to say. Researchers who study writing have long observed that transcription skills, meaning handwriting and spelling, constrain composition. When letter formation is automatic, children write longer, more coherent and more ambitious texts. When it is laboured, they choose short words they can form easily and stop early. Joining letters, done well, is part of what makes handwriting automatic. The movement becomes a flowing pattern rather than a series of separate stabs, and flowing patterns are easier to memorise and reproduce. A teacher who has watched a seven year old finally stop gripping the pencil like a dagger and start to write freely knows what this looks like. The writing gets better because the hand has stopped being the bottleneck.

There is also a body of research suggesting handwriting does something for learning that typing does not. Studies comparing note taking by hand and on laptops have found that handwriting tends to produce better conceptual understanding, partly because the slower pace forces students to summarise rather than transcribe. Brain imaging work with children and adults has found richer neural activity during handwriting than during typing, particularly in areas associated with learning and memory. Some of these findings are contested, and I would not claim that cursive specifically is magic. Much of the research compares handwriting in general with keyboarding, and it does not isolate joined writing from print. But the broad picture is that the physical act of forming letters helps children learn them, recognise them and remember what they have written. If we are going to teach handwriting at all, and almost no one is arguing that we should not, then we should teach the version that is fastest and most fluent.

Some will say print is just as good, and that a clear, unjoined hand does the job. For many children it does, and I would not pretend otherwise. But there are good reasons to prefer a joined style as the goal. A continuous script reduces the chance of reversing or confusing letters, which is one reason it is often recommended for children with dyslexia and related difficulties. Because every word is a single movement with a clear beginning and end, children are less likely to leave gaps in the wrong places or to scatter letters across the line. Teachers who work with struggling writers frequently report that joining helps spelling, since the hand learns common letter strings such as "ing" and "tion" as units. Printed writing asks a child to do the same thing with an extra step, lifting and repositioning the pen again and again. It is slower, and it tires the hand sooner.

Then there is access to the written world. Handwritten documents fill our archives, our families and our classrooms. A child who cannot read cursive cannot read their grandmother's recipe cards, the letters in a local museum, or a teacher's feedback in the margin of their work. This may sound sentimental, but it is a real literacy gap. Historians have already warned that a generation of students cannot read primary sources from even the last century. Reading joined writing requires being taught it, and the most natural way to teach reading it is to teach writing it. A school that drops cursive does not abolish it from the world. It simply makes it a specialist skill, available to the children whose families happen to teach it at home, and so deepens an inequality that schools exist to reduce.

That equity point deserves emphasis. If cursive is left to chance, privileged children will still learn it from parents, tutors and private schools, and will carry its advantages, the speed, the polish, the confidence, into secondary school and beyond. Children without that support will be left with whatever hybrid they happened to pick up. Handwriting has always carried social weight, whether we like it or not. Teachers, examiners and employers form impressions from it, and a fluent, legible hand is read as a sign of care. Taking away formal instruction does not remove that judgement. It only removes the opportunity to prepare children for it fairly.

I should be honest about the weaknesses in the case. Some countries with excellent literacy results teach print for far longer than England or the United States do, and Finland has moved toward keyboard skills in the early grades. The evidence for cursive over print is thinner than advocates sometimes imply, and I do not want to overstate it. Nor should schools spend hours of precious time on elaborate calligraphy, looped capitals or pages of identical copying. A hundred hours of joined writing drill would be a poor use of a young child's week. The sensible position is modest. Teach letter formation carefully in the early years, introduce joins once children have control of their letters, perhaps around age seven, and give a few short, regular practice sessions rather than long ones. Pair this with keyboard skills taught alongside, not instead. Children in the twenty first century need both, and there is no contest between them.

The strongest reason to keep teaching cursive is perhaps the least measurable. Handwriting is a human skill, personal and expressive, and learning it well gives children a sense of ownership over their words. A child's handwriting changes as they grow, becoming recognisably theirs, and that individuality is part of how children come to see themselves as writers. The pleasure of a pen gliding across paper, of a page of tidy, connected lines that look like something a person made, is real, and children feel it. We do not drop drawing from the curriculum because cameras exist, and we do not drop mental arithmetic because calculators exist. We teach these things because they build capacities that tools cannot replace, and because we want children to be able to do for themselves what machines do for us.

Critics are right that no one needs cursive to survive. But education is not only about survival skills. It is about giving children the fullest possible command of the means by which humans have recorded and shared thought for thousands of years. Joined-up handwriting is a small, teachable, well understood part of that. It speeds writing, eases the load on young minds, supports struggling readers and writers, opens the handwritten past, and offers every child, not just the lucky ones, a fluent personal hand. The cost is modest and the benefits, while not dramatic, are broad and durable.

So the answer to the question is yes. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, teach it efficiently and without fuss, and treat it as one literacy tool among several rather than a relic. Let children type, and let them write by hand too. The child who can do both is better equipped than the one who can do only one, and it costs us only a little time each week to make sure every child can.
