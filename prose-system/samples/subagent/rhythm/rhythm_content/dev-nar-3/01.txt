On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was unseasonably mild, the temperature having climbed above forty degrees after days of bitter cold, and the neighborhood's dockworkers, children, and housewives were going about a midday that gave no hint of what was coming. Looming over Commercial Street stood a steel tank some fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and filled with more than two million gallons of molasses that had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a product much in demand for munitions during the war and, after Prohibition took hold, for liquor. The tank had been built hastily in 1915, and the company had treated it with remarkable carelessness: its walls were thinner than the load required, no one had tested it properly by filling it with water, and when it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the seeping syrup in cups, the company simply painted the tank brown so the stains would be less noticeable.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, witnesses heard a low rumble and a sound like machine-gun fire, which was the rivets shooting out of the steel, and then the tank failed and released a wave of molasses that by most accounts stood fifteen or more feet high at its front and moved at something like thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed buildings, tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports, lifted a train, and knocked a firehouse off its foundation, trapping and killing several men inside. The air itself seemed to be pushed ahead of the wave, and people who survived described being knocked flat before the syrup ever touched them. Twenty-one people died, drowned or crushed or suffocated, and roughly a hundred and fifty more were injured; among the dead were children who had been collecting firewood near the tank, and horses too were lost in numbers that rescuers found hard to bear.

The cold that had seemed so distant that morning made the rescue far harder than it should have been, because the molasses, though it began as a surging flood, thickened rapidly as it cooled and became a sticky trap in which rescuers waded to their waists while they searched for survivors. Soldiers, sailors from a docked ship, Red Cross workers, and police poured into the area, and for days afterward the neighborhood was cleaned with salt water from fireboats and sand spread across the streets. Boston Harbor ran brown well into the summer, and residents claimed that on hot days the district smelled of molasses for decades afterward.

Lawsuits followed, and what makes the disaster historically significant is how they went. United States Industrial Alcohol insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank, a claim that fit the fears of the postwar years, but a court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, spent months hearing testimony from hundreds of witnesses and engineers and concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, leaving the company liable. It paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements, and the case helped push Massachusetts and other states toward requiring that engineers and architects certify the plans for large structures.
