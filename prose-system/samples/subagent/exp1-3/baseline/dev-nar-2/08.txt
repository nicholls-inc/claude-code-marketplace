**Apollo 13: The Successful Failure**

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rose from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third lunar landing, and by then the public had grown casual about such things. Two crews had already walked on the Moon. Television networks had stopped carrying the flights live. Jim Lovell, the commander, was making his fourth spaceflight and would have been the first person to fly to the Moon twice. With him were Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, and Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons. Swigert trained intensively and took his place.

The first two days went smoothly. The crew called their command ship Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. They broadcast a relaxed television tour of the spacecraft on the evening of April 13. Then, about 56 hours into the flight and some 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked them to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine step that keeps the supercold contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A little over a minute later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered.

"Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert said. Lovell repeated it a moment later. Warning lights lit up. A main electrical bus had lost voltage. Then Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas venting into space, a sign that something had gone badly wrong with the service module.

The cause lay in an oxygen tank, though no one on the ground knew that yet. Before launch, a heater thermostat inside that tank had been damaged during ground testing, when it was subjected to a higher voltage than it was built for. Nobody caught the damage. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark in the wiring ignited the Teflon insulation in the pure oxygen. The tank ruptured, blew off a service module panel, and crippled the second oxygen tank beside it. The command module's fuel cells, which made both electricity and drinking water from oxygen and hydrogen, began to starve.

In Houston, flight controllers watched their data stop making sense, first suspecting instrument faults, then realizing the readings were real. Flight Director Gene Kranz and his team understood within the hour that the Moon landing was finished. The only goal now was to get three men home alive.

The crew's lifeboat was the lunar module. Aquarius had been designed to support two men for roughly two days on the Moon's surface. Now it had to keep three alive for four. Odyssey's batteries had to be saved for the last hours of the flight, since the command module was the only craft that could survive reentry. Lovell and Haise moved into Aquarius and powered up its systems while Swigert shut down Odyssey piece by piece.

The next question was how to steer home. Apollo 13 was on a trajectory that would swing around the Moon and loop back toward Earth, but not near enough to the planned landing zone. Worse, the service module's main engine could not be trusted after the explosion. Mission planners decided to use Aquarius's descent engine, a motor built for landing, to change course. A first burn put the spacecraft on a faster path home. After rounding the far side of the Moon, where the crew passed farther from Earth than any humans before or since, at nearly 249,000 miles, they fired the engine again. That second burn, about two hours after closest approach, shortened the trip by roughly ten hours and moved the splashdown into the Pacific, where recovery ships could wait.

Then came the problems of staying alive during that trip. Power was scarce, so almost everything was switched off. Cabin temperatures dropped to near freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls. The crew had little to drink, since water was needed to cool the few systems still running. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. Sleep was almost impossible. Lovell later recalled that the three men sat huddled, cold and exhausted, in a cabin that felt like a refrigerator.

The most dangerous problem was the air. Carbon dioxide builds up in a sealed cabin, and lithium hydroxide canisters absorb it. Aquarius carried enough canisters for two men for two days. Odyssey had plenty, but its canisters were square and the lander's receptacles were round. As the gas level crept upward on the lunar module's gauges, engineers on the ground built a workaround from what the crew had on board: cardboard from a flight manual cover, plastic bags, a suit hose, and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio. The crew assembled the adapter, and carbon dioxide fell back to safe levels.

Navigation had its own difficulty. With the main guidance computer off to save power and a cloud of debris around the spacecraft, the crew could not easily use the stars to align their craft. Instead, they used the Earth. Lovell held the planet's terminator, the line between day and night, in the window while another burn was performed manually to correct the flight path. The correction worked.

As the spacecraft closed on Earth, another uncertainty arose. Odyssey would have to be powered back up from cold, using batteries that had sat idle for days, and no one had ever done that procedure in flight. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded, joined engineers in a simulator and worked out a sequence of switch settings that would stay within the battery's energy budget. Mission Control read the new checklist up to the crew.

Four hours before entry, the crew cast off the damaged service module. For the first time they could see what had happened. Lovell reported that an entire side panel was gone, and that much of the module's equipment was visible and damaged. They photographed it, which helped investigators later. Then they shifted back into Odyssey, powered it up, and separated from Aquarius, which had saved their lives and would burn up in the atmosphere.

Reentry on April 17 meant a communications blackout, which normally lasts about three minutes as the ionized air around the capsule blocks radio. This time the silence stretched longer, as the changed flight path altered the geometry, and the control room waited in tense quiet. Then, around the time the blackout should have ended, Swigert's voice came through. Three parachutes opened. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, after almost six days in space.

An accident review board later traced the failure to the damaged tank and its heater system, and NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks and added a third, separate one. Apollo continued with more landings, though public attention never fully returned.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, yet it is remembered as one of the space program's finest hours. Nothing about the failure was planned, and everything about the rescue was improvised under pressure by people on the ground and in orbit working together. Lovell later titled his memoir of the flight *Lost Moon*. The mission was called a "successful failure," and the label has lasted because it fits.
