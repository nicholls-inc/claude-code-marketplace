On a mild January day in 1919, the North End of Boston was busy. It was January 15, a little past noon. Dockworkers ate lunch, children played in the streets, and horse-drawn wagons rattled along Commercial Street beneath the elevated railway. Looming over the neighborhood, at 529 Commercial Street, stood a steel tank 50 feet tall and 90 feet across. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. Molasses was valuable cargo. Fermented and distilled, it became industrial alcohol, used in munitions during the First World War and soon to be needed for rum and other liquor before Prohibition took hold. The tank had been built hastily in 1915, and it showed. Its walls were thin, its rivets poorly set, and the company's engineers had never properly calculated the stresses involved. When it was first filled, it leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the dribbling syrup in cups. Rather than strengthen the tank, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. A fresh shipment had arrived only the day before, and the warmer weather may have made the contents expand.

At about 12:30 p.m., residents heard a low rumble, followed by a sound some compared to a machine gun as rivets shot loose. Then the tank burst. A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, surged outward at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It carried with it a wall of air and a deadly weight of liquid, nearly 13,000 tons of it.

The flood crushed everything in its path. It snapped the supports of the elevated railway, knocking a train car sideways. It lifted buildings off their foundations. A firehouse was torn apart, and men inside were swept away. Wagons, horses, and people were caught in the thick current, and the cold air quickly stiffened the syrup, trapping victims as it thickened around them. Rescuers waded into the sticky mess, but progress was agonizingly slow. Sailors from a nearby ship, police, Red Cross workers, and Boston Navy cadets all joined the effort. In the end, twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Some victims were found days later, and the harbor ran brown for months.

The cleanup was an ordeal. Crews sprayed salt water from fireboats and spread sand and sawdust over the streets. Yet for years afterward, residents claimed the neighborhood smelled of molasses on warm summer days.

The legal aftermath proved just as significant. The company blamed anarchists, claiming that the tank had been bombed. But a court-appointed auditor heard from more than 3,000 witnesses and found otherwise. The tank had been dangerously flawed, built without adequate engineering review. In 1925, the company was held liable and paid damages to victims' families. The case became a landmark in American law, helping establish that companies must answer for negligent construction, and contributing to stricter building codes and a requirement that engineers certify their plans.

Today a small plaque marks the spot, a quiet reminder of a disaster as strange as it was tragic.
