Keep the Joins

Every few years someone declares the death of handwriting, and every few years a school somewhere announces that it will stop teaching children to join their letters. The reasoning sounds sensible. Children will spend their working lives at keyboards and touchscreens, so why spend precious classroom hours looping an l into an e? I think this reasoning is mistaken. Primary schools should keep teaching joined up handwriting, and they should teach it properly, with time, patience and clear expectations. The case rests on how children learn, on what fluent writing does for thinking, and on what a society loses when it quietly decides that a skill is no longer worth passing on.

Begin with the claim that handwriting is obsolete. It is a claim about adults, but primary school is about children. A seven year old is not preparing for a career in an open plan office. She is learning how to turn thoughts into marks on a page, a process that is slow and effortful at first and must become automatic before it stops getting in the way. Handwriting is the main route by which most young children do this. They write their spellings, their sentences, their first stories and their sums by hand, and they will go on doing so for years. Whatever happens in the labour market in 2045, the next decade of a child's education will still depend on a pencil being comfortable in her fingers.

So the real question is not whether handwriting matters but which kind of handwriting serves children best. Here the case for joined up writing is stronger than its critics admit. Printed letters, the kind children learn first, require the pencil to leave the page after every letter. Each lift is a small decision. The writer must stop, relocate and restart, thousands of times in a single page. Joined writing replaces that stop and start rhythm with a continuous movement. The hand learns a flow. Letters that belong together in a word are physically linked, and the word becomes a single gesture rather than a string of separate drawings. For a child who will eventually write for hours in exams, this difference in efficiency is not trivial.

Speed matters more than it first appears. Researchers who study writing in schools have long noticed that children who write slowly and laboriously tend to write less, and to write worse. When the physical act of forming letters consumes most of a child's attention, little is left for choosing words, structuring an argument or remembering how to spell. Psychologists call this a problem of cognitive load. A child who has to think about how to make a letter cannot also think very hard about what to say. Fluent, automatic handwriting frees working memory for composition. Teachers see the result. Children whose handwriting has become effortless tend to produce longer and more ambitious pieces, because the pencil is no longer fighting them.

Some will answer that typing solves this problem more cleanly. Keyboards are fast, legible and easy to edit. But the evidence for simply swapping one tool for the other is thin, and some of it points the other way. Studies comparing note taking by hand and on laptops have often found that handwriting produces better understanding, because the slowness forces the writer to process and summarise rather than transcribe. Brain imaging work with children and adults has found that handwriting activates networks involved in reading and memory in ways that typing does not. These findings are not settled, and anyone who claims certainty either way is overreaching. Still, when the research is uncertain, the sensible course is to keep a method that has worked for centuries rather than discard it for an experiment on the youngest learners.

There is also the matter of what handwriting does for reading. When a child writes a letter by hand, she must recall its shape and produce it from memory. Typing asks her only to find and press a key, so recognition replaces recall. Studies of young children learning letters have suggested that those who practise forming letters by hand recognise them more reliably than those who only type or trace them on a screen. Joined writing extends this benefit to whole words, because the child feels the spelling pattern as a movement. Many teachers who work with children who struggle with spelling report that the flowing, repeated motion of joined letters helps common patterns to stick. The hand remembers what the eye has not yet secured.

A further argument concerns children who find literacy hardest. It is often said that joined writing is an elitist demand that penalises those with poor motor control. I would argue almost the opposite. Printed letters that float on the page with uneven spacing are very hard to make look tidy, and children with weak fine motor skills often produce writing that is both slow and illegible. A consistent joined style, taught from the beginning with clear rules about where each letter starts and ends, gives these children a system to follow. Letter reversals, which trouble many young writers, become less common when letters have a fixed entry stroke and a fixed exit. Dyslexic children in particular are often taught joined writing deliberately, because it reduces the chance of confusing letters like b and d. The strongest argument for teaching it to everyone is that it helps those who most need help.

Then there is the reading of other people's writing. Joined up script is not a private oddity. It fills the world beyond the classroom. It appears in birthday cards, in notes left on kitchen tables, in the margins of old books and in the letters of grandparents and great grandparents. It appears on historical documents that a child may one day wish to study. A generation that cannot read cursive is a generation cut off from a large part of its own family and national past. Archivists already report that fewer young people can decipher handwritten records. It would be a strange outcome of a technological age if the price of progress were that we could no longer read what our parents wrote to us.

Critics will say that this is nostalgia, and I should answer the charge honestly. There is a version of the case for handwriting that is merely sentimental, a longing for ink stained fingers and copperplate that nobody really needs. I do not defend that. Nobody should be asked to produce elaborate loops, swashes and flourishes, and the marks a school awards for neatness have too often been used to humiliate children whose hands simply work differently. A modern approach to joined writing should be plain, practical and forgiving. It should aim at a legible, fluent personal hand, not a museum piece. Children should be allowed to develop their own style once the basic joins are secure, and teachers should praise readability and speed rather than decoration.

Nor is this an argument against technology. Children should learn to type, and should learn it properly, because keyboard skills are genuinely needed. The point is that the two skills are not rivals. A school day is long enough for both, and a child who writes confidently by hand is usually better placed to take up the keyboard, not worse. What should worry us is not that children use computers but that handwriting is being dropped from the curriculum by default, squeezed out by crowded timetables and by the assumption that someone, somewhere, has proved it unnecessary. No one has proved this. The decision to abandon a skill deserves more evidence than a general feeling that the world has moved on.

Finally, consider the simple matter of dignity and self expression. Handwriting is one of the few places where a child's individuality appears in physical form. A signature, a note to a friend, a diary entry, a line in a card for a sick relative all carry something of the person who made them. Children who are proud of their handwriting tend to take pride in their written work more generally. Those who are never given the tools to write fluently are left with a lifelong, quiet embarrassment, the kind many adults feel when asked to write a few words in front of others. A school that sends a child away able to write a clear, confident, joined up hand has given her something small but lasting.

None of this requires a return to the rigid drills of the past. It requires that schools treat handwriting as a real subject with a sensible sequence. Children should learn print first, then begin joins during the early years of primary school, practising briefly and often rather than in long, punishing sessions. Teachers need training in how to teach it, since many have never been shown. Pupils with particular difficulties need extra support, not exemption. And the aim throughout should be fluency and legibility, so that handwriting becomes a servant of thought rather than an obstacle to it.

The strongest reason to keep teaching joined up handwriting is also the simplest. It works. It helps children write faster, think more freely, remember letters and spellings more securely and read the written world around them. It costs very little and asks only for a pencil and some patience. The burden of proof lies with those who want to remove it, and so far they have offered little more than the observation that keyboards exist. Primary schools should keep the joins.
