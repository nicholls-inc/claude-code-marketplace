The Pump on Broad Street

On the night of 31 August 1854, people in Soho, a crowded district in the West End of London, began falling violently ill. The illness started with sudden cramps and diarrhoea and went on to severe vomiting. Victims lost fluid so fast that their skin turned bluish and wrinkled, and many were dead within a day. This was cholera, a disease Londoners knew and feared. Over the next three days, more than 120 people living within a few streets of each other died. Families fled, and shops closed. By the time the outbreak ended, around 600 people had died.

Most doctors of the time believed cholera spread through "miasma," a poisonous bad air that rose from rotting rubbish, sewage and filth. The theory made some sense to them. Cholera struck hardest in poor, dirty, foul-smelling neighbourhoods, and Soho, with its overflowing cesspits and packed lodging houses, certainly smelled.

John Snow, a doctor who lived a short walk from the outbreak, did not believe the miasma theory. Snow was a respected physician best known for his work with anaesthetics; he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. For several years he had been arguing, with little success, that cholera was caught from something people swallowed and not from the air they breathed. His reasoning was simple: the disease attacked the gut first, so whatever caused it most likely entered through the mouth. He suspected it travelled in water that had been contaminated with sewage. Soho gave him the chance to test the idea.

At the time, few homes in the area had running water. People filled buckets at public pumps on the street, drawing from shallow wells beneath. Snow suspected one pump in particular, which stood on Broad Street at the heart of the cluster of deaths. He took a sample of its water and looked at it, but it looked fairly clean, and he could not see anything harmful. He needed different evidence.

He went to the General Register Office, which kept the records of every death in London, and got a list of the cholera deaths in the district, with addresses. Then he walked the streets, knocked on doors and asked questions. Where did this household get its water? Had the person who died drunk from the Broad Street pump?

A clear pattern emerged. Most of the deaths were in houses closer to the Broad Street pump than to any other pump. Several people who lived nearer to a different pump had died too, but when Snow asked, their families told him they preferred the taste of the Broad Street water and walked the extra distance to get it. Children who lived elsewhere had drunk from the pump on their way to school in the area.

The exceptions were just as telling. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street held more than 500 poor residents, yet only a handful caught cholera. It had its own well. A brewery on Broad Street employed about 70 men, and none of them died. They were given free beer as part of their wages and, according to the owner, never drank water from the pump at all.

The strangest case came from Hampstead, miles away in north London. A widow there had once lived in Broad Street and liked its water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her by cart every day. She drank from a bottle delivered on 31 August and died of cholera two days later. Her niece, who visited and drank the same water, also died. No one else in Hampstead had the disease.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow presented his findings to the local parish officials, the Board of Guardians. They were not entirely convinced, but they agreed to act. The next day the handle was removed from the Broad Street pump, so no one could draw water from it.

Snow himself was careful not to overstate what happened next. The outbreak was already fading by the time the handle came off, partly because so many people had fled the area. Removing the handle may have prevented a second wave, but it did not single-handedly end the epidemic. What mattered was the method: Snow had traced a disease to its source by collecting facts on the ground and following them where they led.

The final piece came from an unlikely ally. Henry Whitehead, a local clergyman, set out to prove Snow wrong, and ended up proving him right. Whitehead found that a baby at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill just before the outbreak began. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and emptied the water into a cesspit in front of the house. Workers later found that the cesspit sat only a few feet from the pump's well and leaked into it.

Snow published a map showing each death as a small black bar stacked outside its house, clustered thickly around the pump. It became one of the most famous images in the history of medicine. Even so, the authorities did not accept his explanation at first, and the miasma theory persisted for years. Snow died in 1858, before his ideas were vindicated. Within a few decades, scientists had identified the bacterium that causes cholera, and cities everywhere had begun to separate drinking water from sewage. Today Snow is regarded as a founder of epidemiology, the study of how diseases spread through populations. A replica of the pump, without its handle, stands in Soho, on what is now called Broadwick Street.
