The Apollo 13 Accident and Return

On the evening of April 11, 1970, three men climbed into a spacecraft atop a Saturn V rocket at Kennedy Space Center. Jim Lovell, the commander, had flown to the Moon before, on Apollo 8, but had never walked on it. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, was a late replacement. Ken Mattingly, originally assigned to the seat, had been grounded days earlier after exposure to German measles, though he never actually fell ill. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was a rookie. The launch the next afternoon, April 11 at 2:13 p.m. Eastern, went almost perfectly. Only a second-stage engine shut down early, and the remaining engines burned longer to make up for it. Nobody worried much. By then, the public had grown bored with the Moon.

That boredom was about to end.

For two days the flight was routine. The crew pulled the lunar module, a spidery craft named Aquarius, out of its housing atop the rocket stage. They checked systems. They even gave a television broadcast on the evening of April 13, showing viewers around the world how comfortably they lived in weightlessness. Most American networks did not carry it. Lovell signed off by wishing everyone a good evening. It was about 55 hours into the mission, and they were roughly 200,000 miles from home.

Then, a little after 9 p.m. Houston time, Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the oxygen tanks in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the command capsule. This was standard. The tanks held super-cold liquid oxygen, which tends to stratify, and the stirring fans kept the readings accurate. Swigert flipped the switch.

Moments later came a bang. The spacecraft shuddered. Swigert reported a problem, and Lovell, in a line that has been paraphrased ever since, said: "Houston, we've had a problem here." The controllers on the ground at first suspected an instrument fault. Then the numbers got worse. Oxygen pressure in tank two fell to zero. Tank one began to leak. Two of the three fuel cells, which made electricity by combining oxygen and hydrogen, began to die. Lovell looked out a window and saw a stream of gas venting into space. He knew what that meant.

The cause, discovered later, lay in a chain of small errors stretching back years. The oxygen tank contained heaters and a thermostat switch designed for 28 volts, but ground equipment at the launch site ran on 65. During a pre-flight test the switch had welded shut and the heaters had cooked the tank's interior for hours, melting the Teflon insulation on the wiring. Nobody noticed. When Swigert turned on the fans, a spark ignited the exposed wires inside an atmosphere of pure oxygen. The tank ruptured and blew a panel off the side of the service module.

The mission was no longer about landing on the Moon. It was about survival.

The command module, Odyssey, was the only part of the ship built for reentry, and it was now running on battery power it would need for the final descent. Flight director Gene Kranz and his team made a decision that defined the next four days. They would use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius was built to carry two men for about two days on the lunar surface. It would now have to sustain three men for four. On the evening of April 13, Lovell and Haise powered up the lunar module while Swigert shut down Odyssey to save its batteries. They crowded into the smaller craft.

The ground crews faced a second problem: how to get home at all. The spacecraft was on a path that would swing around the Moon, which was fortunate. Had the explosion happened after entering lunar orbit, the options would have been worse. But the original trajectory would not bring them back to Earth quickly. They needed to speed up the return. Using Aquarius's descent engine, a rocket meant for landing, they fired a burn about five hours after passing the far side of the Moon. The maneuver cut hours from the trip and aimed them at the Pacific. They set a record, still unbroken, as the farthest humans have ever been from Earth, nearly 250,000 miles away.

Then came the hard part, which was the waiting.

Aquarius was not designed for this. Electricity had to be rationed. The crew powered down nearly everything, running the lunar module on a trickle of power, about as much as a few light bulbs. The cabin grew cold, dropping to near freezing. Water condensed on the walls. Drinking water was rationed to a few ounces per person a day, and Haise developed a kidney infection. They slept little. Lovell later said the cold was the worst of it, though the crew stayed focused.

A more immediate threat was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's air scrubbers used lithium hydroxide canisters, and Aquarius had only enough for two people for two days. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's receptacles were round. As the carbon dioxide level climbed toward dangerous territory, engineers on the ground gathered the same items the crew had aboard: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, a hose from a spacesuit, and duct tape. They built an adapter and read instructions to the crew over the radio. The crew assembled it, and the readings fell. It is one of the best-known improvisations in the history of spaceflight.

Meanwhile Mattingly, the grounded astronaut, worked in a simulator on the ground to develop a procedure for restarting the command module with the tiny amount of power available. The checklist had to be written from scratch. The normal startup sequence drew far more energy than the batteries could spare. His final procedure was tested and then radioed up.

By April 17, the crew faced reentry. Shortly before, they jettisoned the damaged service module and got their first clear look at it. A whole panel was missing, and the crew photographed the wreckage, which gave investigators crucial evidence. Then they moved back into Odyssey, still cold and dark, and released Aquarius, which had saved their lives. The little lander burned up in the atmosphere.

Reentry brings a communications blackout, normally about three minutes. This one stretched longer, and the silence on the ground grew heavy. Controllers watched the clock, and nobody spoke. Four minutes passed. Then, finally, Swigert's voice came through. Three parachutes bloomed over the South Pacific, and Odyssey splashed down near the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima at about 1 p.m. Houston time on April 17, 1970.

All three men were alive. They were tired, dehydrated, and thinner, Lovell having lost about fourteen pounds, but they had survived.

The aftermath was swift. An investigation board, led by Edgar Cortright, traced the failure to the tank's design and handling. NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks, added a third, and rethought how critical systems were tested. The Apollo program continued; Apollo 14 landed on the Moon the following year. But Apollo 13 changed how people thought about it. The mission had failed in its goal and succeeded in a more important one. It showed what people could do under pressure, when a room full of engineers and three tired men worked a problem together. Gene Kranz would later insist that failure was not an option, a phrase later made famous by a film. Whatever the exact words, the spirit was real.
