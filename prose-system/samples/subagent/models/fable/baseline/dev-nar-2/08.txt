Apollo 13: The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 13, 1970, the third American mission meant to land on the Moon was nearly 56 hours into its flight and about 200,000 miles from Earth. The crew of Apollo 13 had just finished a television broadcast that none of the major networks carried live. Moon landings, after two successes, had begun to seem routine. Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier spaceflights, signed off and wished the audience a good evening. With him were Fred Haise, who was to walk on the Moon beside him, and Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, who had joined the crew only two days before launch after Ken Mattingly was exposed to German measles.

A few minutes later, Mission Control in Houston asked Swigert to perform a housekeeping chore: switch on the fans that stirred the supercold oxygen and hydrogen in the service module's tanks. Swigert flipped the switches. Inside oxygen tank number two, damaged wiring sparked, the insulation caught fire, and the pressure climbed until the tank burst.

The crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights came on across the instrument panel. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Asked to repeat, Lovell said, "Houston, we've had a problem."

For some minutes nobody understood how bad it was. Controllers suspected faulty instruments, since so many readings had gone wrong at once. But the numbers held. One oxygen tank read empty and the other was falling. Two of the three fuel cells, which combined oxygen and hydrogen to make the ship's electricity and water, were dead. Then Lovell looked out the window and saw a cloud of gas streaming from the side of the spacecraft. It was their oxygen, venting into space.

The command module, called Odyssey, was dying. Without oxygen, the last fuel cell would fail, and the ship would be left with only the small batteries reserved for reentry. The landing was off. The question now was whether three men could get home at all.

The answer lay in the lunar module, Aquarius, still docked to Odyssey's nose. It had its own oxygen, power, and engine, and it had never been needed for anything but the landing. Flight director Gene Kranz and his team decided to use it as a lifeboat. As Odyssey's power drained away, Haise and Lovell scrambled through the tunnel to bring Aquarius to life, hurrying through a startup procedure that normally took hours. Swigert shut down the command module switch by switch, saving its batteries for the final hours of the flight. He finished with only minutes of power to spare.

Aquarius was built to keep two men alive for about two days. It now had to support three men for four.

Houston next had to choose a route. Turning the ship straight around would require the service module's main engine, and no one knew whether the explosion had damaged it. Kranz chose the longer, safer course: continue to the Moon, swing around its far side, and let lunar gravity send the spacecraft back toward Earth. Because Apollo 13 had earlier left the path that would return it home automatically, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine for about half a minute to get back on it.

On the evening of April 14, the spacecraft passed behind the Moon and out of radio contact. Haise and Swigert, who had never been there, took photographs of the cratered surface sliding by beneath them. Lovell, who had orbited the Moon on Apollo 8, had seen it before and knew he would not be walking on it. Two hours after they came back around, the crew fired the descent engine again, this time for more than four minutes, to speed up the return and shift the splashdown to the Pacific, where recovery ships were waiting.

Then came the long coast home, and with it the problem of supplies. Oxygen was plentiful. Electricity was not, and neither was water, which the lunar module used to cool its electronics. To save both, the crew shut off nearly everything, including the guidance computer and the heaters. Each man cut his water to about six ounces a day, a fraction of a normal ration. The cabin temperature dropped to near freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men slept badly in the cold, and Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever.

A more urgent danger was the air itself. Each breath added carbon dioxide to the cabin, and the lunar module's chemical filters, sized for two men over two days, were being used up. The command module had plenty of fresh canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. In Houston, engineers gathered only the materials the astronauts had on board: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight plan cover, a spacesuit hose, and gray duct tape. They built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew step by step. The astronauts assembled it, and the carbon dioxide level, which had been rising toward dangerous levels, fell within hours.

One more course correction was needed. With the computer off, Lovell had to aim the ship by eye. He lined up the edge of the Earth's sunlit face in a sight on his window while Haise held the ship steady on another axis and Swigert timed the burn with his wristwatch. Fourteen seconds of thrust put them back in the narrow corridor for reentry. Too steep an approach would burn up the capsule; too shallow would send it skipping off the atmosphere.

Meanwhile, teams on the ground were writing a procedure that had never existed: how to restart a cold, damp command module using almost no electricity. Ken Mattingly, who never did catch the measles, spent hours in a simulator working out the order of the switches. The finished checklist was read up to the crew, who copied it by hand, line after line.

On the morning of April 17, the astronauts moved back into Odyssey and powered it up. Nothing shorted out, despite the water coating the panels. They released the service module and got their first look at the damage. "There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," Lovell reported. An entire panel had been blown away, exposing the wreckage inside. Then they cast off Aquarius, the lander that had kept them alive. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," Houston radioed.

One fear remained. The heat shield sat just beyond the blast, and no one could tell whether it had cracked. Odyssey hit the atmosphere at nearly 25,000 miles an hour, and the usual radio blackout began. It normally lasted about three minutes. Three minutes passed, then four. Around the world, an audience far larger than the one that had ignored the launch waited. After roughly a minute and a half of extra silence, Swigert's voice came through, and television cameras showed the capsule under three orange-and-white parachutes.

Apollo 13 splashed down in the South Pacific on April 17, 1970, a few miles from the carrier USS Iwo Jima, less than six days after launch. The three men were exhausted, dehydrated, and thinner, and they were alive. NASA later called the mission a successful failure: it never reached the Moon's surface, but it brought its crew home.
