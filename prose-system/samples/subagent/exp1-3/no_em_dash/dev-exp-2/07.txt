A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to official world time, and the bodies that govern timekeeping have voted to stop adding them.

To see why the adjustment exists, you need to know that there are two ways of measuring time. The first is astronomical. A day is the time the Earth takes to spin once, and the Sun's position in the sky tells us the hour. The second is atomic. Since the 1950s, clocks based on the vibrations of atoms, such as caesium, have counted seconds with astonishing steadiness, drifting by less than a second over many millions of years.

The trouble is that the two do not quite agree. The Earth is a poor timekeeper by comparison. Tidal friction from the Moon slowly brakes its rotation, and earthquakes, shifting ice and the churning of the planet's molten core nudge the spin in unpredictable ways. Over the long run, days have been getting slightly longer, so the planet falls behind the atomic clocks.

In 1972, international authorities introduced a compromise. Coordinated Universal Time, the standard that civil clocks around the world follow, would tick at the steady atomic rate. But whenever it drifted more than 0.9 of a second from the Earth's rotation, a leap second would be inserted, usually on the last day of June or December. Clocks would read 23:59:59, then 23:59:60, then midnight. Twenty-seven have been added so far, the most recent at the end of 2016.

For astronomers and navigators this was tidy. For the computer age it has been a headache. Most software assumes that every minute has exactly 60 seconds. A minute with 61 can confuse systems that time-stamp financial trades, coordinate telecoms networks, or keep satellite navigation in step. Leap seconds have caused outages and glitches at major internet companies, and engineers have long complained that they are rare enough that the code written to handle them is seldom tested. Technology firms have taken to "smearing" the extra second across a day, but they do not all do it the same way, which creates its own inconsistencies.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of the second, voted to stop adding leap seconds by or before 2035. In the meantime, the gap between atomic and astronomical time will be allowed to grow, with a larger tolerance to be set in future. Oddly, the Earth's spin has recently been running slightly faster, which has raised the prospect of a "negative" leap second, something never tried and likely to be even more disruptive.

The change will have little effect on daily life. Noon will still be close to the Sun's peak for generations. Eventually, perhaps in a century or so, the accumulated difference might be corrected with a larger adjustment, such as a minute at once.
