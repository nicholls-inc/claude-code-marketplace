On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three astronauts toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was making his fourth spaceflight and would soon become the most traveled human in history. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was on his first mission. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, had joined the crew only days earlier, when the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, was grounded after exposure to German measles. Swigert had trained hard in the backup role, and he stepped in with barely three days of preparation as a full crew member.

The launch went well, apart from a center engine on the second stage that shut down early. The remaining engines burned longer to make up the difference, and the crew settled into the long coast toward the Moon. Over the next two days the mission felt almost routine. The crew transmitted a television broadcast to the public, giving a tour of the spacecraft. Few networks carried it. Americans had grown used to lunar flights, and interest had faded.

The trouble began on the evening of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the flight and roughly 200,000 miles from home. Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the cone-shaped command module. The tanks held supercooled liquids, and stirring prevented them from settling into layers. Swigert flipped the switch. A little over a minute later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered.

Swigert radioed the now famous words, usually quoted as "Houston, we've had a problem." Lovell repeated the message and added that there was a main bus B undervolt, meaning that electrical power was dropping. Warning lights flickered across the panels. Within minutes the crew saw that one of the two oxygen tanks was empty and the other was leaking fast. Out of a window, Lovell saw a gas venting into space. He told Houston the spacecraft was bleeding something away.

The cause, discovered later, lay in events years before the flight. The oxygen tank had been damaged during ground testing, and its heater thermostats had been wired for a voltage the launch pad could exceed. During a test before launch, the heaters had run far hotter than intended and burned away the insulation on the wiring inside the tank. When Swigert flipped the stirring switch, a spark from the bare wire ignited the material inside. The tank ruptured, and the blast blew off a panel on the side of the service module.

The command module depended on the service module for oxygen, water and electricity. With the tanks gone, the fuel cells that produced power would soon die, and the crew would have only the command module's batteries, which were needed for the final minutes before landing. A landing on the Moon was now out of the question. The only goal left was getting three men home alive.

Flight director Gene Kranz gathered his team in Houston and set the tone. The crew would be brought back, he told them, and nobody would panic. Controllers worked in shifts around the clock. The plan began with a decision to use the lunar module, the small landing craft still attached to the command module, as a lifeboat. It had its own oxygen, power and engine, all designed to support two men for two days on the Moon. Now it would have to support three men for four days.

Lovell and Haise moved into the lunar module, and Swigert followed shortly after. Before leaving the command module, the crew shut it down to save what little battery power remained. The lunar module's systems came to life, and its guidance computer provided the first working navigation. Using it, the crew could fire the descent engine, the one meant to carry them to the lunar surface, to change course.

The first problem was trajectory. Apollo 13 had been aimed on a path that would have swung it around the Moon and back to Earth only if the engine of the service module was working, so the spacecraft was heading for a wide miss. After swinging around the far side of the Moon, the crew fired the descent engine to speed the return. That burn cut the trip time and aimed the capsule at a spot in the Pacific Ocean. Later, a smaller burn corrected the course again.

Then came the problems of staying alive. The lunar module was cramped, and as the crew exhaled, carbon dioxide began to build. The lunar module's air scrubbers used round lithium hydroxide canisters to absorb the gas, and they were filling up. The command module had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's scrubbers took only round ones. As carbon dioxide levels climbed toward dangerous levels, engineers in Houston gathered the same items that the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from flight manuals, a spacesuit hose and duct tape. Within hours they had designed a contraption that joined the square canister to the round opening. They read the instructions up to the crew, step by step, and the crew built it. The carbon dioxide levels dropped.

The cold became the next enemy. To save power the crew switched off nearly everything, including the heaters. The temperature inside fell to near freezing, and condensation dripped off the walls. The men slept little, if at all. Water was rationed to a few ounces per person each day, and Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection from dehydration. They wore their spacesuits and moon boots for warmth, and ate cold food.

Meanwhile, at the Johnson Space Center, engineers faced another hard question. The command module would have to be powered up from nothing before reentry, and nobody had ever tried a start-up sequence with such little power. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded, worked in the simulator to develop a procedure that would use as little electricity as possible. He tested it again and again until it worked, and then read it up to the crew.

On April 17, as the spacecraft closed in on Earth, the crew prepared for the final stage. They jettisoned the service module first, and for the first time saw the damage. A whole side panel was gone, and the equipment inside was a mess of twisted metal and dangling wires. Lovell described it to Houston and took photographs. Then they climbed back into the command module, said goodbye to the lunar module that had saved them, and released it to burn up in the atmosphere.

Reentry came with a long communications blackout, normally about three minutes. The seconds ticked by with no word. Controllers waited, silent, and the blackout stretched well past the expected time. Finally, a voice came through, and Swigert said, "OK, Joe." Cheering erupted in Mission Control. The parachutes opened, and the capsule dropped into the South Pacific near Samoa, about four miles from the recovery ship, USS Iwo Jima.

Apollo 13 was called a successful failure. The mission never reached the Moon, but the effort to return the crew showed how much careful engineering, calm leadership and improvisation could achieve. The spacecraft was redesigned, with the oxygen tank wiring and thermostats fixed and a third oxygen tank placed elsewhere. The program went on to fly four more times, and three of those flights landed on the Moon. For the three men who rode home in the cold, the lasting memory was how many people on the ground worked to bring them back.
