In late August 1854, cholera arrived in Soho, a crowded district of London, and it struck with terrifying speed. Within three days, more than a hundred people living near Broad Street were dead. By the end of the first week of September, the death toll had climbed to several hundred. Cholera is a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhea, and it can drain a healthy adult of fluid so quickly that death comes within a day. Nobody in London knew what caused it.

The prevailing belief was that cholera spread through bad air, which doctors called miasma. The idea was that rotting waste and filthy streets gave off poisonous vapors, and that people fell ill by breathing them in. It was a reasonable guess for the time, since the worst outbreaks did occur in the foulest neighborhoods. Germs had not yet been shown to cause disease, and the microscope had not yet given most doctors reason to doubt the vapors.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked a short walk from Broad Street, had doubted this theory for years. He had become well known for his work on anesthesia, and he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. But he had also been studying cholera since the epidemic of 1848 and 1849. He noticed that the illness began with symptoms in the gut, not the lungs. If people were breathing a poison, he reasoned, the first signs should appear in the chest and throat. Instead, the disease seemed to attack the digestive system first, which suggested that people were swallowing something. He concluded that cholera passed from one person to another through contaminated water.

When the Broad Street outbreak began, Snow had an opportunity to test his idea. He started by gathering facts. He obtained the official list of deaths from the registrar and visited the homes of the victims, asking what they had eaten and drunk. Door by door, he built a picture of where the dead had lived.

At that time, most households in Soho had no running water. People fetched what they needed from public pumps in the street, and the pump on Broad Street was well known and popular. Snow plotted the deaths on a map of the neighborhood, drawing a small black bar at each address for every person who had died there. The bars clustered thickly around the Broad Street pump, like a swarm gathering around a single point.

The pattern was striking, but Snow knew that a map alone could be argued away. So he went looking for exceptions, and the exceptions proved the most convincing part of his case. A workhouse nearby housed about five hundred people, yet only a handful died. Snow discovered that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped almost untouched. Its workers were given free beer as part of their pay, and they seem to have rarely drunk from the pump.

Other cases pointed the same way. Snow learned of a woman who lived far away in Hampstead and who had died of cholera. She had once lived in Soho, and she liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it carried to her each day. A niece who visited her and drank the same water also fell ill and died. Neither lived anywhere near the outbreak, and neither had breathed the air of Soho. The one thing they shared with the victims on Broad Street was the water.

On the evening of September 7, Snow presented his findings to the local board of guardians, the officials who managed the parish. He asked them to remove the handle from the pump, which would make it impossible to draw water. The guardians were skeptical, but they agreed. The handle came off the next day.

It would be tidy to say that the epidemic ended at that moment, but the truth is less dramatic. The outbreak had already been fading, because many residents had fled the neighborhood and many of the most vulnerable were already dead. Snow himself acknowledged this. Still, the removal of the handle became the most famous act in the history of public health.

The deeper discovery came later. A local clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who had at first dismissed Snow's idea, began his own investigation of the neighborhood. He was determined to find the starting point. He learned that a baby girl living at 40 Broad Street had died of cholera just before the outbreak exploded. Her mother had washed the child's soiled diapers and emptied the water nearby. Inspectors later found that the cesspool at that house sat very close to the well, and that the brickwork lining the well was cracked and leaking. Waste from a single sick infant had seeped into the water that hundreds of people drank.

Official bodies at the time still rejected Snow's conclusions, and they continued to favor the idea of bad air. Snow died in 1858, only a few years later, without seeing his view accepted. Yet his method outlived the dispute. He had counted cases, mapped them, compared the sick with the healthy, and followed the evidence even where it contradicted received wisdom. Later scientists confirmed that cholera is caused by a bacterium spread through contaminated water, and Snow is now remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the science of how diseases spread through populations.
