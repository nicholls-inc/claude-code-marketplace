Lithium-ion cells are charged with a two-stage protocol called constant-current/constant-voltage (CC-CV). The slowdown near full is a direct consequence of the voltage limit and of how the cell's internal resistance and diffusion behave.

Background: a cell is two porous electrodes (graphite anode, metal-oxide cathode) soaked in electrolyte, separated by a membrane that passes ions but not electrons. Charging forces lithium ions out of the cathode, across the electrolyte, and into the graphite lattice (intercalation), while electrons travel through the external circuit. The cell's open-circuit voltage (OCV) rises with state of charge (SoC), from about 3.0 V empty to about 4.2 V full for a typical cobalt-based cell. The charger cannot exceed roughly 4.2 V, because higher voltages drive electrolyte decomposition, lithium plating on the anode, and cathode degradation.

Stage 1, constant current (CC): the charger holds a fixed current, typically 0.5C to 1C (C-rate is current divided by capacity, so 1C charges in about an hour). The terminal voltage is not the OCV. It is OCV plus the voltage drop across internal resistance, V = OCV + I*R, plus polarisation from concentration gradients. To hold current as OCV climbs, the charger raises terminal voltage until it reaches the 4.2 V ceiling. At that point the cell is typically 70 to 80 percent charged.

Stage 2, constant voltage (CV): the charger now holds 4.2 V and lets current fall on its own. Since V = OCV + I*R + polarisation is pinned, any rise in OCV must be paid for by a drop in I. As the cell fills, OCV approaches 4.2 V, leaving less voltage headroom to drive current. Current decays roughly exponentially, like an RC circuit settling. Charging terminates when current falls to a threshold, commonly 0.05C to 0.1C.

Why the taper is physically necessary, not only a software choice: the voltage limit is a safety and lifetime boundary, so the charger cannot push harder. Meanwhile, lithium concentration at the particle surface in the graphite saturates first, because diffusion inside solid particles is slow. The interior lags, creating a gradient. Surface saturation raises local potential (the anode surface approaches 0 V versus lithium metal, where plating becomes thermodynamically possible), so forcing high current into a nearly full anode risks plating metallic lithium, which reduces capacity and can form dendrites. Tapering current gives diffusion time to equalise concentrations. The OCV curve also flattens or steepens in places (graphite staging), which changes how much voltage headroom each increment of charge consumes.

Practical consequence: the CC stage delivers most of the capacity quickly, while the final 20 to 30 percent takes a disproportionate share of the time. Fast-charging systems therefore often stop short of full or use stepped current profiles, and temperature-dependent limits reduce current further in the cold, where diffusion and plating margins are worst.
