The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted three men away from Florida on what was meant to be America's third landing on the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was the most experienced astronaut alive, making his fourth spaceflight. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot, new to space but a gifted engineer. The command module pilot was Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was pulled from the flight at the last moment. By now the Moon seemed almost routine to the public. Two crews had already walked on it, and television networks had stopped carrying the coverage live. That would change within three days.

The crew named their command ship Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. For the first two days the flight went smoothly. They coasted outward, tested systems, and even sent a television broadcast to Earth showing the inside of the cabin. Lovell signed off by wishing viewers a good evening from the spacecraft. Mission Control in Houston was relaxed enough that controllers were mostly watching for small problems.

On the evening of April 13, about 56 hours into the flight, the ground asked the crew to stir the supercritical oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine task meant to keep the cryogenic fluids from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A few moments later there was a loud bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Warning lights lit up. Swigert reported, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Houston asked for a repeat, and Lovell answered, "Houston, we've had a problem."

The cause lay in oxygen tank number two. Before launch, a ground test had left the tank hard to empty, and technicians boiled off the oxygen using heaters running on the wrong voltage. That damaged the wire insulation inside the tank without anyone knowing. When Swigert's switch sent current through the stirring fans, a spark ignited the damaged insulation in a tank full of pure oxygen. The tank burst, and the blast tore away a side panel of the service module and crippled the neighboring tank. The spacecraft was losing oxygen to space, and with it the fuel cells that supplied electricity and drinking water to the command module.

Lovell looked out a window and saw a stream of gas venting into space. He told Houston the news in a flat voice. It was clear that the Moon was no longer the goal. The only goal now was getting home.

In Houston, Flight Director Gene Kranz gathered his team and gave them a task that became famous in the retelling. The controllers would work the problem methodically and not make it worse. Odyssey was dying, with its batteries and oxygen draining. But attached to it was Aquarius, a lander built to carry two men to the lunar surface for about two days. It had its own oxygen, power, and water, and it could serve as a lifeboat. The crew moved into it, and engineers began working out how to make a vehicle built for two men support three people for four days.

The first question was the route. The spacecraft was on a path that would swing around the Moon and, with the right push, come back toward Earth. After a first correction burn that put them on a free return path, the controllers decided to fire the lander's descent engine again after the craft rounded the far side of the Moon. That burn, about two hours after closest approach, sped up the trip and aimed them at the Pacific Ocean. While passing behind the Moon, cut off from radio contact, the crew became the farthest humans ever from Earth, nearly 250,000 miles away, a record that still stands.

The journey back was hard in ways that went beyond the engineering. To conserve power, almost everything was shut down. The cabin cooled until the temperature hovered near freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The men slept little and sometimes shivered. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day. Haise developed a fever from an infection, and Lovell and Swigert were dehydrated and exhausted. Meanwhile the three of them spent hours following instructions radioed up from the ground.

The most dramatic problem came from the air itself. Exhaled carbon dioxide has to be filtered out of a cabin, and the lander's canisters were designed for two people for a short stay. They were filling up quickly. The command module had spare canisters, but they were square, while the lander's system took round ones. Engineers on the ground gathered the items the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard, a sock, hoses, and duct tape, and worked out a way to build an adapter. They read the instructions up to the crew, who built it in the cramped cabin. It worked, and the carbon dioxide level fell.

Another trial was navigation. The lander's guidance system was not made to steer the combined spacecraft, and with Odyssey powered down, the crew could not rely on the usual alignment methods. For one critical course correction, they lined the ship up by keeping the sunlit edge of Earth in a window. Back in Houston, Mattingly, the astronaut who had been kept off the flight, worked in a simulator with engineers to develop a procedure to restart the command module from cold using only the tiny amount of battery power available. Nobody knew whether the cold, damp electronics would wake up.

On April 17, as the spacecraft closed on Earth, the crew released the damaged service module. For the first time they could see what the explosion had done. An entire panel was gone, and equipment hung out of the side. Lovell reported the damage to Houston in quiet disbelief. Then they let go of Aquarius, the little ship that had kept them alive, and prepared to enter the atmosphere in Odyssey alone.

Reentry brought one more moment of suspense. The blackout of radio contact during the fiery plunge normally lasts about three minutes. This time it stretched longer, and the silence in Mission Control grew heavy as the seconds passed. Controllers called to the crew without a reply. Then, finally, Swigert's voice came through. Three parachutes opened in the sky above the South Pacific, and the capsule dropped into the water near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship Iwo Jima.

The crew had been gone nearly six days. They were thin and tired, but alive. The Moon landing had been lost, but the rescue became one of the most celebrated achievements of the space program. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tanks, and later Apollo missions flew with changes that made the spacecraft safer. The mission is often called a "successful failure," and the phrase fits. The explosion ended the landing, but ingenuity, teamwork, and calm decisions on the ground and in space brought three men safely home.
