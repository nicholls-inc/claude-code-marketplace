Free the Curb: Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet law shaping the ground beneath your feet. It is the minimum parking requirement. It says that every new apartment building, restaurant, church and corner shop must provide a set number of off-street spaces, whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these rules, and the sooner the better.

Start with how the numbers got there. Most requirements were not drawn from careful study of local demand. Planners often copied them from neighboring towns or from a handbook that tallied parking at a handful of suburban sites, usually measured at peak times in places with free parking. The result is a rule of thumb dressed up as science. A requirement of two spaces per home or one space per 250 square feet of retail sounds precise, but it rests on guesses that no one has to defend. When the guess is wrong, the building pays for it anyway.

And it pays dearly. A single surface space costs thousands of dollars to build, and a space in a garage or underground can cost tens of thousands. That cost does not vanish. It is folded into rents, home prices and the menu prices at the restaurant on the ground floor. Renters who own no car still subsidize the cars of others. Researchers who study this have found that mandated parking can add a meaningful share to the cost of an apartment. In a housing crisis, we are forcing builders to pour concrete for vehicles before they can build rooms for people.

The rules also decide what gets built at all. Many small lots simply cannot fit a building plus the required spaces. A duplex that would have worked fine is shelved. A family bakery that would have reused an old storefront is told it needs six more spaces than the site can hold. Old downtowns, which grew before these rules existed, are often illegal to rebuild under today's standards. Think about that. The streets people love best, with their tight blocks and mixed uses, could not be built again without a variance.

Defenders raise a fair worry. If we remove the requirement, will the neighborhood fill up with cars looking for somewhere to stop? It is a reasonable fear, but it misreads what repeal actually does. Repeal does not ban parking. It removes the mandate. Developers who think their tenants and customers need spaces will still build them, because a building without parking where parking is needed will struggle to attract anyone. What changes is that the decision moves from a bureaucrat with a table of ratios to the person who knows the site, the market and the money. Some projects will build more parking than the old code demanded. Many will build less. Both outcomes are healthier than a single number imposed on everyone.

Cities that have tried this offer encouraging evidence. Buffalo ended its citywide requirements in 2017 and saw developers keep building parking where it made sense while freeing up infill projects that had been stuck. Minneapolis, Seattle, Austin and a growing list of others have scaled back or eliminated mandates in large parts of town. Several states have gone further. The sky did not fall. Projects got easier to finance, and the mix of buildings grew more varied.

Then there is the question of what parking mandates do to the places we share. Every required space is land that cannot hold a home, a garden or a shop. Large lots break up the street into a gap between buildings, which makes walking less pleasant and transit harder to serve. Stormwater runs off acres of asphalt and floods nearby streets. The paved surface bakes in summer heat. A city that wants to cut emissions cannot also insist, in its zoning code, that every new building be designed around the private car.

Fairness matters here as well. Not every household owns a vehicle, and those that do not tend to be poorer, older or younger. A rule that forces them to cover the cost of storage for cars they do not drive is a hidden tax that falls hardest on those with the least. Removing it gives people a choice. Someone who wants a cheaper apartment without a space can find one. Someone who wants a space can pay for it separately, which is exactly how we treat most other goods.

Better tools exist for managing the curb itself. Cities can price on-street parking so a space is usually open on a busy block. They can offer residential permits where spillover is a real problem, and they can use the revenue to improve the neighborhood. These approaches handle scarcity directly, instead of burying the cost in every building permit and hoping the numbers come out right.

Cities do not need to replace one rigid rule with another. They should trust builders, tenants and customers to weigh what parking is worth, and they should manage public streets with prices and permits rather than mandates. Housing would be cheaper, downtowns would be easier to restore, and land would go to its best use instead of its required one.

The minimum parking requirement was an experiment that never earned its place in the code. It is time to end it.
