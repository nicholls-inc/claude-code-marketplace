# Agreeing on a Secret in Public

## The problem

Alice and Bob have never met. The only channel between them is one that anyone can listen to: a phone line, a web connection, or a conversation across a busy street. Alice wants to agree on a secret number with Bob, which they will then use to scramble their later messages. If she says the number aloud, an eavesdropper hears it too. She could lock it in a box, but then she needs a key, and getting the key to Bob needs a secure channel, which is the thing they lack. This looks like a circle with no way in.

For most of history, people assumed two strangers could not do this. In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a method that breaks the circle. It is now called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, and it is one of the foundations of public-key cryptography. The eavesdropper is conventionally called Eve, and that name is used below.

## A paint analogy

Picture a starting colour that everyone can see, say yellow. Alice picks a secret colour, red, and mixes it into the yellow to get orange. Bob picks a secret blue and mixes it into yellow to get green. They announce their mixtures publicly: Alice shouts "orange" and Bob shouts "green".

Now Alice takes Bob's green and stirs in her secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange and stirs in his secret blue. Both end with the same brown, because a mixture of red, yellow and blue is the same whichever order the colours were added in. Eve heard yellow, orange and green. She knows the brown came from all three secret ingredients, but she cannot easily work out which two colours were added.

The analogy captures the idea, but it has a flaw. Paint mixing is not exact, and "unmixing" is not a precisely defined problem that anyone can check. The real method needs an operation that is exact, quick to carry out, and hard to reverse in a way that can be pinned down. Clock arithmetic supplies this.

## Clock arithmetic

Anyone who has read a clock knows that 9 hours after 5 o'clock is 2 o'clock. Fourteen wraps round a 12-hour clock to 2. Mathematicians write this as 14 mod 12 = 2, where "mod" means "the remainder after dividing by". The same idea works for any size of clock. Choose a number p, and every answer is the remainder after dividing by p.

Now take a base number g and raise it to a power a, which means multiplying g by itself a times. Written g^a mod p, the results jump around the clock in a way that looks random once p is large. Computing this is easy. Even when the exponent has hundreds of digits, a computer can work out g^a mod p in a fraction of a second, using repeated squaring: square the number, square again, and multiply together the right pieces.

Reversing the process is different. Suppose someone gives you g and p, and tells you that g^a mod p = 8. Finding a is called the discrete logarithm problem. For a small clock you can simply try exponents one at a time. For a clock with hundreds of digits, no method is known that finishes in reasonable time on ordinary computers. The calculation is easy in one direction and hard in the other, and that asymmetry is what public-key cryptography is built on.

## The exchange, step by step

Here is the method with small numbers, so it can be followed by hand. Both sides agree in public on a prime p = 23 and a base g = 5.

Alice picks a secret number a = 6. She computes A = 5^6 mod 23. A shortcut helps: 5^2 = 25, which leaves remainder 2 mod 23. Then 5^4 = 2 × 2 = 4, and 5^6 = 4 × 2 = 8. So A = 8.

Bob picks a secret number b = 15. He computes B = 5^15 mod 23, which works out to 19.

Alice sends 8 to Bob in the open. Bob sends 19 to Alice in the open.

Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power: 19^6 mod 23 = 2.

Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power: 8^15 mod 23 = 2.

Both arrive at 2. The reason is in the exponents. Alice's calculation is really (5^15)^6, which equals 5^(15 × 6). Bob's is (5^6)^15, which equals 5^(6 × 15). Multiplication does not care about order, so the two exponents are both 90, and both sides are computing 5^90 mod 23. Each person used only their own secret and the other's public number, yet they end up with the same value. That value, 2, is the shared secret.

Eve has seen 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret she must recover either a or b, which means solving 5^a mod 23 = 8. With a modulus of 23 this is trivial, since she can test all 22 possibilities in seconds. That is why the example uses tiny numbers only to show the mechanics.

Real systems use primes with about 600 decimal digits, or an equivalent level of strength from a different mathematical setting called elliptic curves. There are around 10^600 possible exponents to try. The number of atoms in the observable universe is roughly 10^80, so brute-force search is hopeless. No known classical method avoids that search for well-chosen numbers.

## Limits

There are two important caveats.

The first is identity. The exchange by itself does not prove who you are talking to. An attacker sitting in the middle can run one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, relaying numbers between them, and end up sharing a key with each. Real systems prevent this by having each side sign its public number with a key whose owner has been independently verified. Web browsers do this using certificates.

The second is the assumption of hardness. Security rests on the belief that discrete logarithms are hard to compute. A sufficiently large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve them efficiently, which is why standards bodies are developing replacement methods, usually called post-quantum cryptography.

## What happens next

In practice, the shared number is not used directly as the key for every message. Both sides feed it through a hashing function, which turns it into a fixed-length key of the right size. That key then drives a fast symmetric cipher, such as AES, which encrypts the actual conversation. The public-key step is slow and is used only once, at the start, to agree on the secret. The rest of the conversation uses the faster method.

So two people who have never met can agree on a secret in full view of an eavesdropper. Each keeps a private number, shares only a public transformation of it, and combines the other's public value with their own private one. The arithmetic guarantees they reach the same result, and the difficulty of reversing the transformation keeps Eve from doing the same.
