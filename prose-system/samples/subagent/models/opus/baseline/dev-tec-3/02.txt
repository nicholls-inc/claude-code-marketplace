How a Lithium-Ion Cell Charges, and Why the Last 20% Is Slow

A lithium-ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions between two host materials. During charge, an external source drives electrons from the cathode through the external circuit to the anode. The cathode is usually a layered metal oxide such as NMC, or iron phosphate (LFP), and the anode is usually graphite. To keep each electrode electrically neutral, lithium ions leave the cathode lattice, cross the electrolyte and separator, and slot between the graphite layers. This is called intercalation. In normal operation no metal is deposited; the ions move between two crystal structures.

The cell's open-circuit voltage (OCV) rises with state of charge. For an NMC cell it goes from about 3.0 V empty to 4.2 V full. When current flows, the terminal voltage sits above the OCV by an overpotential, which has three parts: ohmic drop across the electrolyte and contacts, charge-transfer resistance at particle surfaces, and concentration polarization from ions that cannot diffuse fast enough. To first order, V_terminal ≈ OCV(SOC) + I·R_eff.

Chargers therefore use a constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV) profile. In the CC phase the charger pushes a fixed current, often 0.5C to 1C (1C is the current that would fill the cell in one hour), and the terminal voltage climbs. When it reaches the maximum allowed voltage, the charger switches to CV mode: it holds the terminal voltage fixed and lets the current fall. Charging ends when the current drops to a small threshold, commonly C/20.

The slowdown comes from the voltage ceiling. In CV mode the current is roughly (V_max − OCV)/R_eff. As the cell fills, OCV approaches V_max, the headroom shrinks and the current decays. A capacitor charging through a resistor toward a fixed supply behaves the same way. The CC phase typically delivers 70–80% of capacity, and the CV tail delivers the rest but can take as long again.

Two failure mechanisms set the ceiling. At the cathode, voltages above the rated maximum oxidize the electrolyte and destabilize the lattice, which causes gassing, heat and capacity loss. At the anode, nearly full graphite sits close to 0 V versus lithium metal. A large current adds enough overpotential to push it below zero, and lithium then deposits as metal (plating) instead of intercalating. Plating permanently uses up lithium and can grow dendrites that short the cell. Solid-state diffusion makes this worse: particle surfaces saturate before their cores, so a high current near full charge mostly raises surface concentration and polarization rather than storing more charge.

Cold slows both diffusion and reaction kinetics, so battery management systems cut charge current at low temperature. Because of the CV tail, fast-charging specifications usually quote 10–80% times rather than 0–100%.
