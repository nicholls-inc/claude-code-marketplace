On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, a day unusually warm for a Boston winter, a steel tank on Commercial Street in the city's North End gave way and released more than two million gallons of molasses onto the neighborhood. The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, which stored the syrup there so it could be fermented and distilled into industrial alcohol, a trade whose demand had been swollen by the war and by the coming of Prohibition. The tank stood about fifty feet high and ninety feet across, it had been built in haste, and it had never been properly tested; when it was first filled, it leaked so badly that the company chose to paint it brown to hide the seepage instead of repairing it, and neighbors who learned to collect the dripping syrup in pails seem to have become accustomed to the groaning and shuddering of the thing.

Around half past noon, as workers ate lunch and children played near the waterfront, the tank ruptured with a sound that witnesses compared to a machine gun, as the rivets shot out of the steel, and a wave of molasses perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high moved outward at something like thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed buildings, lifted a freight car and pushed it aside, and tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports just as a train was approaching, so that the train stopped with its front wheels at the edge of the gap. Twenty-one people died, some drowned in the syrup and others crushed by debris, and around a hundred and fifty were injured; a number of horses perished as well, and the firehouse nearby was pushed off its foundation, with firemen trapped in the wreckage.

Rescuers found that the cold air made the molasses thicken, so that anyone caught in it was held fast, and the sticky mass, a couple of feet deep in places, slowed the work of the police, soldiers, sailors from a docked ship, and Red Cross workers who waded in to reach the victims. The search for bodies continued for days, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer, while the smell of molasses is said to have lingered in the neighborhood for decades afterward on hot days.

Because the company had blamed anarchists, claiming that the tank had been bombed, the matter went to a lawsuit that became one of the first major class actions in Massachusetts, and an auditor named Hugh Ogden spent three years hearing from some three thousand witnesses and a great many experts. He concluded that the tank had been structurally unsound, with steel too thin and a design that had never been checked by anyone qualified, and the company ended up paying out roughly a million dollars in settlements, much of it going to the families of the dead in sums of a few thousand dollars each. The disaster is often credited with helping to push Massachusetts toward requiring that engineers and architects approve and sign off on large construction, and so the flood, which seems almost comic in the telling, left behind a quiet but lasting change in how buildings are regulated.
