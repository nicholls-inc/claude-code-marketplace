In the late summer of 1854, a terror settled over Soho, a crowded, noisy district of London. Within days, hundreds of people were dying of cholera. The disease struck fast. A healthy man could be seized by violent vomiting and diarrhoea in the morning and be dead by nightfall, his body drained of water, his skin turned blue and wrinkled.

Nobody knew what caused it. The reigning belief, held by most doctors and officials, was that cholera spread through "miasma," a poisonous vapour rising from rotting rubbish, sewers and filth. Bad air, in other words. London certainly had plenty of it. The city stank.

John Snow, a physician who lived a few streets away, did not believe it.

Snow was an unusual man. Born the son of a labourer in York, he had risen through sheer ability to become one of London's most respected doctors. He was a pioneer of anaesthesia, and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He drank no alcohol and ate carefully. And he had been thinking about cholera for years. In 1849, during an earlier epidemic, he had published a short pamphlet arguing that the illness was not carried in the air at all. It was swallowed. Something in contaminated water, he suggested, passed from the sick to the healthy.

Almost no one had taken him seriously. Now he had a chance to prove it.

The outbreak was concentrated in a few streets around Broad Street, and Snow began where any good detective would: with the facts on the ground. He walked the neighbourhood. He knocked on doors. He asked who had died, where they lived, and what they had been drinking. It was slow, grim work, carried out among grieving families while the epidemic was still raging.

Gradually, a pattern emerged. The deaths clustered around a public water pump on Broad Street. Households near it were devastated. Those farther away were far less affected. To make the pattern visible, Snow drew a map of the area and marked each death with a small black bar at the address where it occurred. The bars piled up around the pump like iron filings around a magnet.

But a cluster alone proved little. Snow went looking for the exceptions, and the exceptions were what convinced him.

Nearby stood a workhouse housing more than five hundred poor people. Only five of them died. Why? The workhouse had its own well, and so its inmates never needed the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street had no deaths among its workers at all. The men were given free beer, and they also had their own supply of water. Meanwhile, far away in Hampstead, a widow died of cholera. She had once lived in Soho, and so adored the taste of the Broad Street water that she had it carried to her in bottles. Her niece, who drank from the same bottles, died as well.

Snow also tracked down people who had died despite living far from the pump, and found that most had sent a child there to fetch water, or passed it daily on the way to school.

By early September he was certain. On the evening of the 7th he went to the local board of guardians, the men responsible for the parish, and laid out his evidence. They were not wholly persuaded. But they were desperate, and the case was strong enough to act upon. The next day, the handle was removed from the Broad Street pump. Without it, no one could draw water.

The epidemic was already fading by then. Many residents had fled, and the worst of the deaths had come in the first few days. So historians still argue about how much the removal of the handle actually saved. That is an honest uncertainty, and it should be stated plainly. But the story does not end there, because Snow was not finished.

He and a local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, kept digging. Whitehead, who had initially been sceptical of Snow's theory, knew the neighbourhood intimately and began to hunt for the original source of the infection. He found it. A baby girl living at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with diarrhoea just before the outbreak, and died. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies in a bucket and emptied the water into a cesspool beside the house. That cesspool, it turned out, was leaking, and it lay only a few feet from the well.

The contamination was traced to the very doorstep.

Even so, the authorities were slow to accept the result. A official inquiry concluded that the water was not to blame, and the miasma theory held on for years. Snow died in 1858, aged only forty-five, never having seen his idea triumph.

Yet it did triumph. In the decades that followed, microbiologists identified the bacterium that causes cholera, and the link to contaminated water was confirmed beyond doubt. Cities built clean water supplies and proper sewers. Snow's map became one of the most famous images in the history of science. He is now remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations.

What he really showed was a method: count carefully, map the facts, hunt for the exceptions, and follow the evidence even when everyone around you insists it is wrong.
