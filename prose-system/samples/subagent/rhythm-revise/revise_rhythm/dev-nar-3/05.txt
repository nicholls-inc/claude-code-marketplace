On a mild January afternoon in 1919, the North End of Boston, a crowded working-class neighborhood of Italian and Irish immigrants, was going about its business. Along the waterfront stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet high and ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held more than two million gallons of crude molasses, shipped up from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol and, in a nation about to go dry under Prohibition, into munitions and rum.

The tank had been rushed into service in 1915, and nobody had cared much for its construction. Its steel plates were thin, its rivets poorly set, and it was never properly tested; when it was first filled, it groaned and leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the dripping syrup in cups. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown, which hid the leaks from view without stopping them. Warm weather had arrived only a day earlier, after a bitter cold snap, and a fresh shipment of molasses had just been pumped in, warming and expanding the load.

At about 12:30 on January 15, 1919, residents heard a sound like a machine gun as rivets shot out of the tank, and then it burst. A wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high at its crest, by some accounts, surged outward at around thirty-five miles an hour, carrying chunks of steel and a roar that people said they felt in their chests. It crushed buildings, lifted a train off its elevated tracks, and tore a firehouse from its foundation. Men, women, children and horses were caught in a syrup that grew thicker and stickier as the cold air worked on it. Rescuers waded in to their knees, and some of those trapped could not be pulled free before they suffocated.

Twenty-one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured, and the cleanup took weeks, with sailors from a nearby ship, police, and Red Cross workers hosing the streets with salt water from fireboats, then covering them in sand and sawdust. The harbor ran brown until summer, and for decades afterward residents claimed that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled sweet.

The aftermath was as important as the disaster itself: the company blamed anarchists and claimed the tank had been bombed, a convenient story in the Red Scare climate of the time, while survivors and victims' families filed one of the first major class-action suits in Massachusetts. A court-appointed auditor heard testimony for years, and in 1925 he found that the tank had been structurally unsound, rejecting the sabotage theory. The company paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements, a few thousand dollars per death.

The flood left a lasting legacy in law and engineering, helping push Massachusetts and other states to require architects and engineers to certify and sign off on large structures, and strengthening the idea that companies must answer for cutting corners with public safety. A disaster that sounds almost comic became a grim lesson in how carelessness, not villainy, can cost lives.
