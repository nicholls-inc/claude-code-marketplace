A leap second is a one-second adjustment added to clocks every so often so that the time we use stays in step with the spinning Earth.

Two kinds of time are involved. For most of history, people defined the second as a slice of the day, which is the time it takes the Earth to turn once. Since 1967 the second has instead been defined by atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of caesium atoms with astonishing steadiness. These clocks are far more regular than the planet. The Earth wobbles, and its rotation is nudged by tides, the movement of molten rock in its core, and the shifting of oceans and ice. Over the long run, tidal friction has tended to slow the spin slightly, so the day has been growing a little longer.

The result is that atomic time and Earth time slowly drift apart. In 1972 the world adopted a compromise called Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC. It ticks at the steady atomic rate, but whenever the gap with Earth time threatens to grow beyond 0.9 seconds, an extra second is inserted at the end of June or December. The last minute of that day then lasts 61 seconds, and clocks read 23:59:60. Since 1972, 27 leap seconds have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016.

So why stop? The main reason is that leap seconds cause trouble for computers. Most software assumes that every minute has 60 seconds. A minute with 61 can confuse systems that time-stamp financial trades, coordinate telecoms networks, or keep large data centres in agreement. Some outages in past years have been blamed on mishandled leap seconds. Companies have devised their own workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across a whole day, but these differ from one firm to the next, which creates new inconsistencies. Because the event is rare and unpredictable, it is also poorly tested.

Another factor is that the Earth has lately been spinning slightly faster, not slower. That raises the odd prospect of a negative leap second, where a second is removed. Such a thing has never been tried and would be even riskier for software.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the metric system, voted to abandon leap seconds by or before 2035. Until then, the gap between atomic time and Earth time will be allowed to grow well beyond the current limit, with a new tolerance to be decided. Astronomers, who still need to track the Earth's true position, will have to make their own corrections. For everyone else, clocks will simply run on, uninterrupted. The gap is expected to become noticeable only after a century or more, and a correction could be considered then.
