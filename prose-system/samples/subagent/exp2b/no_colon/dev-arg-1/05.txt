Let the Lots Go Empty, Not the Streets

Every city that requires a minimum number of parking spaces for each new building is running a quiet experiment in central planning, and the results are in. We have been forcing developers to build parking nobody asked for, and the bill lands on all of us. Cities should repeal minimum parking requirements entirely.

Start with how these rules came about. Mid-century planners took a handbook of parking demand figures, mostly gathered at suburban sites with free parking and little transit, and wrote them into law. A restaurant needs one space per hundred square feet. An apartment needs two spaces per unit. Nobody tested whether those numbers matched real demand in a dense neighborhood, a walkable downtown, or a building full of people who do not own cars. The numbers became law anyway, and they have shaped our cities ever since.

The first problem is cost. A surface space takes up roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles, and structured parking can cost tens of thousands of dollars per space to build. Underground garages cost far more. That money does not vanish. It gets folded into rents and sale prices, so a tenant who owns no car still pays for a space they never use. Researchers who have studied this find that bundled parking can add a meaningful share to the cost of an apartment. In a housing crisis, mandating an expensive amenity for every unit is a strange way to make homes affordable.

The second problem is land. Parking requirements eat the ground that could hold homes, shops, and offices. A small lot that could fit a four-unit building might only support two once the parking is accounted for, or might not pencil out at all. Entire corridors have been hollowed out this way, with storefronts replaced by asphalt and buildings pushed far apart. Cities that want density, walkability, and a healthy tax base are legislating against their own goals.

The third problem is that the rules punish the small and the old. A new development can absorb a parking mandate if it has the capital. A family converting a corner building into a cafe cannot. Historic main streets are full of buildings that would be illegal to build today because they have no parking, and many of them are the most valued places in their towns. Requirements make it hard to reuse these structures, because a change of use can trigger a demand for spaces that the site cannot physically hold.

Defenders of the rules have a fair worry. If a new building has too little parking, residents and customers will park on neighboring streets, and neighbors will be angry. This is a real concern, but it is a problem about the street, not about the building. The right tool is managing the curb through permits, metered pricing, and time limits. A city that prices its curbside spaces sensibly can keep them available without forcing every private owner to guess the right number of spaces. Parking that is free and unmanaged will always feel scarce, and no minimum can fix that.

The deeper point is that developers and lenders are not fools. Removing the minimum does not mean no parking gets built. It means the amount is chosen by the people who bear the cost and know their market. A suburban shopping center will still provide plenty of spaces, because customers expect them. A downtown apartment building near a train station may build fewer, and its residents will be glad of the lower rent. Where cities have loosened or removed their requirements, such as Buffalo, which scrapped its minimums citywide in 2017, and Minneapolis, which followed, developers have kept building parking where it makes sense and skipped it where it does not. The sky did not fall. Streets did not fill with desperate drivers. What changed is that builders gained flexibility.

There is also a climate and health argument. Abundant, free parking is a subsidy for driving. It makes every trip by car cheaper and every trip by foot, bike, or bus relatively less appealing. When cities stop requiring that subsidy, they stop tilting the field toward the most land-hungry, polluting way of getting around. Fewer garages and lots also means less paved surface, less heat, and less runoff.

Some people will say that removing minimums is an attack on drivers. It is not. Plenty of people need cars, including the elderly, people with disabilities, and workers with irregular hours, and they will keep needing them. The point is to let supply follow real demand rather than a formula written decades ago. A driver who wants a space can still pay for one. What changes is that the person who does not drive no longer subsidizes the one who does.

The best case for repeal is also the most modest. We do not make builders guess how many elevators, bathrooms, or windows a market wants in order to force a number into the code. We trust them to respond to demand. Parking should be treated the same way. If a space is worth having, someone will pay for it. If it is not, the land should be free to do something more useful.

Cities that care about affordable housing, lively streets, small business, and sensible use of land should take the first step. Strike the minimums from the code, manage the curb with prices, and let each project decide what it needs. The empty lots we leave behind will be a far better sign of progress than the full ones we have been mandating.
