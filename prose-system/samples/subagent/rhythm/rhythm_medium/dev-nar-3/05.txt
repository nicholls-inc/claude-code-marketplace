On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Dockworkers ate lunch, children played near the waterfront, and horses and wagons crowded the streets beside the harbor. Looming over the neighborhood stood a steel tank some fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, which was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a trade that grew more urgent as Prohibition approached and as munitions manufacturers wanted ethanol.

The tank had been built hastily in 1915, and almost everything about its construction was questionable. Its owners, led by the company's treasurer Arthur Jell, had not hired an engineer to review the design, and the steel plates were thinner than the load demanded. During filling the tank leaked so badly that streams of molasses ran down its sides. Rather than repair it, the company painted it brown to hide the stains. Neighbors collected the dripping syrup in cans, and workers noticed the tank groaning, but complaints went nowhere. Only once was it filled to capacity and tested for water tightness, and the test was never properly checked.

A fresh shipment had arrived days earlier, and the sudden rise in temperature, after a bitter cold snap, may have destabilized the contents. At about 12:30 in the afternoon, witnesses heard a deep rumbling, followed by a sound like machine-gun fire as rivets shot out of the steel. The tank burst open, and a wave of molasses between eight and fifteen feet high surged outward at an estimated thirty-five miles per hour. It carried a pressure wave strong enough to knock buildings off their foundations. It buckled the girders of the elevated railway, tossed a truck into the harbor, and smashed through homes, the fire station, and a warehouse.

The cold weather had thickened the syrup, so escape proved nearly impossible. People were swept off their feet and trapped in a sticky mass that clung to their clothes and limbs, and some drowned in it. Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Rescuers, including soldiers, sailors, and Red Cross volunteers, waded into the muck, but cleanup took weeks. The harbor ran brown well into the summer, and locals said the area smelled of molasses for decades.

The disaster led to one of the first major class-action lawsuits in Massachusetts. United States Industrial Alcohol, which had absorbed Purity Distilling, insisted that anarchists had bombed the tank. After years of testimony from some three thousand witnesses, an auditor appointed by the court ruled in 1925 that the tank had been structurally unsound, and the company paid roughly a million dollars in settlements. The case also helped change the law, because it pushed Massachusetts and other states to require that licensed engineers and architects approve and sign off on major construction plans.
