Imagine two people, Asha and Ben, who have never met and who can only talk over a line that anyone can listen in on. They want to end up knowing the same secret number, one that nobody else knows. For most of history this was thought to be impossible. If a secret must be shared, surely someone has to carry it from one person to the other, and the carrier could be watched. Yet there is a way to do it entirely in the open, and it rests on one idea: some jobs are easy to do but extremely hard to undo.

A paint analogy shows the shape of the trick. Suppose Asha and Ben agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow. Each then picks a private colour and tells no one, Asha choosing red and Ben blue. Each mixes their private colour into the shared yellow and sends the resulting jar to the other. Anyone listening sees the yellow, and sees two mixed jars. Now each person adds their own private colour to the jar they received. Asha adds her red to Ben's mix, which holds yellow and blue. Ben adds his blue to Asha's mix, which holds yellow and red. Both end up with yellow, red and blue in the same amounts, so both have exactly the same shade. The eavesdropper holds only the two half-mixed jars. Adding either private colour is impossible without knowing it, and unmixing paint to find out what went in is, in practice, hopeless.

Numbers can play the part of paint, provided we choose a kind of arithmetic with the same one-way quality. The kind we need is the arithmetic of remainders, which you already know from reading a clock. If it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, you do not say it is fifteen o'clock. You say three, because you went round the dial and started again. That is arithmetic with a clock that has twelve positions. We can build a dial with any number of positions. Let us use a dial with twenty-three positions, numbered 0 to 22. Whenever a calculation gives a number bigger than 22, we divide by 23 and keep only the remainder. So 30 becomes 7, and 50 becomes 4, since 50 is two lots of 23 with 4 left over.

Next we need powers. Five to the power of three means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. On our twenty-three position dial, 125 is five full laps of 23, which is 115, with 10 left over, so the answer is 10. The important feature is that going forward is easy and going backward is not. If I tell you to start with 5, raise it to some power, and report the remainder after dividing by 23, you can do that with a pencil. But if I give you only the remainder and ask what power I used, you have almost nothing to go on. The remainders jump around with no visible pattern, and the only general method is to try the powers one after another until one matches.

Here is the whole exchange. Asha and Ben agree publicly on two numbers: 23 for the dial size and 5 as the base. These are like the shared yellow, and the eavesdropper may know them. Asha secretly picks 6 and Ben secretly picks 15. These are the private colours.

Asha works out 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625. Dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8, so she sends Ben the number 8. Ben works out 5 to the power 15 and divides by 23, which leaves 19, and he sends Asha the number 19. Those two numbers, 8 and 19, cross the open line, so everyone can see them. They are the half-mixed jars.

Now each finishes the mixing. Asha takes Ben's 19, raises it to her private power of 6, and takes the remainder on the 23 dial. The calculation is 19 to the power 6, which is 47,045,881, and the remainder after dividing by 23 is 2. Ben takes Asha's 8, raises it to his private power of 15, and does the same. The result is also 2. Both now hold the number 2, and neither ever sent it.

Why do they agree? Asha's final number is 5 raised to the power 15, then raised to the power 6. Ben's is 5 raised to the power 6, then raised to the power 15. Each amounts to 5 multiplied by itself 6 times 15, or 90, times, and the order in which you do the powers does not matter. The remainder step does not disturb this, because taking remainders along the way gives the same final answer as taking it only at the end. So both land on the same number, just as both paint mixes ended up the same shade.

Now consider the eavesdropper. She knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret she needs either Asha's 6 or Ben's 15. With a dial this small she can simply try powers of 5 until she finds one that leaves 8, and she will find 6 after a few attempts. So this tiny example is not secure at all, and is only there to show the mechanics. But the difficulty of the backward step grows enormously as the dial grows.

Real systems use a dial with hundreds of digits, and private numbers just as large. At that size, the forward calculation is still fast for a computer, thanks to clever shortcuts such as repeatedly squaring. But trying every possible power would take more steps than there are atoms in the Earth. No one has found a fast way to run the backward step on a well-chosen dial, and mathematicians have tried hard for decades. The security does not come from a hidden method. The method is published for everyone to read. It comes from the sheer cost of the one step that cannot be shortcut.

Related ideas let people go further than agreeing on a secret. In public-key encryption, a person publishes a number that anyone can use to lock a message, while only they hold the private number that unlocks it. The principle is the same: an operation that is easy to perform and punishingly hard to reverse, with a small piece of private knowledge that makes reversal easy for one person only.

There is one honest limit worth knowing. The exchange above protects against someone who listens, but not against someone who interferes. If an impostor sat in the middle and played Ben to Asha and Asha to Ben, each would agree a secret with the impostor and not with each other. This is why real systems also check identities, for example with certificates issued by trusted bodies, so that Asha knows the numbers really came from Ben. When you see a padlock in your web browser, this combination of agreeing a secret in public and checking who you are talking to is what happens in the first moments of the connection, and it is how strangers on the open internet can share secrets safely.
