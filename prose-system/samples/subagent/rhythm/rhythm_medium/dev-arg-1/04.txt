Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules, and they should do it soon. The case is not that cars are bad or that nobody should park anywhere. The case is that a city government has no business deciding, lot by lot and building by building, how many parking spaces a private owner must supply, and that decades of doing so have left our cities more expensive, less walkable, and less free than they ought to be.

Consider what a minimum parking requirement actually does. It tells a developer that a new apartment building must include a fixed number of spaces per unit, that a restaurant must provide so many per thousand square feet, and that a medical office must provide more still. These numbers are rarely derived from local evidence. Many were borrowed from a handbook of suburban parking counts that sampled a small number of sites, often in places with no transit and no neighbors within walking distance. Once they were written into code, they hardened into law and spread from city to city as though they were laws of nature.

The first cost is financial, and it lands on people who never see the line item. A surface space takes up roughly the footprint of a small studio apartment once you count the aisles and ramps, and a structured or underground space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build. A developer who must build those spaces will recover the cost somewhere, and that somewhere is rent and purchase prices. Tenants who own no car end up subsidizing the parking of those who do. In a housing market already short of homes, adding a mandatory expense to every new unit is exactly the wrong policy, and it falls hardest on the households with the least money to spare.

The second cost is to the shape of the city itself. When every building must be surrounded by or stacked on top of parking, buildings spread apart, and the distances between destinations grow until walking becomes unpleasant or impossible. Small lots become undevelopable because they cannot fit the required spaces along with a building worth constructing. Old main streets cannot be infilled, and historic corner shops cannot reopen under new ownership, because the code demands spaces that the site never had and never needed. The result is a landscape of gaps, where the empty asphalt between destinations quietly pushes everyone toward driving, which in turn is used to justify more parking.

Defenders of the requirements raise a fair worry, which is that without them, new buildings will flood the surrounding streets with parked cars and make life miserable for existing residents. This deserves a serious answer rather than a dismissal. But the worry mistakes what repeal actually does. Removing a minimum does not ban parking; it simply lets the owner decide. Developers building for car-owning customers will still provide spaces, because tenants and shoppers want them and will pay for them. Lenders and tenants tend to insist on it. What changes is that a building near a train station or in a dense neighborhood is no longer forced to build for a level of driving that its residents will never reach. Where spillover into public streets is a real problem, the answer is to manage the curb with permits and fair pricing, not to force every private lot to oversupply.

There is also the matter of evidence, which has been accumulating as cities have tried the alternative. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing list of others have followed in some form. Developers in those places have continued to build parking where the market wants it, and they have also built housing on lots and in configurations that the old rules would have blocked. The apocalyptic predictions of gridlock and angry neighbors have not materialized on any broad scale. What has appeared instead is a modest, healthy diversity of projects, with more homes, more small businesses, and fewer vacant concrete lots.

It is worth pausing on the principle underneath all of this. Parking is a good like any other. Some people value it highly and some barely at all, and the sensible way to match supply to demand is to let prices and private judgment do the work rather than a formula in a municipal code. We do not mandate a minimum number of bookshelves per household or a minimum number of seats in every restaurant, because owners know their own customers better than a planning committee does. The same humility should apply to parking.

None of this requires a city to be hostile to drivers. Cities can keep ample parking where it makes sense, build public garages where a district needs them, and price on-street spaces so that they are actually available when someone needs one. Repeal simply moves the decision from a one-size-fits-all rule to the people closest to the facts. A city that makes that move is choosing to trust builders, residents, and markets over a number that was never well founded, and it will be rewarded with cheaper homes, livelier streets, and a built environment that can finally adapt to how people really live.
