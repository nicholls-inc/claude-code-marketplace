On 15 January 1919, a steel tank on Commercial Street in the North End of Boston burst and released about 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, which used molasses to make industrial alcohol and, in the end, munitions. The tank stood about 50 feet high and 90 feet across, close to the harbour, and it was surrounded by homes, a firehouse and the elevated railway.

The tank had been finished in 1915, and it had never been right. It leaked so freely that neighbours collected the molasses that ran down its sides. Instead of repairing it properly, the company painted it brown so that the stains would be harder to see. The man who supervised its construction, Arthur Jell, was not an engineer and had no training in the design of large tanks. The structure was never tested by filling it with water, and no architect or engineer was asked to review the plans. The steel was thinner than the load required, and the rivets were poor. A shipment of molasses had arrived from the Caribbean a few days earlier and had been pumped into the tank while the air was warm. On the morning of the accident the temperature rose above 40 degrees Fahrenheit after several days of bitter cold, and the fermenting contents may have added to the pressure.

At about 12.30 in the afternoon, residents heard a rumbling and a sound like machine-gun fire, which was probably the rivets shearing. The tank failed, and a wave of molasses roughly 25 feet high at its front moved out at an estimated 35 miles an hour. It tore the elevated railway track from its supports and bent a train car. It knocked a firehouse off its foundations, and the building collapsed on firemen who were resting inside. It also pushed buildings from their foundations and filled the streets to a depth of several feet.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. The dead included children who had been collecting wood near the tank, a number of the firemen, and workers from nearby businesses. Rescuers found it very hard to reach people, because the molasses was thick, cold and sticky, and it became more so as it cooled. Some victims were not recovered for days. Horses also died, and the work of clearing the neighbourhood took weeks. Workers used salt water from the fire boats and covered the streets with sand and sawdust. The harbour was brown with molasses until the summer, and some residents said the area smelled of it for years.

The families of the dead and injured sued the company, and the case became one of the first large class action suits in Massachusetts. A court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, heard testimony for about three years. Company lawyers had argued that anarchists had blown up the tank, but the evidence of poor construction was stronger, and the auditor concluded that the tank had not been built to hold what it contained. The company was found responsible, and in 1925 it paid out around 600,000 dollars in settlements. The payments to the families of those killed averaged about 7,000 dollars each. The disaster is often cited as one reason that Massachusetts and other states began to require licensed engineers and architects to sign off on large structures.
