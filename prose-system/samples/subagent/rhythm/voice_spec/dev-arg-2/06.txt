Software teams should stop estimating how long individual pieces of work will take. They should put that effort into keeping work small and shipping it often, and forecast from what has finished. This is a view about practice, and it rests on what estimates do in most organisations, which differs from what they are meant to do.

An estimate is a guess made when the team knows least about the work. The Sydney Opera House is the standard example from construction. In 1957 the New South Wales government expected it to cost seven million Australian dollars and to be finished in 1963. It opened in 1973 and cost 102 million. Software tasks are smaller, but the cause is the same: the hard parts are the ones nobody has found yet. Kahneman and Tversky described the planning fallacy in 1979, the habit of planning for the best case, and later studies found it in groups as well as in individuals.

Being wrong is not the whole cost. Once a number is written down, a manager treats it as a commitment and an engineer treats it as a limit to defend. A task estimated at five days that needs three tends to take five, and one that needs eight gets reported as nearly done for the last three. Take a team of eight that spends two hours each fortnight sizing tickets. That comes to 416 hours a year, a little over ten working weeks of one person's time, spent producing numbers that are wrong in ways nobody can predict.

Managers still have to say when something will be ready, and that need is real. The alternative is to answer from data instead of from a judgement on each task. A team that has finished work in small slices for a few months can count how many items it completes each week and how many days an item takes from start to finish. Those figures have a wide but stable range. The team can then say that forty remaining items will take between eight and twelve weeks at the current rate, and a manager can plan around that without anyone sizing a ticket. Practitioners report that this matches story-point estimates for accuracy. I know of no large controlled comparison, so that is a claim to test in your own team.

The strongest objection is that some dates are fixed by a contract, a regulation or a conference. Then the useful question is what can be left out to meet the date, not how long the full scope will take. Cutting scope each week against a fixed date works with uncertainty rather than against it.

A team that stops estimating gives up a ritual and keeps what the ritual was meant to provide, a reasonable belief about when work will be done. It gets that belief from counting what has finished, which is something the team can check against its own record.
