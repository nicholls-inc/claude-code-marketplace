In late August 1854, cholera tore through Soho, a crowded, working-class district of London. Within about ten days, more than 500 people living within a few hundred yards of Broad Street were dead. The physician John Snow lived nearby, and he had spent years preparing for exactly this kind of event.

At the time, nearly everyone of standing believed that cholera spread through "miasma," foul air rising from rotting waste, sewers and swamps. The idea seemed sensible. Cholera struck poor, filthy districts, and those districts smelled terrible. Snow disagreed. Cholera, a disease of violent diarrhoea and rapid dehydration, attacked the gut first, not the lungs. If bad air were the cause, he reasoned, the illness should begin in the chest or throat. It looked far more like something swallowed. In 1849 he published a short pamphlet arguing that cholera was carried in contaminated water. Few were convinced.

Snow got a chance to test his idea in 1854. A few years earlier he had studied a larger puzzle in south London, where two water companies supplied neighbouring houses, often on the same street. One company drew water downstream from the city's sewage. The other had moved its intake upriver to cleaner water. Households served by the dirty supply died of cholera at many times the rate of those served by the clean one. It was a natural experiment, and it supported him. But Soho offered something more immediate.

Snow began by doing what the authorities had not: he went door to door. He obtained the list of the dead from the local registrar and began visiting the families. He asked where they got their water. Again and again the answer was the same: the public pump on Broad Street. The water was said to taste pleasant, and many people drank it daily. Snow plotted the deaths on a map of the neighbourhood, marking each with a small black bar. The bars clustered thickly around the pump, and thinned with distance.

The map did not do all the work, and Snow knew it. A cluster could simply reflect where people lived. So he looked for the exceptions, which proved more persuasive than the pattern. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed hundreds of people, yet very few died. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well. A brewery on Broad Street employed dozens of men, and none of them died. The workers were given free beer, and they also had a well on the premises. Meanwhile, a woman living far from Soho died of cholera. Her family explained that she had once lived in the area and so loved the taste of the Broad Street water that she had it carried to her each day. Her niece, who drank from the same bottle, also fell ill and died.

On 7 September, Snow took his evidence to the parish board of guardians, the local officials responsible for the district. He asked them to remove the handle of the Broad Street pump, so that no one could draw water from it. They agreed. The handle came off the next day.

It would be satisfying to say that the epidemic stopped at once, but the truth is messier. By then the outbreak had already been declining, as many residents had fled the area and others had died. Historians still debate how many lives the removal of the handle actually saved. Snow himself acknowledged that the decline had begun before it.

What happened next is just as revealing. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, began as a sceptic of Snow's theory, but he knew the neighbourhood and its people intimately. He pursued the question further and discovered what Snow had not. The first known case, a baby girl at 40 Broad Street, had fallen ill just days before the surge. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspool that lay only a few feet from the pump's well. Examination later showed that the brickwork was cracked and leaking. The sewage had been seeping directly into the water that so many people drank.

Despite all this, the official inquiries of the time did not accept Snow's conclusion. A committee reported that there was insufficient evidence to blame the water. The belief in miasma persisted for years, and Snow died in 1858, at only 45, never having seen his view accepted. It was only later, after the work of Robert Koch and others, that the bacterium responsible was identified and the water-borne explanation was settled.

Snow's real achievement lay in his method. He did not know about bacteria. He worked out the cause by counting, mapping and asking questions, and by taking seriously the cases that did not fit. That approach, tracing a disease through who gets sick and where they have been, became the foundation of modern epidemiology.
