How Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

The problem

Imagine you want to share a secret with someone you have never met, and the only way to reach them is by shouting across a crowded room. Everyone can hear every word. It sounds impossible. If the secret is spoken aloud, the whole room knows it. Yet this is exactly what happens every time your phone connects to a shop's website or a banking service. Two machines that have never met agree on a shared secret while anyone can listen in, and the listeners learn nothing useful.

The trick is not hiding the conversation. The trick is having a conversation in which the important numbers are never spoken at all.

Paint instead of numbers

Start with an analogy using paint. Alice and Bob stand in the crowded room. They agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow. Everyone sees it.

Each of them then secretly picks a private colour. Alice picks red and Bob picks blue. They do not tell anyone. Alice mixes her red into the yellow and Bob mixes his blue into the yellow. Each passes the resulting mixture across the room for all to see.

Now Alice takes Bob's mixture, which is yellow and blue, and adds her private red. Bob takes Alice's mixture, which is yellow and red, and adds his private blue. Both end up with yellow, red and blue combined. Same final colour, for both of them.

An eavesdropper, call her Eve, saw the yellow and both mixtures. But she cannot unmix paint. She would need to pull the red out of Alice's mixture, and that is hard. So Eve cannot make the final colour.

The key idea is that mixing is easy but unmixing is very hard. Mathematics has a version of this, and it works with ordinary numbers.

The one-way street of clock arithmetic

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, because the clock wraps around after 12. Mathematicians call this working modulo 12, and they write it as mod. We will use a different clock, one with 23 hours, so that after 22 comes 0 again. In this world, 25 is the same as 2, since 25 is one full lap of 23 plus 2 left over. Whenever a number gets too big, we divide by 23 and keep only the remainder.

Now think about raising a number to a power. Writing 5 to the power 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. On our 23-hour clock, 125 is five laps of 23, which is 115, with 10 left over. So 5 to the power 3 gives 10 on this clock.

Here is the important observation. Going forward is easy. Give me 5 and a power, and I can quickly tell you where you land on the clock. But going backward is hard. If I tell you that 5 raised to some mystery power lands on 8 and ask which power, you have no shortcut. The answers jump around the clock in what looks like a random pattern. You would have to try the powers one by one.

With a clock of 23 hours, trying them one by one takes seconds. But real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits in their size. Trying every power would take longer than the age of the universe, even on the fastest machines. That gap between easy forward and impossible backward is the one-way street the whole idea rests on.

The exchange, step by step

Here is the full procedure with small numbers so you can check every step yourself.

Step one. Alice and Bob agree publicly on two numbers. The clock size is 23, and the base number is 5. Anyone may know these. Eve writes them down.

Step two. Each picks a private number. Alice picks 6 and Bob picks 15. These are never spoken or sent anywhere. They are like the private paint colours.

Step three. Alice raises 5 to the power of her private number 6. That is 15,625. Dividing by 23 gives 679 laps with 8 left over. So Alice's public number is 8. She announces it to the room.

Bob raises 5 to the power of his private 15. That is a very large number, but the clock arithmetic can be done in pieces. The result on the 23-hour clock is 19. Bob announces 19 to the room.

Step four. Alice takes Bob's public number, 19, and raises it to the power of her private number, 6. Bob takes Alice's public number, 8, and raises it to the power of his private number, 15. Each reduces the answer on the 23-hour clock.

Alice's calculation gives 2. Bob's calculation also gives 2.

They have the same number, and that number is their shared secret. Neither ever said it aloud.

Why do they match?

This is the part that feels like magic, so it is worth seeing why it is not. Alice's final number is 5 raised to Bob's power and then raised to her power. Bob's final number is 5 raised to Alice's power and then raised to his power. Multiplying powers works the same in either order, so 5 to the power of 15 times 6 is the same thing whichever way you build it up. Both of them have in effect computed 5 to the power of 90, and the clock arithmetic agrees along the way.

Why Eve is stuck

Eve heard everything that passed through the room. She knows the clock size 23, the base 5, Alice's public number 8, and Bob's public number 19. What she does not know is either private number.

To get the secret, she needs to find Alice's private power, meaning the power that takes 5 to 8 on this clock. Then she could do what Alice did to Bob's number. Or she could find Bob's power and do what he did. But finding such a power is the hard backward direction. With our tiny clock, Eve could just try powers until one works. With a real clock, enormous in size, she cannot.

Note that Eve is not blocked by a lock she might eventually pick. She faces a calculation that, as far as anyone knows, has no fast method. That is why this approach is trusted.

Where the name comes from

So far we have only described agreeing on a shared secret. The wider family of methods is called public-key cryptography, because each person holds a public number that everyone may see and a private number that stays with them. The two are linked by mathematics in such a way that the public one is easy to make from the private one, and nearly impossible to reverse.

Other designs in this family do more than agree on a secret. Some let you lock a message with someone's public number so that only their private number can open it. Others let you prove a message came from you, by signing it with your private number so anyone can check the signature with your public one. The one-way street idea is the same in each case.

What the secret is used for

Once Alice and Bob share a secret number, they use it as the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method, in which both sides use the same key to lock and unlock their messages. That kind of scrambling is quick but needs a shared key to start with. Public-key cryptography solves exactly that starting problem. It lets strangers create a shared key out of thin air, in public, and then switch to fast private conversation.

A caution worth knowing

There is one weakness the clock trick does not fix on its own. Eve could do more than listen. She could stand in the middle and pretend to be Bob to Alice, and pretend to be Alice to Bob, making a separate secret with each. Real systems guard against this by having numbers vouched for by trusted organisations, and by signatures of the kind described above. So the maths protects against listeners, and extra checks protect against impostors.

The takeaway

Two strangers can agree on a secret in public because some calculations are easy to do and brutally hard to undo. Each person mixes a private number into a public one, they swap the results, and each mixes in their own private number again. The order of mixing does not matter, so both land on the same answer. Anyone watching sees only the mixtures, and unmixing is the part nobody knows how to do quickly.
