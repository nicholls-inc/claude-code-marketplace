Stop Making Cities Build Parking Nobody Asked For

Most cities decide how much parking a new building needs before anyone knows who will use it. A planning code written decades ago says an apartment requires two spaces, a restaurant one space per hundred square feet, a bowling alley five per lane. The numbers look precise, but most were borrowed from another city's code or from surveys of suburban sites with free parking and no bus service. Cities should delete them. Parking minimums raise the cost of housing, waste urban land, and commit everyone to driving whether they want to or not, and the evidence from cities that have repealed them shows the feared chaos does not arrive.

Start with cost. A space in a surface lot costs several thousand dollars to build, and one in a structured or underground garage commonly costs tens of thousands. That money does not come from the driver. It is folded into rents, into the price of a meal, into the cost of groceries. A tenant who owns no car pays for a stall she will never use, and the poorest households, who are least likely to own cars, subsidize the wealthiest, who own several. If a city proposed a tax on carless renters to fund car storage for their neighbors, it would be laughed out of the council chamber. Parking minimums do exactly that, only invisibly.

The requirements also stop housing from being built at all. A small lot near a town center might hold twelve apartments, but once the code demands eighteen parking spaces, the arithmetic fails. The developer either builds six units around a car park, or builds nothing, or goes to the edge of town where land is cheap enough to pave. The modest, walkable buildings that people admire in older neighborhoods, the corner shop with flats above it, the three-story block with no driveway, are illegal to replicate under most modern codes, and not because of any safety rule. They simply cannot fit the mandated cars.

Then there is the land. In many American downtowns, parking occupies more ground than the buildings it serves. Every lot pushes destinations further apart, which makes walking less practical, which means more people drive, which appears to justify more parking. The requirement produces the demand it claims to be responding to. Asphalt also sheds stormwater, traps heat, and generates almost no tax revenue compared with the building that might stand in its place. A city that requires parking is requiring its own least productive land use.

The strongest objection is spillover. If the new apartments have no parking, residents will park on the surrounding streets, and existing neighbors will lose their spots. This concern is real, but it identifies a problem with how curb space is managed, not a reason to mandate garages. Streets are public property, and cities have ordinary tools to allocate them: permits, meters, time limits, prices that rise where demand is high. Using a building code to protect free curb parking is a roundabout and enormously expensive way to avoid putting up a sign.

A second objection is that people still need cars, especially in places with poor transit. They do, and removing minimums does not remove parking. It removes the mandate. Developers continue to build parking because buyers and tenants want it and lenders often insist on it. What changes is that the amount is set by people with money at stake and knowledge of the site, not by a table in the zoning code. A block of flats beside a train station might provide very little. A suburban family housing development will provide plenty. Both outcomes are fine.

The record supports this. Buffalo ended minimums citywide in 2017, and researchers who studied the projects that followed found that many built less parking than the old rules would have forced, particularly mixed-use developments, while others built as much as before or more. Minneapolis, which eliminated its requirements in 2021 alongside other zoning reforms, has permitted housing at a pace well above its neighbors and seen rents rise more slowly than in the wider region. Parking reform was one of several changes there, so it cannot claim all the credit, but it was part of what made small apartment buildings viable again. No city that has made this change has reversed it because drivers ran out of places to park.

Repeal is also cheap. It needs no new budget line, no new agency, no construction. It is the deletion of a rule. Cities that worry about the transition can begin near transit stops and in town centers, pair the change with curb management, and expand from there.

Parking minimums were a guess made on behalf of every future building, and the guess was almost always too high. Cities should let the people who build and occupy each building decide how many cars it needs to hold.
