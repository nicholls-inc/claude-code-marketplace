How Strangers Share a Secret in Public

Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met. They want to agree on a secret number, perhaps to scramble their messages so nobody else can read them. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Every word they say is overheard by a third person, Eve, who is clever and patient. It sounds impossible. If Eve hears everything, how can there be anything left that only Alice and Bob know?

It is possible, and the trick is one of the most important ideas of the last fifty years. It is the reason you can type a card number into a shop's website while strangers' computers carry your data along the way. This explanation uses nothing beyond school arithmetic.

The paint analogy

Start with colours, because the idea is easiest to see there.

Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow. Eve hears this, and that is fine. Next, each of them secretly picks a private colour. Alice chooses red. Bob chooses blue. These they never reveal.

Alice mixes her red into the yellow and sends the resulting orange tub across the line. Bob mixes his blue into the yellow and sends over a green tub. Eve sees both tubs going past. She knows the yellow they began with. She can see orange and green.

Now Alice takes Bob's green and adds her own secret red. Bob takes Alice's orange and adds his own secret blue. Both end up with yellow, red and blue mixed together: the same muddy brown. They share a colour that never crossed the line.

Eve, meanwhile, holds yellow, orange and green. To get the brown she would need either red or blue, and neither was ever sent. Worse, paint mixing is easy in one direction and nearly impossible to undo. Given a tub of orange, you cannot reliably separate out exactly which red went in.

That one-way quality is the heart of everything. Now let us find the same thing in numbers.

A one-way street in arithmetic

We need a mathematical operation that is easy to do but very hard to reverse. Here is a good candidate: clock arithmetic.

You already know clock arithmetic, even if you have never named it. If it is 9 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 2 o'clock, not 14. You wrap around. Mathematicians call this "mod". We write 14 mod 12 = 2, meaning "divide by 12 and keep the remainder."

For our secret we use a clock with a prime number of hours, a number that only divides by 1 and itself. To keep the sums small, we will use a 23-hour clock. Real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits.

Now take a base number, which we will call g, and multiply it by itself some number of times, wrapping around the clock each time. For example, 5 multiplied by itself 6 times is 15,625. Divide that by 23 and the remainder is 8. So 5 to the power 6, mod 23, is 8.

Going forwards is mechanical. Going backwards is the problem. If I tell you the answer is 8, the base is 5 and the clock has 23 hours, what was the power? You would have to try 1, 2, 3 and so on until something matched. With a clock this small that takes a minute. With a clock of 600 digits, the search would outlast the sun.

Why does the wrap-around make it so hard? Without it, ordinary numbers grow steadily, and you could estimate the power by seeing how big the answer is. On the clock, the results jump about unpredictably. 5 to the power 1 is 5, to the power 2 is 2, then 10, 4, 20, 8. Nothing about "8" hints that it came from the sixth step.

The exchange, step by step

Here is the whole procedure with our small numbers.

Step one, in public. Alice and Bob announce the clock size, 23, and the base, 5. Eve hears both. These are the yellow paint.

Step two, in private. Alice picks a secret number, 6. Bob picks his, 15. These are the red and the blue. They never say them aloud.

Step three, mix and send. Alice works out 5 to the power 6, mod 23. That is 8, and she sends it. Bob works out 5 to the power 15, mod 23. That is 19, and he sends it. These are the orange and green tubs. Eve sees 8 and 19.

Step four, add the private part. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power, 6, mod 23. The answer is 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power, 15, mod 23. The answer is also 2.

Both have reached 2, without 2 ever being spoken.

Why do the two answers match? Because both are really 5 raised to the power 6 times 15, which is 90. Alice got there by doing 6 first and then 15. Bob did 15 first and then 6. Multiplication does not care about order, so the end point is the same. Neither the paint nor the numbers care which ingredient went in first.

What does Eve know? She has 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the secret she would need Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. Recovering either means reversing the one-way street. With big numbers, she cannot do it in any realistic time.

Public and private keys

The word "key" in public-key cryptography comes from this split. Each person has a private number that stays with them, and a public number that is derived from it and shared freely. In our example Alice's private key is 6 and her public key is 8. Bob's private key is 15 and his public key is 19.

The pairing is the clever bit. Anyone may hold your public key without it giving away your private one, because getting from private to public is the easy direction, and getting back is the hard one. It is like a padlock you hand out openly. Anyone can snap it shut, but only you hold the key that opens it.

The method we walked through is known as Diffie-Hellman, after the two researchers who published it in 1976. A related scheme called RSA, from the following year, uses a different one-way street. It relies on the fact that multiplying two huge primes together is quick, while taking the product apart to find them is brutally slow. Both ideas have the same shape: easy to do, impractical to undo.

What the secret is used for

The shared number is rarely the message itself. Instead, Alice and Bob use it as the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method, one that both of them can run and Eve cannot unpick without the number. The slow, clever public-key step happens once, at the start of a conversation. After that, the speedy scrambling handles the bulk of the data.

An honest caveat

There is one weakness worth knowing about. Our story assumed Eve only listens. If she can also interfere, she might sit in the middle, pretending to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, and make a separate secret with each. Public-key methods alone do not prevent this. Real systems add a way to prove who owns a public key, using certificates that vouch for identities. That is why your browser checks a padlock icon before trusting a site.

It is also true that "hard" does not mean "proven impossible." Nobody has shown that the one-way street cannot be reversed quickly; we simply have not found how. Quantum computers, if built at scale, could break today's versions, which is why newer schemes are being developed.

The takeaway

Two strangers can build a shared secret in public because some jobs are easy to do and ruinous to undo. Each sends a mixed-up version of a private choice. Each then adds their own private choice to what they received. Order does not matter, so they arrive at the same place, while a listener who saw every message is left holding ingredients she cannot separate.
