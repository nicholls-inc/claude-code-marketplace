Apollo 13: The Accident and the Return

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of three earlier spaceflights and was making his fourth trip, his second to lunar orbit. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, was on his first flight. The command module pilot was Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days before. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons. Swigert stepped in, trained hard, and was ready.

The first two days were so smooth that the mission seemed almost routine. Apollo 13 was NASA's third planned lunar landing, and the public had begun to treat such flights as ordinary. The crew's live television broadcast on the evening of April 13 drew little attention from the networks. Lovell gave viewers a tour of the two spacecraft they were flying: the command module, named Odyssey, and the lunar module, named Aquarius. Then he signed off and the men prepared for a quiet evening.

About fifty-five hours into the flight, and roughly 200,000 miles from home, Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the cryogenic tanks aboard the service module. The tanks held liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen, which fed the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity and water. Stirring kept the contents from stratifying and kept the gauges accurate. Swigert flipped the switch.

A few moments later, the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights lit across the panel. Swigert said, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it a moment later, adding that the main bus B voltage was low. In Houston, controllers saw their data go strange. Power was falling. Oxygen pressure in one tank had dropped to zero, and the second tank was leaking away.

The cause would take weeks to establish. Oxygen tank number two had been damaged years earlier on the ground, when it was accidentally dropped during handling. A later test had left the insulation on its internal wiring scorched, and nobody realized it. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark from the bare wires ignited the Teflon insulation inside a tank of pure oxygen. The pressure surged and the tank burst, blowing a panel off the side of the service module and wrecking the neighboring systems.

Within minutes it was clear that this was no minor fault. Two of the three fuel cells failed as the oxygen ran out. The command module would soon be running on batteries meant only for the final descent to Earth. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams worked the problem through the night. The Moon landing was out of the question. The only goal now was to bring the men home alive.

The crew moved into the lunar module, which had been designed to support two men for two days on the Moon's surface. It would now serve as a lifeboat for three men over about four days. Its batteries and oxygen supply were enough, though barely, if everything was rationed with great care. The engineers decided the spacecraft would swing around the far side of the Moon and use its gravity to curve back toward Earth. A few hours after passing the Moon, the crew fired Aquarius's descent engine to speed up the return and aim for a Pacific splashdown. That burn was not part of any plan the lunar module had been built for, and it worked.

Life aboard was miserable. To save power, the crew shut down nearly everything, including most heaters. The cabin temperature fell to near freezing, and condensation beaded on the walls. The men were cold, tired, and short of water. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection. Lovell lost around fourteen pounds, and all three men were dehydrated, because they rationed their drinking water to conserve what the spacecraft needed to cool its electronics.

Then came a second crisis, in the air itself. Exhaled carbon dioxide builds up quickly in a closed cabin, and the lunar module's filters were never meant to handle three men for so long. Spare filters existed in the command module, but they were square and the lunar module's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the same kinds of items the crew had aboard: cardboard, plastic bags, hoses, spacesuit parts, and duct tape. They built an adapter that fit a square canister to a round port, then read the instructions up to space step by step. The crew copied the device, and the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

Another problem remained: how to bring the command module back to life. Odyssey had to be powered up for reentry, using only the limited battery power left, and no one had ever written a procedure for starting the ship from a cold, dead state. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, worked in a simulator with other engineers for days, hunting for a sequence that would draw the least current. They found one and sent it up to the crew.

Navigation was also tricky. Without the main guidance systems fully available, the crew had to make course corrections by hand. In one case, Lovell used the Earth itself as a reference, lining up the planet's edge against the window to hold the ship steady while the engine fired. The margin for error was slim. If the spacecraft entered the atmosphere at too shallow an angle, it could skip off into space, and if too steep, it could burn up.

On April 17, about four hours before reentry, the crew released the damaged service module. For the first time they could see how much was missing: an entire side panel was gone, and the ship looked mutilated. They took photographs for investigators. Then they climbed into Odyssey, powered it up as rehearsed, and released Aquarius, which had saved them. It burned up in the atmosphere later.

Reentry brought a final, anxious silence. Every returning spacecraft goes through a communications blackout while plasma surrounds it, normally about three minutes. This time the blackout dragged on past the expected time. In Houston, controllers waited and watched the clocks run out. Then, after roughly a minute longer than planned, the capsule's radio crackled, and Swigert's voice came through. Parachutes bloomed over the South Pacific, and Odyssey splashed down near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima.

The flight had lasted almost six days. The astronauts were tired, thin, and unshaven, but all three were well. Millions of people around the world had followed the story, and the crowds watching the news had grown with each day. President Nixon later awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom to the mission team.

Apollo 13 never reached the lunar surface, yet it is often remembered as a success of a different kind. It showed what trained people could do together under pressure with no margin for error, and it led to redesigns of the oxygen tanks and the wiring that made later Apollo flights safer.
