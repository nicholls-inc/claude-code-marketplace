Let the Market Park Cars

Every city has a rule that most residents have never read and yet live inside every day. It says that any new apartment building, shop, restaurant or clinic must provide a set number of parking spaces. The number comes from a formula, not from anyone's actual need. Cities should delete these rules. Not adjust them, not soften them, but remove them and let builders and tenants decide how much parking makes sense.

Start with where the numbers come from. Most minimums trace back to a handbook of parking generation rates compiled by traffic engineers. Those rates were drawn from small samples of suburban sites with free parking, often measured at their busiest hour. A planner then treats the peak of a peak as the standard that every project must meet forever. The result is a mandate to build for the single worst day of the year, so that for the other three hundred and sixty four days the spaces sit empty. Anyone who has walked past a vast lot at a half empty shopping center has seen the outcome.

Those empty spaces are not free. A surface space costs thousands of dollars to build and a space in a structure or underground can cost tens of thousands. Developers do not absorb that cost. They pass it to tenants and buyers, who pay for a space whether or not they own a car. Researchers have estimated that bundled parking adds hundreds of dollars a month to rents in some cities. In a housing crisis, a rule that makes every apartment more expensive to build is hard to defend. Households without cars are often the poorest households, and they end up subsidizing their neighbors' vehicles.

Parking rules also eat land. A building that must devote half its footprint to spaces has less room for homes, shops or trees. Some small lots cannot be developed at all, because the required spaces will not physically fit. Old buildings cannot change use for the same reason. A corner bakery cannot become a cafe if the cafe needs more spaces than the bakery did. The mandate quietly freezes neighborhoods in place and spreads everything out, which makes walking and transit less practical and driving more necessary. Then the planners point to all the driving as proof that parking is needed. It is a loop that feeds itself.

Skeptics raise a fair worry. If cities stop requiring parking, will developers build none, leaving neighborhoods choked with cars circling for a space? The evidence says no. Where minimums have been removed, developers still build parking, because lenders, buyers and tenants want it. Buffalo scrapped its citywide minimums in 2017 and development carried on, with projects choosing parking levels suited to their location. Auckland, Minneapolis and several other places have followed, and the sky has not fallen. What changes is that the amount of parking varies with the place. A downtown tower near a train station builds fewer spaces. A suburban townhouse complex builds more. That is exactly the point.

Removing a mandate is also not a ban. Owners can still provide as much parking as they like. What disappears is the power of a city official to override a private decision that is better informed than any formula. If a restaurant owner thinks customers will not come without a lot, she can build one. If a developer thinks buyers want garages, he will build garages. Those closest to the money and the risk have every reason to get it right.

What about the street? Spillover parking is a real issue, but the answer is to price the curb, not to require private lots everywhere. A residential permit program or a metered rate that rises when spaces fill up keeps a few spots free and turns the curb into a managed resource. Cities that do this raise revenue and can spend it on the neighborhood. That is a far better tool than forcing every builder to guess how many cars will arrive in twenty years.

There is also a fairness argument that rarely gets heard. Parking minimums are a hidden tax on people who walk, bike, ride buses or cannot afford a car. They are also a hidden subsidy for people who drive. Few voters would endorse that deal if it were put on a ballot, yet it sits in the zoning code unnoticed. Climate goals, housing goals and safety goals all point the same way. More homes near jobs and transit, fewer lanes of asphalt, and fewer reasons to drive for every errand.

None of this means cars will vanish. Most people will keep driving for years, and many new buildings will include plenty of parking. The question is only who decides. Right now the decision is made by a formula written decades ago for a different kind of city. It should be made by the people who will build, buy, rent and use the places in question.

Cities that remove minimums lose nothing but a rule that was never working. They gain cheaper homes, livelier streets and land put to better use. Elected officials looking for a housing policy that costs the treasury nothing should start with the simplest one. Strike the parking minimums from the code.
