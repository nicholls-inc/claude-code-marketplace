On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Cape Kennedy carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise toward what was meant to be NASA's third landing on the Moon. Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of four spaceflights and was making his second trip to lunar distance. Haise was a rookie who would pilot the lunar module, which the crew had named Aquarius. Swigert was a late replacement for the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles and was pulled from the crew only days before launch, and the command module he would fly was called Odyssey. The mission had already shown small signs of trouble, since one of the Saturn V's second-stage engines shut down early, but the other engines compensated and the spacecraft reached orbit and then set out for the Moon.

For two days the flight was so routine that the television networks barely bothered to carry the crew's broadcasts. On the evening of April 13, about fifty-five hours into the mission and more than 200,000 miles from home, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine procedure meant to keep the supercritical fluids from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch, and a little over ninety seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert reported the now famous understatement, "Houston, we've had a problem here," and Lovell repeated it a moment later.

The cause lay in oxygen tank number two. Years earlier, during ground testing, the tank had been damaged and then subjected to a heater that ran far hotter than it was designed for, which burned away the insulation on the wires inside. When the fans switched on, a spark ignited what remained, and the resulting fire ruptured the tank and blew off a panel of the service module's side. Oxygen tank number one was damaged too and began to leak. Fuel cells needed that oxygen to make electricity and water, and the crew's air supply came from the same tanks. Within minutes the command module's power was draining and Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas streaming into space. He understood what it meant: the Moon was no longer possible, and the question became whether the three men could get home alive.

The flight director, Gene Kranz, gathered his teams and set the tone, and the only workable plan was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, power and water, but it was built to support two men for about two days on the lunar surface, not three men for four days in deep space. The crew moved into it and shut down the command module to save its batteries for reentry. The engineers on the ground, meanwhile, began to work out how to ration everything.

The first decision concerned the route home: the spacecraft was on a free-return trajectory that would swing around the Moon and back toward Earth, but an earlier engine burn had moved it off that path. Controllers considered a direct abort, which was risky with a damaged service module, and instead chose to continue around the Moon and use Aquarius's descent engine to speed the return. About five hours after the explosion the crew fired that engine to put the ship back on a course for Earth. Later, after rounding the far side of the Moon, farther from home than any humans had ever been, they burned the engine again to shorten the trip and aim for a splashdown in the Pacific.

Staying alive was harder than steering: to save power, the crew switched off nearly everything, including the heaters, and the cabin grew bitterly cold. Temperatures dropped toward freezing, moisture condensed on the walls, and the men slept little. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and Haise developed a kidney infection that left him feverish and shivering. Most threatening of all was the air itself, since carbon dioxide was building up because the lunar module's canisters of lithium hydroxide, which scrub the gas out of the air, were meant for two people and were filling fast. The command module had spare canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round.

On the ground, engineers gathered the same kinds of items the crew had on board, such as plastic bags, cardboard from flight manuals, suit hoses and duct tape, and worked out how to fit one to the other. They read the instructions up to the crew, who built an improvised adapter that let the command module's canisters work in Aquarius. The carbon dioxide level fell, and the crew breathed easier.

More problems remained: the guidance platform in Aquarius needed to be aligned, and without the usual star sightings, because debris from the explosion was glittering around the spacecraft, the crew aligned using the Sun. Another course correction was needed, and Lovell steered by holding the Earth in the window and keeping the terminator steady. Meanwhile, Mattingly, who had never gotten the measles, worked in the simulator on the ground to devise a procedure for powering the command module back up using only the small amount of electricity left, a sequence that had never been planned for because no one had expected to need it.

As the crew approached Earth on April 17, they jettisoned the damaged service module and, for the first time, saw how much harm the explosion had done: an entire panel was gone, and the side of the module was scorched and ragged. Then they powered up Odyssey, moved back into it, and released Aquarius, which had saved their lives, to burn up in the atmosphere. Reentry brought a tense communications blackout that ran longer than expected, because the trajectory was slightly different from a normal return. Controllers waited, silent, until the parachutes appeared on television and the crew's voices came back.

Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near the recovery ship Iwo Jima shortly after one in the afternoon, Houston time, on April 17. The men were tired, dehydrated and cold, and Haise was ill, but all three were alive. Lovell had lost a good deal of weight, and each man had lost some. A review board later traced the accident to the tank's faulty history and to a chain of small errors in design and testing, and the oxygen tanks were redesigned before flights resumed.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, but it is remembered as one of the most remarkable episodes in the history of spaceflight, a case in which a failure became a rescue because the people involved, in the capsule and on the ground, refused to treat the situation as lost. Kranz's flight controllers later said that the phrase usually attached to the event, "failure is not an option," was invented for the 1995 film, but the spirit it describes was real. Three men who had set out to walk on the Moon returned instead as proof of what careful, stubborn improvisation can do.
