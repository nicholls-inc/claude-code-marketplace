Park the Mandate: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will see a quiet kind of planning failure. Vast fields of asphalt sit empty beside strip malls, apartment blocks and restaurants. These lots are not accidents. They are the result of rules that tell every builder exactly how many parking spaces to provide, whether or not anyone wants them. Cities should repeal these minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon.

Start with what the rules actually do. A minimum parking requirement is a formula written into zoning law. A restaurant must supply a set number of spaces per thousand square feet. An apartment building must supply one or two per unit. The numbers often trace back to a handbook of traffic counts taken at suburban sites with plentiful free parking, then applied as though they described real demand everywhere. Planners did not discover how much parking a business needs. They guessed, and then they made the guess mandatory.

The first problem is cost. Parking is expensive to build, and someone pays. A surface space can cost several thousand dollars. A space in a structure or underground can run to tens of thousands. Developers do not absorb that cost out of generosity. They pass it on in higher rents, higher prices and fewer homes built at all. Because the requirement applies to everyone, it taxes the household that owns no car just as much as the household that owns two. A young worker who takes the bus pays a share of the garage beneath her building. This is a hidden subsidy for drivers, paid by everyone else, and it falls hardest on the people with the least money.

The second problem is housing. Many cities are in a shortage, and parking rules make it worse. On a small lot, the space required for cars can decide whether a project is possible. Small infill sites, which could hold a modest block of flats, are left vacant or underbuilt because the stalls will not fit. Older buildings that would be cheap to convert stay empty because the conversion would trigger new parking demands that the site cannot meet. Every unit that never gets built because of a stall requirement is a family priced out or pushed to a longer commute.

The third problem is the shape of the city itself. Land is the scarcest thing a city has. Requiring that so much of it be given over to storing empty vehicles spreads destinations apart, which makes walking absurd and transit hard to run. Shops that could sit side by side are separated by lots. Distances grow, so driving becomes necessary, so more parking seems necessary, and the cycle feeds itself. Mandates did not merely respond to car dependence. They helped manufacture it. Meanwhile the paved surface sheds heat and floodwater in ways that grass and buildings do not, and the tax revenue per acre from a parking lot is a fraction of what the same land would earn as shops or homes.

Defenders of the rules raise a fair worry. If cities stop requiring parking, will streets fill with cars and neighbors lose their curb space? The worry deserves an answer, but it mistakes what repeal means. Removing a minimum is not banning parking. Developers who believe their tenants need spaces will keep building them, because a building with no parking in a car-heavy area rents poorly. Lenders and buyers will insist on it where it matters. What changes is that the decision moves from a formula in a code book to the people who know the site, who are putting their own money on the line. Some projects will build more than the old rule demanded. Many will build less. Both outcomes are better than a single number imposed on everyone.

Where spillover onto public streets is a real concern, there are direct tools. Cities can price curb parking so that a few spaces are always open, and return the revenue to the neighborhood through street improvements. They can issue permits for residents in crowded areas. These approaches treat the street as the valuable shared resource it is, rather than hiding its cost inside every building permit.

The evidence from places that have acted is encouraging. Several large American cities, among them Buffalo, Minneapolis and Anchorage, have dropped their citywide minimums in recent years. A number of states have followed with laws that limit or end the mandates near transit. The sky has not fallen. Developers there still build parking when the market calls for it, and they now have the freedom to build homes and shops that the old rules would have blocked. Cities that waited for disaster found instead a modest, steady increase in projects that had previously failed to pencil out.

Repeal is also unusually cheap for a policy that matters this much. It requires no new subsidy, no new agency and no large budget line. It simply takes away a requirement. For a council worried about affordability, climate goals or a thin tax base, few reforms offer so much return for so little political and fiscal cost.

None of this is an attack on drivers. People who drive will still find places to park, and likely at a fairer price. The point is that parking should be provided because someone wants it and will pay for it, not because a formula decreed it decades ago. A city that insists on a fixed number of empty stalls beside every door is spending its most precious land and its residents' money on a guess. The kindest and most sensible thing a planning department can do is to stop guessing, strike the mandate from the code, and let people build what their neighborhoods actually need.
