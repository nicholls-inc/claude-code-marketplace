**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Every city has a quiet rule that shapes its streets more than most of its celebrated plans: the minimum parking requirement. It says that a restaurant must provide so many spaces per thousand square feet, an apartment building so many per unit, a church so many per seat. Cities should repeal these rules. Not tweak them, not reduce them by a third, but remove them and let builders and tenants decide how much parking they actually want.

Start with the premise behind the rules. Planners in the mid-twentieth century set parking ratios using manuals that averaged the peak demand observed at suburban sites, often with free parking, often with few alternatives. These numbers were then written into law as if they were physics. In practice they are guesses, and they are frequently wrong. Surveys of apartment buildings regularly find that required parking sits half empty even at the busiest hour. When the law demands more than the market would supply, the surplus is not free. Someone pays for it.

That someone is nearly everyone. A single structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and a surface space consumes land that could hold housing or shops. Those costs get folded into rents and menu prices. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the spaces beside her building. A household priced out of a new apartment because the developer had to build a garage is subsidizing drivers it may never meet. Requirements make housing more expensive precisely where we are told we have a shortage of it.

They also quietly kill buildings that would otherwise exist. On a small downtown lot, the arithmetic of required spaces can make a project impossible: the ramp will not fit, or the space the parking takes leaves too few units to pay for the construction. The corner building with shops below and apartments above, the kind of place people pay a premium to live near when they can find one, is often illegal to build today because of how much asphalt the code demands around it. Older neighborhoods that we admire were built before such rules, and many could not be rebuilt under them.

Defenders raise a reasonable worry: without requirements, won't new buildings flood the street with cars and make life miserable for neighbors? This worry is worth taking seriously, but it misreads what repeal does. Eliminating a minimum does not ban parking. Developers who think tenants want spaces will keep building them, because buildings without parking they need will be harder to rent or sell. Lenders and buyers care about this. What changes is that the decision is made by someone who bears the cost of getting it wrong, rather than by a formula written decades ago.

Where cities have tried this, the sky has stayed put. Buffalo repealed its citywide minimums in 2017. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing list of others have followed, and California and other states have limited the rules near transit. Reports from these places describe developers continuing to build parking in many projects, while others build less and more housing. The feared collapse into chaos has not materialized, in part because the on-street problem has a direct fix that has nothing to do with mandates: price the curb. Permit programs and metered parking let a city manage scarce street space openly rather than pretending it is free.

There is also a basic fairness argument. Parking minimums force a particular lifestyle into the building code. They treat car ownership as the default for every household and every trip, and they penalize the people who live differently: the elderly who no longer drive, the young who cannot afford to, the families who choose one car instead of two. A neutral rule would let each project fit its own circumstances. A building next to a rail station and one at the edge of a highway interchange should not be forced to meet the same ratio.

Finally, consider what land is for. Cities are, at bottom, places where many activities sit close enough to each other to be useful. Every acre handed over to storing empty vehicles stretches distances, lowers tax revenue per acre, and pushes the destinations people want farther apart. Studies of tax productivity show compact, mixed-use blocks often generate far more revenue per acre than the big-box sites surrounded by lots. Cities struggling to pay for roads and pipes have an interest in using their land well.

None of this requires hostility to cars. Drivers will still park, and many buildings will still provide plenty of spaces, because people will want to rent them. The point is that parking should be a service that is provided when wanted and paid for by those who use it, not a tax levied through the zoning code on housing, small businesses, and everyone who walks past.

A city that removes its minimums is not abandoning planning. It is admitting that a number pulled from a manual should not outrank the judgment of those who build, finance, and live in its buildings. Repeal the rule, price the curb sensibly, and let the city grow into what people actually need.
