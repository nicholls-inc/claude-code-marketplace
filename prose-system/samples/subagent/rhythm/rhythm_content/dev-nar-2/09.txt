Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center on April 11, 1970, carrying James Lovell, Fred Haise and Jack Swigert toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon, and the crew was not quite the one that had trained together, because Ken Mattingly, the command module pilot, had been grounded three days earlier when it emerged that he had been exposed to German measles by a colleague and had no immunity, so Swigert, the backup, took his place after only a few days of preparation. The launch had its own small alarm, since the center engine of the Saturn V's second stage shut down about two minutes early under violent vibration and the other four engines compensated by burning longer, yet the spacecraft reached orbit and then set out for the Moon with no sign that anything was seriously wrong. Lovell, a veteran of Gemini and of Apollo 8, which had circled the Moon at Christmas 1968, was commanding his fourth flight, and he and Haise were to descend to the Fra Mauro highlands in the lunar module Aquarius while Swigert stayed in orbit aboard the command and service module Odyssey. For two days the flight was so uneventful that it had stopped being news, and the crew's television broadcast on the evening of April 13, in which they gave viewers a tour of the two spacecraft, was not carried by the major American networks.

About fifty-six hours into the mission, when the ship was roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked Swigert to switch on the fans that stirred the supercritical oxygen and hydrogen stored in the service module's tanks, a routine step meant to keep the contents from stratifying and to get accurate quantity readings. A little after nine in the evening Houston time there was a bang that Lovell and Haise felt through the structure of the ship, warning lights lit across the panels, and Swigert reported, in words that became famous in a slightly altered form, that they had had a problem. Oxygen tank two had ruptured. The cause lay in events that predated the flight by years: the tank had been dropped about two inches during handling at the factory in 1968, probably damaging an internal fill tube, and later, during a countdown test, ground crews had needed to boil off its oxygen with the tank heaters running on a 65-volt supply that the thermostat switches inside, designed for 28 volts, could not survive, so the switches had welded shut and the heaters had cooked the tank's Teflon wire insulation without anyone knowing. When Swigert turned on the fan, a spark in that damaged wiring ignited the insulation in an atmosphere of nearly pure oxygen, the pressure rose until the tank failed, and the blast tore away a panel of the service module and crippled the neighbouring tank as well.

Within minutes oxygen pressure was falling and two of the three fuel cells, which produced the command module's electricity and drinking water as well, were dying, and when Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space he understood that Odyssey could not be saved. The flight controllers in Houston, working under flight director Gene Kranz, first tried to preserve the command module, but the numbers would not allow it, and the decision was made to treat the lunar module as a lifeboat, even though Aquarius had been built to support two men for about two days on the lunar surface and now had to sustain three men for about four. The crew moved across, powered Odyssey down to preserve its batteries for reentry, and the mission, in every practical sense, changed from reaching the Moon to returning from it.

The next question was the route home. The ship was on a trajectory that would have taken it past the Moon and into a wide miss of Earth if nothing were done, and the service module engine, the usual means of changing course, was considered too risky to fire after the explosion, so controllers chose to use Aquarius's descent engine, which was never designed to push a connected stack of spacecraft. About five hours after the explosion Lovell and Haise fired it for a precisely timed burn that put the ship back on a path that would swing around the Moon and return to Earth, and after rounding the far side, where they passed some 158 miles above the surface and reached a distance from Earth of about 248,000 miles that remains the human record, they fired the engine again to speed the return and aim for the Pacific, shaving about ten hours off the trip.

Then came the long, uncomfortable middle of the flight, in which a series of smaller problems threatened what the large decisions had secured. With the spacecraft's heat largely gone the cabin temperature dropped toward freezing, the men slept badly in the cold, condensation beaded on the walls, and they rationed water to a few ounces a day to keep the lunar module's electronics cool, so that Haise, who developed a fever and a urinary infection, and the others became dehydrated and lost weight. More urgent was carbon dioxide, which rose steadily in Aquarius because its lithium hydroxide canisters, meant for two men, were saturating, while the command module's spare canisters were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round. Engineers on the ground assembled from what the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard, suit hoses and duct tape, an adapter that let the square canisters be fitted to the round system, then read the procedure to the crew over the radio, and the crew built it and watched the readings fall to safe levels.

Navigation and steering demanded improvisation of their own, because the ship's guidance platform had been shut down with the rest of Odyssey and the crew had to align it by sighting the Sun through the lunar module's telescope, and when the trajectory drifted enough to threaten a steep and dangerous reentry they performed a manual course correction of about fourteen seconds, using the terminator of the Earth as a reference for attitude. Meanwhile Mattingly, working in a simulator with other engineers, wrote the sequence that would bring a cold, dormant command module back to life using far less power than anyone had planned for, and it had to be exactly right, since the batteries that would run Odyssey through reentry could not be recharged and had been drained of some of their margin by early mistakes in the emergency.

On April 17 the crew separated the damaged service module a few hours before reentry and finally saw what had happened, with Lovell describing an entire side of the spacecraft blown out and a tangle of debris hanging from the bay. They then moved back into Odyssey, released Aquarius, which had saved them and would burn up in the atmosphere over the Pacific, and endured the long wait for reentry. The blackout of radio contact lasted longer than the three minutes expected, and the silence at Mission Control stretched on while controllers wondered whether the heat shield had been damaged, until at last the capsule's voice came through, and parachutes opened, and Odyssey settled into the water near Samoa within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, almost six days after launch.

A review board led by Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank's design and handling history, and NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks, removing the thermostats' vulnerability and adding a third tank, before Apollo 14 flew the following year. The astronauts had not walked on the Moon, but the flight became a measure of what a trained team on the ground and in space could do when the plan had burned away, and it is remembered less as a failure than as a rescue carried out, hour by hour, by people who refused to treat the situation as hopeless.
