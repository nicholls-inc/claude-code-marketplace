Joining Up: Why Primary Schools Should Keep Teaching Cursive Handwriting

Every few years someone declares joined-up handwriting dead. Children type and swipe, the argument goes, adults barely pick up a pen except to sign for a parcel, and so the hours primary teachers spend drilling loops and leaders are a sentimental waste. The argument sounds modern and reasonable. I think it is wrong. Joined-up handwriting is not a quaint craft like calligraphy or copperplate. It is the most efficient way for a person to write by hand, and writing by hand is still one of the main ways children learn to read, spell, think and take part in school. Primary schools should go on teaching it, and they should do it properly.

**What the argument is actually about**

It helps to be clear about what is at stake. Nobody seriously proposes that children stop handwriting altogether. Even the countries and states that have downgraded cursive still expect children to form letters with a pencil. The real question is whether children should stop once they can print separate letters, or go on to learn a joined style that lets the pen move from one letter to the next without lifting.

Put that way, the case for stopping looks much weaker. Printing is where handwriting begins, not where it should end. A child who prints every letter as a separate act lifts the pen, moves it, puts it down and starts again dozens of times in a single sentence. That is slow, and the slowness matters most at the age when children are asked to write at length for the first time. Joining is what turns letter formation into fluent handwriting. When a child stops joining, they are not choosing a modern alternative. They are stopping halfway through learning the skill.

**Fluency frees the mind for thinking**

The strongest reason to teach joined-up writing is not speed for its own sake. It is what speed and automaticity make room for. Working memory, the mental space where we hold the sentence we are building, is small in adults and smaller in children. If a seven-year-old has to think about how to form each letter, that effort comes out of the same small supply they need to remember what they meant to say, choose a word, spell it and keep track of the full stop. Researchers who study children's writing, Virginia Berninger among the best known, have found again and again that handwriting fluency predicts the quality and length of what children compose. Children who write easily write more and write better, because the mechanics have stopped competing with the ideas.

Joined-up writing is how that ease is reached. Once a child has practised common letter strings (th, ing, ed, ough) as single flowing movements, the hand carries them along without conscious attention. The movement becomes a unit, much as a practised pianist plays a chord rather than three separate notes. That is why so many teachers report that children who join confidently write longer and more fluent stories. The point is not that cursive is beautiful. The point is that it gets out of the way.

**Handwriting and literacy are tied together**

There is also good evidence that writing by hand helps children learn letters and words in a way that typing does not. In a well-known brain-imaging study, Karin James and Laura Engelhardt found that young children who had practised writing letters by hand showed more activity in reading-related brain areas when they later looked at those letters than children who had practised by typing or tracing. More recent work from the Norwegian researchers Audrey van der Meer and Ruud van der Weel reported more widespread brain connectivity when people wrote by hand than when they typed. Studies like these have limits: samples are small, and they mostly compare handwriting with typing rather than joined writing with print. But they point in a consistent direction. Making a letter by hand, with the movement felt and controlled, helps fix that letter in memory.

Joined-up writing builds on this in a way that is especially useful for spelling. When common letter patterns are written as one connected movement, the hand learns the shape of the word as well as the eye. Many teachers of children with dyslexia have long supported continuous cursive for this reason, and specialist organisations have recommended it, partly because a cursive script makes letters such as b and d harder to reverse, and partly because it reinforces spelling patterns through movement. The evidence here is less tidy than enthusiasts sometimes claim, and I would not suggest cursive is a cure for anything. But to drop it would remove a tool that many struggling readers and spellers find helpful, and it would do so for no educational gain.

**"They'll just type" is not good enough**

The most common objection is that handwriting is obsolete because adult life is digital. There are three answers.

First, primary school is not adult life. Children aged five to eleven do most of their learning on paper, and they will for years yet. Exercise books, worksheets, mathematics working, spelling tests and, in England, national assessments are still mostly handwritten. A child whose handwriting is slow and effortful is held back in every subject, not only in English. Whatever happens to adults in offices, the classroom still runs on pencils.

Second, handwriting has a cognitive value that the keyboard does not replace. The famous 2014 study by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer suggested that students who took notes by hand processed the material more deeply than those who typed, because they could not copy everything word for word and had to summarise. Later attempts to replicate it gave weaker results, and the debate continues. But the basic idea, that the slight slowness of the pen forces selection and reworking, matches many people's experience. Handwriting is not only a way of recording thought. Done fluently, it is a way of thinking.

Third, it is a false choice. Teaching joined-up writing does not stop schools teaching typing. Children can and should learn both. The question is only whether a well-designed handwriting programme deserves its modest place in the primary timetable. Fifteen or twenty minutes a few times a week, concentrated in the early years, is not much to ask for a skill used every day for a decade or more of education.

**What other places have learned**

It is worth looking at what happened where joined-up handwriting was dropped. When the Common Core standards were adopted across much of the United States from 2010, cursive was left out, and many schools stopped teaching it. Within a decade a number of states had brought it back by law; California passed a law in 2023 requiring cursive to be taught in the primary grades. Lawmakers cited literacy, fine motor skills and the simple embarrassment of teenagers who could not read a handwritten letter from a grandparent or a historical document. Finland's move away from compulsory cursive in 2016 was reasonable in its own context, but it does not show that print-only writing produces better results. It shows a country making a bet. England's national curriculum, which expects children to begin joining letters from around Year 2, is not behind the times. It reflects how fluent writing actually develops.

The cultural argument deserves mention too, though I do not want to lean on it too heavily. A great deal of the world's written record (letters, diaries, census returns, wills, the notebooks of scientists and novelists) is in joined-up script. A generation that cannot read cursive is cut off from much of that record, or dependent on others to transcribe it. That is not the main reason to teach joining, but it is a real cost of abandoning it.

**The fair objections, and how to meet them**

Critics raise some points that deserve to be taken seriously. Some children, including those with developmental coordination disorder or other motor difficulties, find joining very hard, and forcing them to do it can cause distress and slow them down. The answer is flexibility, not abolition. A good handwriting policy teaches joining to all children and allows exceptions for those whose needs call for a print script or early use of a keyboard. We do not abolish reading lessons because some children need specialist support to learn to read.

A second objection is that schools have sometimes taught joining badly: insisting on elaborate looped styles, entry strokes on every letter from Reception, or marking down perfectly clear handwriting because it did not match the school's model. Those criticisms are fair, but they are criticisms of bad teaching, not of joined-up writing. The aim should be a simple, practical, legible joined style, taught once letter formation is secure, with the goal of fluency rather than ornament. Many adults end up with a personal hand that joins some letters and not others. That is fine. It is the result of having learned to join, not a reason never to start.

A third objection is about time: the curriculum is crowded and something has to give. But handwriting is not an add-on competing with literacy. It is part of literacy. Time spent making handwriting fluent is paid back many times over in the speed and quality of everything else a child writes.

**Conclusion**

The case against joined-up handwriting rests on an image of a world where nobody writes by hand. That is not the world primary children live in, and probably not the world they will grow into either. People still make notes, fill in forms, write cards, sign their names and, crucially, learn by writing. The best evidence we have suggests that fluent handwriting supports composition, that forming letters by hand supports reading and spelling, and that joining is the route by which handwriting becomes fluent. Teaching it costs little, harms no one when it is done sensibly, and gives children a tool they will use for years. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, not out of nostalgia, but because it works.
