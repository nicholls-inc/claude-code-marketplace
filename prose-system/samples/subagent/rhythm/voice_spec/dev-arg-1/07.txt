Cities should remove minimum parking requirements. The rule asks a planner to decide how many parking spaces a building needs, and the planner has no good way to know. A flat ratio of two spaces per home, or one for every 300 square feet of shop floor, is written once and applied to every site, whatever the street, the tenants or the transit nearby. The people who do know are the ones putting up the money, and they would build parking where it pays and skip it where it does not.

Minimums began in the middle of the last century, when cities worried that new buildings would send cars onto neighbouring streets. The ratios were often taken from manuals of traffic engineering that measured the peak demand at suburban sites where parking was free. Donald Shoup, an urban planner at the University of California, Los Angeles, laid out the weakness in The High Cost of Free Parking in 2005. The requirements are set to the demand that exists when parking costs the driver nothing, and so they assume the outcome they are meant to predict. A shop required to supply enough spaces for the busiest hour of the busiest day will have a large empty lot most of the year.

The cost falls on people who may not own a car. A surface space takes up about 300 square feet once aisles and ramps are counted, and a space in a structure is commonly estimated at $25,000 to $50,000 to build in the United States, with underground spaces costing more. That money is recovered through rents and prices. A requirement of one space per flat on a cheap flat adds a larger share to the cost than on an expensive one, so the rule weighs most on the homes meant to be affordable. Around one in four households in the largest American cities has no car, and these households pay for parking they will not use.

The land is the other cost. Parking spread across a lot pushes buildings apart, lengthens walks between shops and makes the area less useful for anyone not driving. Some small projects fail on the arithmetic alone. An old building with a corner shop on the ground floor may not be able to change use, because the new use would trigger a requirement the lot cannot meet. Many cities hand out variances for this, which means the cost of the rule is paid in time and legal fees by whoever can afford to apply.

The usual objection is that without minimums, cars will fill the streets and neighbours will suffer. This deserves a reply, because parking trouble is real and people feel it. But removing a minimum does not forbid parking. It leaves the decision with the developer and the lender, who need tenants and know that a block with no parking will rent for less to many of them. In practice most new buildings in car-dependent places still include parking, usually fewer spaces than the old rule demanded. Where the street is the problem, the better answer is to manage the street, with permits for residents or a price on the kerb, so that spaces are scarce because they are priced and not because they were never built.

There is also evidence from places that have tried it. Auckland dropped minimum requirements across most of the city in 2016. Buffalo, New York, did so in 2017 and was among the first large American cities to do it. Minneapolis followed in 2021, and California passed a law in 2022 that bars minimums near public transport. England removed national minimum standards for housing in 2011, and London's current plan sets maximums in the centre and no minimums at all across much of the city. None of these places reported the collapse that opponents predicted. Developers built parking where buyers wanted it, and some projects that had been stuck on the requirement went ahead.

I would say the strongest argument is a modest one. A city that removes minimums is not declaring cars a bad thing. It is declining to make a guess on behalf of every owner of every lot. If a supermarket in the suburbs wants a thousand spaces, it will build them, since it knows its customers. If a block of flats near a rail station can do with a third of what the old rule asked for, it should be allowed to, and the savings can go into homes. In my view the case rests on this point: the rule is a forecast, and forecasts are better made by those who bear the cost of getting them wrong.

A fair version of the reform has some limits. Cities should keep the requirements for accessible spaces for disabled people, and they should keep rules on bicycle parking and loading bays where deliveries would otherwise block a road. They should also pair the change with sensible kerb pricing so that the shared street is not treated as free storage. None of that needs a minimum number of car spaces on every lot. The simple step is to take the number out of the rulebook and let the people building and using the place decide how much parking it needs.
