Two people who have never met share a problem that sounds impossible. They want to agree on a secret, such as a password or a number, but the only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen in on. If one of them says the secret aloud, everyone hears it. If they had met beforehand and swapped a secret in private, there would be no problem. Public-key cryptography solves this by letting them build the shared secret together, in the open, in a way that only they can finish.

The easiest way to see the idea is with paint. Suppose Alice and Bob each have a private colour that they never reveal, and there is also a public colour, say yellow, that everyone knows. Alice mixes her private colour into a tub of yellow and sends the mixture to Bob. Bob does the same with his own private colour and sends his mixture to Alice. Anyone watching can see both tubs, but unmixing paint is practically impossible. Now Alice adds her private colour to the tub she got from Bob, and Bob adds his private colour to the tub he got from Alice. Each tub now holds yellow plus Alice's colour plus Bob's colour, so the two end up with the same shade. The eavesdropper has seen only the two half-mixed tubs. To reproduce the final shade, they would need one of the private colours, and those never left the owners' hands.

Mixing paint is easy and separating it is nearly impossible. That one-way quality is the heart of the trick. To do the same thing with numbers, we need a calculation that is easy to do forwards and very hard to undo. Mathematicians have several, and the classic one uses clock arithmetic.

You already know clock arithmetic. If it is 10 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 3 o'clock, because the clock wraps around after 12. You are working with the remainder after dividing by 12. We can wrap around at any number we like, not just 12. Suppose we choose 23, so that after reaching 22 the count goes back to 0. Then 30 becomes 7, because 30 is one full lap of 23 plus 7 left over. We write "mod 23" for this, and say that 30 is 7 mod 23.

Now think about repeated multiplication. Take a number, say 5, and multiply it by itself a chosen number of times, wrapping around at 23 each time. Doing this forwards is straightforward. Working backwards is the hard part. Suppose I tell you that 5 multiplied by itself some unknown number of times, wrapped at 23, gives 8, and I ask how many times I multiplied. There is no formula that gets you the answer directly. You have to try one, two, three times and so on until you hit 8. With a wrap-around of 23 that takes seconds. With a wrap-around that is a number hundreds of digits long, the same search would outlast the lifetime of the universe, even on every computer on Earth.

Here is the scheme with small numbers so you can follow it. Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers: the wrap-around, 23, and the base, 5. These play the part of the yellow paint. Everyone can know them.

Alice secretly picks a number, say 6. She multiplies 5 by itself 6 times, wrapping at 23. That gives 15625, which is 8 mod 23, since 23 times 679 is 15617 and the leftover is 8. She sends 8 to Bob. This is her half-mixed tub.

Bob secretly picks a number, say 15. He multiplies 5 by itself 15 times, wrapping at 23, and gets 19. He sends 19 to Alice.

Now each does the second step. Alice takes the 19 she received and multiplies it by itself 6 times, her secret count, wrapping at 23. Bob takes the 8 he received and multiplies it by itself 15 times, his secret count, wrapping at 23. Both calculations land on 2. It is not luck. Alice has effectively multiplied 5 by itself 15 times and then 6 times over, and Bob has done 6 times and then 15 times over. Both have multiplied 5 by itself 90 times in total, and the order in which the multiplying was done does not change the answer. Each has the number 2, and the shared secret never crossed the line.

What did the eavesdropper hear? The numbers 23 and 5, which were public, and the two messages, 8 and 19. To find the secret, they would need Alice's count of 6 or Bob's count of 15. With numbers this small they could simply try every count until 5 wrapped to 8, which would take a minute with pencil and paper. That is why real systems use enormous numbers, with hundreds of digits. Alice and Bob's computers can still do the forward calculations quickly, using a shortcut of repeated squaring so that they never have to perform billions of multiplications one by one. The eavesdropper, having no shortcut for going backwards, is stuck with a search that is hopelessly long.

The name "public-key" comes from a close relative of this idea. In such systems each person has a pair of linked numbers. One, the public key, can be given to the whole world. The other, the private key, is kept secret. The two are related so that anything locked with the public key can only be unlocked with the private one. Someone who has never met you can look up your public key and use it to lock a message that only you can open, just as Bob used Alice's public mixture to build a secret that only Alice could complete. The mathematics behind a lock like that is different in detail, often based on the difficulty of splitting a huge number back into the two large primes that were multiplied to make it, but the principle is the same. Doing the forward calculation is cheap, and undoing it without a secret is ruinously expensive.

In practice the shared secret is rarely used for anything fancy. Once Alice and Bob both hold it, they feed it into an ordinary scrambling method that is fast and works well when both sides hold the same key. The slow, clever number work is used only for the hand-shake, to set up the key. After that they talk through the faster scrambling, and the eavesdropper hears only noise. This is what your browser does when you see the padlock beside a website's address.

There is one weakness in the paint story that is worth knowing about. The scheme protects against someone who merely listens. It does not by itself protect against someone who can intercept and replace messages. A cunning enemy could sit in the middle, run the exchange separately with Alice and with Bob, and pass messages along while reading all of them. To stop this, Alice and Bob need some way to be sure the public numbers really came from each other. The internet handles this with certificates, which are digital statements signed by trusted organisations vouching that a given public key belongs to a given website. That adds a layer of trust, but the core idea stays the same.

So the answer to the puzzle is that Alice and Bob never need to share the secret itself. They share only pieces that are easy to make and hard to take apart, and each adds a private ingredient that nobody else holds. The listener sees everything that passes between them and still cannot rebuild what they have built, because the one step needed to undo the mixing is a step no one knows how to do quickly.
