The Broad Street Pump

On the night of 31 August 1854, people in Soho, a crowded district in the middle of London, began to fall violently ill. The illness was cholera. It started with sudden diarrhoea and vomiting, and the body lost water so fast that a healthy adult could be dead within a day. Over the next three days about 127 people died in the streets around Broad Street. Within ten days the toll passed 500. Shops shut and families loaded what they could onto carts and left. Those who stayed watched the undertakers' wagons pass along streets where almost every house had lost someone.

Most doctors of the time believed cholera was caused by "miasma", a poisonous air that rose from rotting rubbish and sewage. The theory made sense to them. Cholera hit the poorest, dirtiest and smelliest parts of the city hardest, so bad air seemed the obvious culprit. If you wanted to stop the disease, you cleaned up the stench.

John Snow, a 41-year-old London doctor, did not accept this. He was best known as an expert in anaesthesia; the year before, he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during the birth of her eighth child. For several years, though, he had been thinking about cholera. Snow noticed that the disease attacked the gut, not the lungs. If it were breathed in, he reasoned, it ought to show itself first in the chest. It looked more like something swallowed. In 1849 he had published a short pamphlet arguing that cholera spread through water contaminated with waste from people who already had it. Few people took him seriously.

The Soho outbreak was happening a short walk from his home, and Snow saw it as a chance to test his idea. Each neighbourhood drew its water from public pumps in the street, and the most popular one in the area stood on Broad Street. Snow suspected it at once.

He began with the facts he could check. He got the list of cholera deaths from the General Register Office, which recorded every death in the city, and then he went door to door. He asked families where the dead person had lived, where they had worked, and, above all, where they had fetched their water. He marked each death on a street map as a small black bar outside the house. The bars piled up around the Broad Street pump, thick in the streets close by and thinning out further away.

A cluster on a map was not proof by itself, and Snow knew it. What made his case convincing was the way he dealt with the exceptions, the people who should have died if he was wrong, and the ones who should have lived if he was right.

Near the pump stood a workhouse holding 535 poor residents. Given how close it was, dozens of deaths might have been expected. Only five occurred. The workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street employed about 70 men, and none of them died. The workers were given an allowance of beer and, Snow was told, hardly drank water at all; the brewery also had its own well.

Then there were deaths far from Soho. A widow living in Hampstead, miles away, died of cholera on 2 September. No one else in her area had the disease. Snow learned from her son that she had once lived on Broad Street and liked the taste of its water so much that she had a bottle of it carried out to her every day by cart. Her niece, who visited and drank the same water, also died after returning home to Islington. Bad air in Soho could not explain those two deaths. The water could.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow presented his findings to the local parish officials, the Board of Guardians. They were doubtful, but they agreed to act. The next day, 8 September, the handle was taken off the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it.

The outbreak was already slowing by then, partly because so many residents had fled, and Snow himself said that removing the handle was not the only reason the deaths stopped. What mattered more was the method. He had followed the illness case by case, back to a single source.

One more piece of the story came later. Henry Whitehead, a local clergyman, set out to prove Snow wrong and ended up proving him right. He found that the first victim was probably a five-month-old girl living at 40 Broad Street. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and poured the water into a cesspit just a few feet from the well beneath the pump. The cesspit was leaking.

Official opinion moved slowly. A government committee rejected Snow's explanation in 1855, and he died in 1858 before his ideas were widely accepted. The germ that causes cholera was identified with confidence by Robert Koch in 1884. By then London had built a modern sewer system that carried waste away from its drinking water, and Snow's map had become one of the founding documents of public health.
