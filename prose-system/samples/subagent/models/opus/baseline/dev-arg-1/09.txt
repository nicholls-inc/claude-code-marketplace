**Free Parking Isn't Free. Cities Should Stop Requiring It.**

Few rules shape the American city more than one most residents have never read: the minimum parking requirement. Somewhere in nearly every zoning code is a table that tells builders how many parking spaces they must provide. A restaurant might need one space for every hundred square feet, an apartment one or two per unit, and a dentist's office some number that a committee settled on decades ago. These numbers sound technical and harmless. They are neither. Cities should get rid of them.

The case starts with cost. Parking takes up land and costs money to build. A surface space uses about 300 square feet once you count the drive aisles. A space in a parking structure can cost tens of thousands of dollars, and an underground one costs more. When the law requires those spaces, the cost does not go away. It ends up in rents, in the price of a sandwich, and in the projects that never get built because they no longer pencil out. People who don't own a car pay too, and they are often the people who can least afford it. A household without a car still pays for a garage space through its rent. That is a subsidy moving from people who drive less to people who drive more, and the city mandates it.

Next is housing. Many cities are in an affordability crisis, and parking minimums make it worse in ways that are easy to miss. On a small infill lot, a requirement of two spaces per unit can decide whether a building has twelve apartments or six. It can also kill a project outright, because there is no room for a ramp and no budget for a garage. The small apartment buildings, corner shops and duplexes that make older neighborhoods pleasant are often illegal to build today, partly because they can't fit the parking the code demands. If a city says it wants more housing while it keeps these rules, it is working against itself.

The usual reply is that without minimums, drivers will flood the streets looking for spaces and neighbors will be angry. That worry deserves an answer, and the answer is that removing a requirement is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build parking where tenants and customers want it, because lenders, buyers and businesses insist on it. A grocery store in a suburb is not going to open with no parking lot. Ending the mandate lets the amount of parking follow actual demand instead of a guess written into the code. Where curb space really is scarce, cities have better tools: residential permit districts, meters priced to keep a few spaces open on every block, and enforcement. Those tools manage parking directly. A blanket requirement on every new building is a blunt and costly way to do the same job.

The minimums were never as scientific as they look. The planner and economist Donald Shoup spent decades showing how many of these figures were copied from other cities or based on peak-demand surveys of suburban sites with free parking and no transit. They were then applied everywhere, including downtowns and transit corridors where many people don't drive at all. The result is a landscape of half-empty lots that spread destinations apart, which makes walking less practical, which makes people more dependent on cars, which seems to justify still more parking. The rule creates the demand it claims to measure.

Cities that have dropped minimums have not fallen apart. Buffalo removed them citywide in 2017. Minneapolis followed in 2021, and Austin, one of the largest and most car-oriented cities in the country, did the same in 2023. California now bars cities from imposing minimums on most projects near major transit. Early research on these reforms finds what you would expect. Developers keep building parking, but they build less of it where demand is low, and they deliver housing and reuse old buildings that the old rules had blocked. Nobody had to give up a car.

This is also a question of who should decide. A small business owner opening a café in an empty storefront knows more about her customers than a zoning table written in 1972 does. A developer building near a rail station knows that many tenants would rather pay less rent than pay for a space they won't use. Removing minimums doesn't require anyone to give up parking. It lets the people who pay for it and use it make the call.

None of this is an argument against cars, which will stay central to how most Americans get around for a long time. The argument is against a rule that hides the cost of driving inside everything else, pushes up the price of housing, and spreads cities out in ways that make every trip longer. Parking should be treated like any other use of land, built where people value it and paid for by the people who use it.

Cities can keep managing their streets and pricing their curbs, and they should. But they should stop requiring every new building to come with a quota of parking spaces. Free parking has always had a cost, and cities should stop requiring everyone to pay it.
