Shortly after half past noon on January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unseasonably mild day. The temperature had climbed above 40 degrees Fahrenheit after a bitter cold snap, and along Commercial Street workers were eating lunch outdoors, children were gathering firewood, and horses stood hitched to delivery wagons. Towering over them all was a steel tank fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, holding about 2.3 million gallons of molasses.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. Molasses was valuable: fermented, it yielded industrial alcohol used in munitions as well as rum. The tank had been built in a hurry in 1915 to meet wartime demand, and it showed. It was never properly tested by filling it with water. It leaked so badly from its seams that neighborhood children collected the drippings in pails. Residents said it groaned and rumbled. The company's response was to paint it brown, so the leaks were harder to see.

A fresh shipment had arrived from the Caribbean a couple of days earlier, filling the tank nearly to the top. Then, at about 12:40, it failed. Witnesses described a deep roar and a rapid series of cracks like machine-gun fire as the rivets shot out. The steel walls split apart, and the entire contents burst out at once.

A wave of molasses perhaps twenty-five feet high at its peak swept outward at an estimated thirty-five miles per hour. It was far heavier than water, and it struck with crushing force. It knocked a firehouse off its foundation, buckled the steel supports of the elevated railway, and smashed wooden houses into kindling. Pieces of the tank flew like shrapnel. People and horses were picked up, hurled against walls, and buried.

Then the wave slowed, and the second horror began. As the molasses cooled in the winter air it thickened, trapping survivors in a waist-deep brown mire. Rescuers, among them more than a hundred sailors from a training ship docked nearby, along with police, firefighters, and Red Cross nurses, waded in and struggled to pull people free. The search went on for days. Some bodies were so coated that they were hard to recognize.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. The dead included city laborers, teamsters, a firefighter, and two ten-year-old children. Many horses were also killed.

Cleanup took weeks. Crews pumped salt water from the harbor to wash the streets, and the harbor ran brown into the summer. Molasses was tracked throughout the city on shoes and streetcar seats, and Bostonians claimed for decades that on hot days the North End still smelled sweet.

The company blamed anarchist bombers. The victims' families sued, and after years of hearings and testimony from thousands of witnesses, a court-appointed auditor concluded that the tank had simply been too weak for its load. The company paid out roughly $628,000 in settlements. The case helped push Massachusetts, and later other states, to require that engineers sign off on plans and that construction be inspected, a lasting legacy of a disaster that sounds absurd and was anything but.
