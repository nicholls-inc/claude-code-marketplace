In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell on Soho, a crowded district of London. Cholera, a disease that drains the body of water within hours, killed people who had been well at breakfast and were dead by nightfall. Within days, hundreds in the streets around Broad Street were dying.

John Snow, a physician who lived nearby, was not surprised that cholera had returned. London had suffered it before. What troubled him was the explanation most people accepted. Doctors of the day believed the disease spread through "miasma," a foul-smelling vapour rising from rotting waste and damp ground. Germs were not yet understood. Snow doubted the miasma theory. He thought cholera was something people swallowed, and that it multiplied in the gut, not the lungs.

He had reasons. Cholera victims suffered mainly in the stomach and bowels, not the lungs, which seemed odd for a disease carried on the air. And in an earlier epidemic, in 1848 and 1849, he had noticed that households sharing a water supply often shared the sickness too. He had published his idea in 1849. Few took it seriously.

Now he had a chance to test it. Snow set out on foot, going house to house, asking who had died and where, and what they had drunk. This was slow, grim work, carried out while the neighbourhood was still burying its dead. He took the addresses from the official register of deaths, then walked the streets himself to learn more.

A pattern emerged. Deaths clustered tightly around one spot: the public water pump on Broad Street. People drew their drinking water from it by the bucket. Snow marked the deaths on a map of the district, one small bar for each victim, stacked at the address of each household. The bars crowded together near the pump and thinned out with distance.

But a cluster on a map is only a hint. Snow went looking for exceptions, and the exceptions proved more convincing than the rule. A workhouse nearby housed about five hundred people, yet only five of them died. It turned out the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street lost not a single worker. The men there were given free beer, and they also had a well on the premises. They seem not to have drunk from the pump at all.

Then came the strange cases of people who lived far from Broad Street. A widow in Hampstead, several miles away, died of cholera. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had it carried to her by cart. Her niece, who drank the same water, died too. Nobody else in her neighbourhood fell ill.

By now Snow was sure. On the evening of 7 September he went to the local board of guardians, the officials who ran the parish, and laid out his evidence. He asked them to act. Their decision was small but decisive: they removed the handle from the pump, so that no one could draw water from it.

The epidemic was already fading by then. Many residents had fled the district, and the number of new cases had begun to fall before the handle was taken away. Historians still argue over how much the removal actually changed things. Snow himself was honest about this. Even so, the evidence he gathered was powerful.

The final piece came from a local clergyman, Henry Whitehead. He had doubted Snow at first, but he knew the neighbourhood and its people, and he began investigating on his own. Whitehead traced the first case to a baby girl who had fallen ill just before the outbreak. Her family lived at 40 Broad Street, and her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspool near the well. Inspection showed that the brickwork of the well was cracked, and that waste could seep through.

So the source was found. Contaminated water had carried the disease straight into the mouths of the neighbours.

The authorities were slow to agree. Official inquiries, in fact, rejected Snow's theory at first, and the pump handle was soon put back. Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, never knowing how completely he would be vindicated. Only years later did scientists identify the cholera bacterium and confirm what he had argued. His map, his house-to-house questioning, and his readiness to hunt for the awkward exceptions became a model for the science of public health. He had solved the puzzle without knowing what the culprit looked like.
