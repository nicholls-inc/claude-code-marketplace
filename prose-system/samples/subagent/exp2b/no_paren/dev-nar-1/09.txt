In late August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded district of London. Within days it had become one of the worst outbreaks the city had seen. Over about ten days, more than five hundred people died in a few streets, and many families were wiped out. Most of the dead lived within a short walk of one spot, the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street, where a public water pump stood.

At the time, nobody knew what caused cholera. The disease struck suddenly. A healthy person would be seized by violent vomiting and diarrhoea, lose enormous amounts of fluid, and often die within a day or two. The dominant belief among doctors and officials was the miasma theory. This held that diseases spread through foul air, rising from rotting rubbish, sewers and graveyards. London in the 1850s smelled terrible, so the idea seemed sensible. Bad smells were everywhere, and so was sickness.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked in Soho, did not accept it. He was a respected doctor, known for pioneering the use of anaesthetic gases in childbirth and surgery, and he had been thinking about cholera for years. In 1849 he had published a short pamphlet arguing that the disease was not carried in the air at all. He believed it was swallowed. Something in contaminated food or drink, he reasoned, passed from the sick to the healthy and multiplied inside the body. The symptoms, after all, centred on the gut, not the lungs. Few people took his pamphlet seriously.

The Broad Street outbreak gave Snow a chance to test his idea on his own doorstep. He began by doing something simple and tedious. He went door to door, and he studied the official records of deaths, trying to learn where each victim had lived and what water they had drunk. He drew a map of the district and marked each death with a small black bar at the address of the home. As the bars piled up, they clustered thickly around the Broad Street pump.

The map alone did not prove the case. A believer in bad air might say the foul vapours simply hung heavier over that corner. So Snow went further, and the details he gathered are what make the investigation so convincing. He noticed that there were other pumps nearby, and that people living closer to those pumps were far less likely to have died. He found that some households a little farther from Broad Street, but who preferred the taste of its water, had suffered. One striking case was a woman in Hampstead, miles away, who had died of cholera after a cart brought her bottles of water from the Broad Street pump, which she liked. Her niece, who drank the same water, died too. Nobody else in her neighbourhood fell ill.

He also looked for the people who should have been struck down but were not. A brewery on Broad Street employed dozens of men, yet almost none of them caught cholera. Snow learned that the workers were given free beer and rarely drank from the pump, and that the brewery had its own well. Similarly, a workhouse nearby, housing hundreds of people, had very few deaths. It too had its own private water supply. These absences were as telling as the deaths.

Snow took his findings to the local board of guardians on the evening of 7 September. He argued that the pump handle should be removed, which would force people to find water elsewhere. The officials were doubtful, but they agreed to act, and the handle was taken off the next day. The outbreak was already fading by then, since many residents had fled the district in terror, so the removal cannot be credited with ending it. Even so, the gesture became famous, and it is the part of the story most often retold.

The truth behind the pump emerged more slowly. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, who had at first doubted Snow, began his own inquiries among his parishioners. He discovered what Snow had missed, the likely first victim, a baby girl who had fallen ill with cholera in a house on Broad Street shortly before the outbreak. Her mother had washed the child's soiled nappies and tipped the water into a cesspool, which was found to be leaking dangerously close to the well that fed the pump. This gave a plausible route by which the infection had entered the water.

Even then, Snow's view did not win quickly. An official inquiry concluded that there was insufficient evidence to blame the water, and the miasma theory held on for years. Snow himself died in 1858, aged only forty-five, without seeing his ideas accepted.

His legacy was larger than one pump. He had shown how to reason about disease in a population, by counting cases, mapping them and comparing those who fell ill with those who did not. This is the foundation of modern epidemiology. A generation later, the microbe that causes cholera was identified, and Snow's conclusion was fully vindicated. Today he is remembered as a founder of public health.
