Cities should scrap minimum parking requirements

Every city that wants more housing, shops and walkable streets is running into the same rule. Before a new apartment building can open, its developer must provide a set number of parking spaces, whatever the project's size, location or residents' needs. A studio near a subway stop may still have to include one space per unit. Those spaces cost money to build, and that cost gets passed on to renters, buyers and shoppers. Cities should remove these minimums from their planning codes and let builders decide how much parking to provide.

Start with cost. Underground parking can run tens of thousands of dollars per space, and structured parking in many cities costs more than the apartment it serves. Donald Shoup, the UCLA planner who spent decades studying the issue, showed in The High Cost of Free Parking that developers recover those costs through rent and prices, so people who never drive still pay for a space they do not use. A household without a car is effectively subsidizing the one that has two. For lower-income residents, who own fewer cars, this is a regressive transfer dressed up as a neutral rule.

Then consider land. Parking takes up enormous amounts of urban space. Surface lots sit idle between shopping trips, and garages wrap around buildings and break up street frontage. Lots that could hold homes, offices or a corner cafe instead hold storage for cars that are parked most of the day. Shoup's estimates of the number of parking spaces per car in the United States, which he put at roughly three, suggest how much of the built landscape is given over to storage.

The minimums also hurt the businesses they are meant to help. A restaurant or shop in an older neighborhood often cannot reopen or expand because its building lacks the required spaces. Historic buildings, which were built before cars were common, are especially likely to fail the test. Rules that make small, mixed-use projects financially impossible protect nothing except large lots and the properties that already have them.

Cities that have acted give evidence. Buffalo removed citywide parking minimums in 2017, and Minneapolis did so in 2021 as part of its plan to allow more housing across the city. California's AB 2097, which took effect in 2023, bars cities from enforcing minimums near major transit stops. None of these places has descended into chaos. Developers have continued to build parking where residents and tenants want it, because the market rewards buildings people can use. Many new projects still include parking. The difference is that the decision now rests with someone who has to justify the cost.

The strongest objection is spillover. If a new building provides too few spaces, residents may park on neighboring streets and frustrate longtime neighbors. This is a real concern, but the answer is to manage the curb, not to force every building to subsidize free storage. Cities can price on-street parking so spaces turn over, introduce residential permits where spillover appears, and invest in transit, bike lanes and car-share so fewer households need a car at all. Those tools target the problem directly. Minimums do not. They impose the same supply on a dense downtown block and a quiet outer-ring street, regardless of what either one needs.

Accessibility is another concern. Disabled residents and visitors need reliable spaces close to their destinations, and no reform should weaken those standards. Accessible parking requirements can and should remain in place. Removing the general minimum does not remove the accessible minimum; it only stops penalizing everyone else.

Finally, consider what the rule assumes. A minimum parking requirement presumes that a planner in an office can predict how many cars a building will need, years in advance, for every tenant and every use. That prediction is usually wrong in both directions, and the errors are expensive. Removing minimums does not forbid parking. It asks builders to supply the amount their customers will pay for, and it returns a basic decision to the people who bear its consequences.

Cities want housing that people can afford, streets that feel alive and businesses that can open their doors. Parking minimums work against all three. Repealing them is one of the cheapest reforms a council can make, and one of the few that lowers costs while expanding choice. Cities should repeal them.
