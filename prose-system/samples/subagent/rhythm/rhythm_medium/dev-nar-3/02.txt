On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was busy with ordinary life. Dockworkers, shopkeepers, and children on their lunch break moved through streets beside the harbor, and few of them paid much attention to the enormous steel tank that loomed over Commercial Street. The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of crude molasses. The molasses was meant to be turned into industrial alcohol, which was then used in munitions and, with Prohibition only days from taking effect, in the making of liquor.

The tank had been built quickly in 1915, in a rush to profit from wartime demand. Its construction was careless, and the company's officers never had an engineer or architect review the design. The steel was thinner than it should have been, the rivets were poorly set, and no one tested it properly by filling it with water first. When workers first filled it, the tank groaned and leaked so badly that neighborhood residents collected the dripping molasses in cups and pails. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown, which hid the stains and did nothing to fix the flaws.

Shortly after half past noon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a rumbling sound and a sharp series of bangs as the rivets popped like machine-gun fire. A wave of molasses perhaps fifteen feet high, by some accounts, burst out and rushed through the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a force that tore the elevated railway from its supports, crushed freight cars, and flattened wooden houses as though they were made of paper. A firehouse was knocked from its foundations, and men inside were trapped in the wreckage.

Cold air that January afternoon was a cruel complication. Although the day had been warm, the molasses quickly thickened as it spread, and it held its victims fast. People caught in the flood struggled to free themselves but sank deeper in the sticky mass. Rescuers, including sailors from a nearby ship, soldiers, police officers, and Red Cross workers, waded in to pull out survivors. They worked for days, though the thick syrup slowed every effort. Twenty-one people died, among them children, and about a hundred and fifty more were injured. Horses also perished in large numbers.

The cleanup went on for weeks. Crews sprayed salt water from fireboats and covered the ground with sand and sawdust, yet the stickiness clung to everything. The harbor ran brown until summer, and residents claimed that the smell lingered on warm days for decades.

The aftermath mattered as much as the disaster itself. Families sued the company, which insisted that anarchists had blown up the tank. After one of the longest civil trials in Massachusetts history, a court-appointed auditor found the company responsible, largely because the tank had been so poorly designed. The company paid roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements. The case also helped push states to demand that qualified engineers approve large structures, a reform that shaped building codes for generations.
