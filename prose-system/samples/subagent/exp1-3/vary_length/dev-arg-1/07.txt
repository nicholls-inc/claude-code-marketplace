**Let the Parking Lot Go: Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements**

Walk through almost any American city and count the places where a building could stand but a parking space sits instead. The empty lot behind the pharmacy. The sea of asphalt around the strip mall. The ground-floor garage under an apartment block that could have held two more homes. None of this happened by accident. It happened because the law demanded it.

Minimum parking requirements tell developers how many spaces they must build, whatever the market wants. A restaurant might need one space per hundred square feet. An apartment might need two per unit. Cities should repeal these rules entirely, and the sooner the better.

Start with the foundation of the whole system, which is that nobody knows the right number. Planners typically borrow ratios from a manual published by traffic engineers, built on surveys of suburban sites with free parking and almost no transit. Those figures were never meant to be law. Yet they hardened into code, copied from one city to the next, and now they dictate what gets built on land worth millions.

The costs are real, and they are large. A single surface space can cost thousands of dollars to build; a space in a structured garage can cost tens of thousands; an underground one, more. Developers do not absorb that bill. They pass it along in rents and prices, which means a tenant who owns no car still pays for a space they never use. Researchers have estimated that bundled parking raises rents by a meaningful share. It is a hidden tax, and it falls hardest on the people with the least money.

Housing suffers most. Every space has to go somewhere, and in a tight urban lot, it displaces living area. Some small projects become impossible because the parking math simply will not fit. A site that could hold twelve apartments holds eight, or none. In a country with a housing shortage, we are legally mandating storage for cars before we allow shelter for people. That is a strange ordering of priorities.

Consider also what the rules do to the look and feel of a place. Required parking spreads buildings apart. Distances grow. Shops sit behind lots, and the walk from one door to another becomes a trek across open pavement. Once everything is far apart, driving becomes the only sensible choice, which then seems to justify the requirement. It is a loop, and the law keeps it running.

Defenders raise a fair worry. If a new building offers too little parking, will its residents and customers flood the neighboring streets? It is a legitimate concern, and I do not dismiss it. But notice what it actually argues for. The problem is not a shortage of required spaces. It is that street parking is usually free or nearly free, so demand is unlimited. The fix is to price curb space sensibly, perhaps with permits for residents or meters that rise when blocks fill up. Cities that have tried this report that a little pricing clears congestion far better than a mandate ever did.

Another objection is that removing the rules will leave everyone without a place to park. This misreads what repeal means. Eliminating a minimum does not ban parking. It simply lets builders decide. A suburban supermarket will still build a big lot because shoppers will not come without one. A downtown apartment near a train station may build fewer spaces, because its renters would rather have lower rents. Developers are not in the business of building places no one can reach. They have every incentive to supply what buyers want, and they know their customers far better than a code written decades ago.

The evidence from cities that have moved is encouraging. Buffalo scrapped its minimums citywide in 2017. Minneapolis and several other cities have followed, and a growing list of places in California and elsewhere has dropped requirements near transit. Early results suggest that builders still provide parking where it makes sense, while projects that once stalled have been able to go ahead. The sky has not fallen. Streets have not turned to chaos.

There is a deeper principle here, too. A city should not need to pretend to know how many cars its residents will own in ten years. Habits change. Ride-hailing, e-bikes, remote work, and (someday) autonomous vehicles all shift demand in ways no planner can predict. A fixed ratio locks in yesterday's assumptions. Flexibility lets buildings adapt, and it lets a parking garage built today become something else tomorrow, instead of a concrete relic.

So what should a sensible repeal look like? Strike the minimums from the code. Keep sensible rules for accessible spaces, since people with disabilities need reliable access. Price the curb. Let property owners and lenders sort out the rest, as they do with almost every other feature of a building, from elevators to bike racks.

Critics will say this is radical. It is not. It is the removal of a rule that never had much evidence behind it and that quietly raises housing costs, wastes land, and entrenches car dependence. Cities regulate plenty that deserves regulating. How many parking spaces a private business must pour concrete for is not one of those things.

Let the market count the cars. Let the land serve people.
