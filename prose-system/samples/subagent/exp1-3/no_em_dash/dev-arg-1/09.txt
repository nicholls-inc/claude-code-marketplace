Let the Lot Go: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements

Walk through almost any American city and you will find two kinds of land use. One is the stuff people came for: homes, shops, restaurants, offices. The other is the stuff a rule told someone to build next to it: asphalt, painted lines, and a great many empty spaces. Minimum parking requirements are the rule that produces the second kind. Cities should repeal them.

The idea behind the requirement is simple. If a new apartment building or restaurant does not supply enough parking, customers and residents will clog the street hunting for spots. So the zoning code dictates a number: perhaps one and a half spaces per apartment, or one space for every hundred square feet of dining room. Developers must build at least that many, whatever they think their customers need.

The trouble is that nobody knows the right number, and the codes pretend otherwise. Many requirements trace back to a handbook of parking generation rates compiled from surveys at suburban sites with ample free parking, often with few data points behind each figure. Planners copied those numbers into local law as if they were physical constants. A building in a walkable neighborhood near a train station gets the same demand estimate as one beside a highway interchange. Reality varies enormously; the code does not.

Because the cost is hidden, it is easy to ignore. It should not be. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles and ramps, and in many cities structured parking costs tens of thousands of dollars per space to build. Underground parking costs more. Those expenses do not vanish. They are folded into the rent, the mortgage, the price of the sandwich. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the space the code forced into the building. Economists who study this describe it as a bundled subsidy for drivers, paid disproportionately by people who drive less and earn less.

The effect on housing is the part that should trouble anyone worried about affordability. Required parking eats land and budget that could have gone to homes. On a small lot, a parking mandate can make a project impossible: the ramp and the stalls leave no room for the units that would have made the numbers work. Developers respond by building fewer, larger, pricier units, or by not building at all. Cities that say they want more housing while holding on to these rules are pressing the accelerator and the brake at once.

Small businesses suffer too. Converting an old corner building into a cafe often triggers a parking requirement the lot cannot meet, which is why so many charming, walkable commercial streets are effectively frozen in place. The old buildings that gave those streets their character would be illegal to build today.

Defenders raise fair objections, and they deserve a straight answer. First: without mandates, won't the streets fill up with parked cars? Sometimes, in some places, yes. But that is an argument for managing the curb, not for forcing every private building to oversupply. Cities can price on-street parking to keep a space or two open on each block, issue residential permits, and return the revenue to the neighborhood. A priced curb allocates a scarce resource; a mandate just hides the scarcity behind a minimum no one chose.

Second: won't developers build too little and leave their own tenants stranded? Developers and lenders are not eager to build housing people cannot use. Where drivers are plentiful, parking will still be built, because tenants will pay for it. The evidence from places that have relaxed the rules points this way. Buffalo eliminated its citywide requirements in 2017, and Minneapolis, Anchorage, and others have followed with their own rollbacks. Builders in these cities still provide parking where the market wants it, and they build less where it does not. Removing a minimum is not the same as imposing a maximum. It simply lets the number reflect the project.

Third: what about people who depend on cars, including the elderly and those with disabilities? Accessible spaces are governed by separate accessibility law, and nothing here touches that. Beyond it, the best protection for car-dependent residents is a city with more housing choices and cheaper prices, not one where every new building must carry a parking surcharge.

There is also a larger stake. Land is the foundation of a city's economy and its tax base. A parking lot generates far less tax revenue per acre than the mixed-use buildings it displaces. Forcing a vast share of urban land into storing empty vehicles is a poor bargain for municipal budgets, for transit, and for the climate, since cheap, abundant parking quietly encourages the driving that cities are trying to reduce.

None of this requires banning cars or declaring parking evil. It requires a modest humility: the person who knows how many spaces a building needs is the one who is paying for them. Let owners, lenders, and tenants decide, and let the city focus on managing the public street.

Repeal the mandates. Keep the parking that people actually want, and put everything else to better use.
