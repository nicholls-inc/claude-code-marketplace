The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran on his fourth spaceflight. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot and a rookie. The third seat belonged to Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons. Swigert trained hard in the short time left and was ready. Few people watching the launch thought the mission would be anything but routine. Two Apollo crews had already walked on the Moon, and the public's attention had begun to drift.

The crew named their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius. For the first two days the trip went smoothly. The astronauts settled into their routines, and the television broadcast they planned for the evening of April 13 was meant to show viewers at home what life inside the spacecraft looked like. Most networks did not carry it.

At about fifty-five hours into the flight, the spacecraft was roughly two hundred thousand miles from Earth. Mission Control asked the crew to stir the supercooled oxygen and hydrogen in the service module tanks, a routine chore that keeps the contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A little over a minute later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert reported, "Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it a moment later, and the phrase passed into history.

Warning lights lit up across the panel. Voltage was dropping. Haise saw a main bus fail, then another. Out of a window, Lovell saw gas streaming into space. The cause was an oxygen tank that had ruptured. Years before, during testing on the ground, the tank's heaters had run far too long and baked away the protective coating on the wiring inside. Nobody knew. When Swigert sent power through the stirring fans, a spark ignited the damaged insulation inside a tank of nearly pure oxygen. The tank burst, and the blast blew a panel off the side of the service module and crippled the neighboring tank as well.

Oxygen fed the fuel cells that made electricity and water for the command module. Without it, Odyssey was dying. Within a few hours its power would be gone, and the crew could not simply turn around and go home. The big engine on the service module could not be trusted, and the flight path was aimed at the Moon.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers in Houston made the call that saved the mission. The lunar module, Aquarius, was a small, fragile craft built to carry two men to the lunar surface for a day or so. It had its own oxygen, batteries and engine. Mission Control told the crew to climb into it and use it as a lifeboat. Lovell and Haise powered it up, while Swigert shut down the failing command module to save what little power remained. The landing was out of the question. The goal was no longer the Moon but survival.

The next question was which way to go. A direct return would have required firing the damaged service module engine, which was too risky. Instead, controllers chose to let the spacecraft swing around the far side of the Moon and be flung back toward Earth by lunar gravity, then to speed the trip with a burn of the lunar module descent engine. A few hours after the explosion, the crew performed that first burn. After they rounded the Moon, a second, longer burn shortened the trip home by many hours. At its farthest, Apollo 13 was nearly two hundred fifty thousand miles from Earth, farther than any humans have ever been.

Then came the hard part, which was making the lifeboat last. Aquarius had been designed to support two men for about two days, and now it had to support three for four. Engineers on the ground worked out how to shut down almost everything. The crew switched off the guidance computer, the heaters and most of the lights. They ran the craft on a trickle of electricity, less than a household appliance draws. Without heat, the cabin grew bitterly cold, dropping close to freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls. The men slept little, if at all. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day, and Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. All three lost weight and were dehydrated.

A second danger was quieter and just as deadly. Every breath the crew exhaled added carbon dioxide to the air. The lunar module had canisters to scrub it out, but they were designed for two men for two days, and they were filling up. The command module had plenty of spare canisters, but they were square and the lunar module sockets were round. In Houston, a team of engineers was handed a pile of the same loose items the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard, a spacesuit hose and duct tape. They designed an adapter and read the instructions to the astronauts step by step. The crew built it, fitted it, and watched the carbon dioxide level fall. It remains one of the most famous improvisations in engineering.

Meanwhile Ken Mattingly, the man who had been grounded, spent long hours in a simulator on the ground. He and the engineers had to find a way to restart a command module that had been left cold and powerless for days, using only the tiny amount of battery power left for reentry. The procedure had never been attempted, and it had to be written from scratch. Its success would decide whether the crew lived or died.

Along the way the spacecraft drifted off course and needed a correction. With the guidance system turned off, Lovell steered by hand, using the Earth itself as a reference and timing the burn with a watch. It worked.

On April 17, the crew prepared for the end. First they discarded the service module, and for the first time they could see the damage. A whole side panel was gone, and the wreckage hung twisted and ragged. Then they powered Odyssey back up, nervous that it might fail after days of cold. Finally they released Aquarius, which had saved their lives, and let it fall away.

Reentry came with one last problem. During the return, the command module is wrapped in plasma, and radio contact is lost for about three minutes. As the clock ticked past that time, Houston heard nothing. A minute passed, then another. Controllers called again and again. In the control room, people stood in silence. Then, after about four minutes of blackout, Swigert's voice came through, and the room erupted. Parachutes opened, and the capsule settled into the South Pacific near Samoa. The recovery ship, USS Iwo Jima, was waiting.

The astronauts were tired, thin and sick, but they were alive. The mission had lasted almost six days. Investigators later traced the accident to the tank's faulty design and testing history, and NASA changed the way the spacecraft was built. Later Apollo missions went on to land on the Moon. Apollo 13 never did, and it is remembered not as a failure but as proof of what skilled people can accomplish when everything goes wrong.
