Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. I hold this view with less confidence than many of its defenders, because the claim that joined writing is mentally special is thinner than they usually make it sound. My case is plainer. Children will need to write by hand for a good many years yet, in exams, on forms, in notes and on lists, and a joined script is the fastest and least tiring way to do that which schools know how to teach. It costs a small share of the school week. Dropping it would save little time and would leave some children without a skill that others would pick up at home.

It helps to say where things stand. England's national curriculum, revised in 2014, asks teachers to introduce joins in Year 2, when children are six and seven, and to have pupils using the diagonal and horizontal joining strokes in Years 3 and 4. It also asks them to teach which letters are better left unjoined, so the curriculum already treats joining as a tool and not a rule. In the United States, the Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out, and a number of states later wrote it back in through their own legislation. Finland went further in the other direction. Its national core curriculum of 2016 removed compulsory cursive and gave the time to typing. That decision is the strongest example the other side has, and it deserves a straight answer.

The arguments against teaching joined writing are mostly sound as far as they go. Teaching time is limited, and typing is a skill that children will use every day of their working lives. Nobody has shown that joined writing produces better spelling or better composition than print that is neat and fluent. Many adults end up with a hybrid of their own, joining some letters and printing others, and they manage. Reviews of handwriting research, including the work of Steve Graham in the United States, find that what matters most is that a child's writing is legible and automatic, so that attention is free for the content. They do not find that one particular style gives a decisive advantage. If I claimed otherwise I would be overstating the evidence.

The better evidence supports handwriting itself over the keyboard, and that matters because the choice facing schools is not really between joined and print. In a 2012 study led by Karin James, children of about five who practised letters by hand showed more activity in brain regions linked to reading than those who typed them, though the sample was small. A 2009 study by Virginia Berninger and colleagues found that pupils in grades two, four and six wrote more words, and wrote them faster, with a pen than at a keyboard. In 2014 Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer reported that students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those who used laptops, although later attempts to repeat the result have found smaller effects or none. Taken together the findings are mixed, but they point toward an advantage for children who can write fluently by hand, and they give no reason to treat handwriting as a relic.

Once a school accepts that children should write by hand, the question is which form to teach, and here joining has a practical case. Printed letters are made of separate strokes, and the pen leaves the paper after almost every letter. A joined script keeps the pen on the page through most of a word, so a word like "something" needs a handful of lifts where printing needs more than nine. Fewer lifts mean fewer starts and stops, and in practice a faster and more even line. I put this forward as a view drawn from how the movement works and not as a result from a trial. The setting where it counts is the exam hall. A pupil sitting nine GCSEs will spend something in the region of twenty to thirty hours writing by hand, since papers in England are still handwritten in nearly every subject. A pupil whose hand cramps, or whose writing slows after forty minutes, loses marks to a problem that has nothing to do with the subject. An examiner who cannot read a word cannot give credit for it. Speed and legibility are worth a good deal in that setting, and joined writing, taught well, offers the best chance of both.

Some specialists argue that joined writing also helps children with dyslexia and with motor difficulties. The argument is that each word becomes a single movement, which gives the hand a pattern to remember and makes it harder to reverse letters such as b and d, and that the gaps between words stand out because the letters inside a word are connected. Many teachers in special education hold this view. The evidence for it is limited, so I treat it as a reasonable position and not as a finding. Other specialists say the reverse for some children with motor difficulties, which is one reason schools need to let those children print.

The cost is the point opponents tend to examine least. Suppose a school gives fifteen minutes of handwriting practice three times a week to pupils in Years 2 to 4. Over a school year of 38 weeks that comes to about 28 hours, which is roughly 3 per cent of the 900 or so hours a primary pupil spends in lessons. Much of that time would go on handwriting in any case, since print has to be taught too, so the added cost of joining is smaller still. Touch typing, by comparison, takes months of regular practice to learn well, and schools in Finland have had to find the time for it. The two skills do not compete for a single slot.

There is also a question of who loses. If schools stop teaching joins, children from homes where adults care about handwriting will still learn them, from parents, from workbooks or from a tutor. Others will settle into whatever script they invent, and some of those scripts will be slow or hard to read. I offer this as a prediction and not as something measured, but it is how schools have tended to work in other areas, where a skill that is dropped from the classroom survives in the homes that can afford to keep it. The same applies to the keyboard, which is another reason to teach both in school and not to trade one for the other.

Teaching joins well also means teaching them with restraint. England's key stage 2 writing assessment has at times made joined handwriting a condition of reaching the expected standard, and I think that is a mistake, because it turns a tool into a test of conformity and marks down a child whose clear print would serve as well. A school should choose a simple style with few loops, such as an italic hand with short exit strokes, and avoid ornamented forms that take longer to learn and longer to write. Children who find joining hard should be allowed to print or to use a keyboard without being treated as failing. By the end of primary school pupils should be free to develop a personal hand, which most of them will do anyway, so long as it stays legible at speed.

Finland's choice was reasonable in its setting, and it may suit other countries. I still think the better course is to keep joined writing in the primary curriculum, in a modest and flexible form. The case does not rest on a belief that cursive improves the mind. It rests on three facts: children will write by hand for years, fluency matters when they do, and a joined script taught in around thirty hours a year is the most dependable way schools know to get them there. If the evidence later shows that print does the job as well, schools can change. Until then the small cost is worth paying.
