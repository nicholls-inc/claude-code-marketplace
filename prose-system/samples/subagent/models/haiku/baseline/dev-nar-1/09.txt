In the last days of August 1854, London was in the grip of a cholera outbreak that moved faster than anyone could explain. In the streets around Broad Street, in Soho, people began vomiting, cramping and passing the watery diarrhoea that marked the disease. Within about ten days, more than 600 people in the neighbourhood were dead. Some families lost several members in a single night.

At the time, most doctors believed cholera came from miasma, the bad air that rose from rotting matter and filth. John Snow, a physician with a practice in Soho, disagreed. For years he had argued that cholera spread through something people swallowed, most likely water contaminated with the waste of earlier patients. Most of his profession ignored him. Now his own neighbourhood gave him a chance to test the idea.

Snow began with the simplest tool he had: a map. Working from parish records and his own visits, he marked each death with a short black bar at the house where it happened. The bars did not spread evenly. They bunched thickly around one spot, the public water pump on Broad Street (today Broadwick Street). Most of the dead, he found, had drunk from that pump. Many people who lived nearby but used other supplies had been spared.

The map was persuasive, but it was not proof, so Snow went door to door. He asked families where they got their water and what they drank. Each exception he chased down. A woman who had moved to Hampstead, several miles away, had died after she had Broad Street water sent to her because she preferred its taste. Workers at the nearby brewery drank beer and water from their own supply, and very few of them fell ill. The workhouse on Hopkins Street had its own well and almost no deaths. Every exception fitted the water theory. None fitted the air theory.

Snow then looked closely at the pump itself. The well lay only a few feet from an old cesspool, a pit that collected sewage from nearby houses, and Snow suspected that the cesspool leaked into the well. Accounts of the household that had first drawn attention described soiled baby linen being rinsed in that cesspool, so the infection may have started there. Snow could not prove every link in the chain, but the pattern was consistent.

He took his evidence to the local authorities. Among the officials was William Farr, the statistician who ran the government's registration office, and Farr was still inclined to blame bad air. Nevertheless, on 8 September the parish removed the handle from the Broad Street pump, so nobody could draw water from it. By then the outbreak was already fading. People had fled the district, and many who would fall ill had already been infected. Snow later admitted that the removal may not have ended the epidemic by itself. Its real importance was that it showed the local authorities were willing to act on the water theory.

Snow's wider argument rested on a comparison. In south London, two water companies served neighbouring houses. The Lambeth company had moved its intake upriver, above the sewage outflow. The Southwark and Vauxhall company still drew water downstream from London's waste. Houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall suffered far higher cholera death rates than those supplied by Lambeth. Nature had, in effect, run an experiment, and the difference was hard to explain by air.

Snow published his findings in 1855 in On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, which included his map. Many readers remained unconvinced, and the official explanation changed only slowly. Snow died in 1858 at the age of 45, still arguing his case. Within a few decades, though, the germ theory of disease had confirmed what he had worked out from a street map. Today a replica of the Broad Street pump stands near the original site, and the story is taught as a model of careful, evidence-led reasoning.

The lesson of the Broad Street outbreak is simple. Look at where the deaths happen. Ask what the people who died have in common, and what the people who did not die have in common. Then test the answer against the evidence, including the cases that do not fit. Snow did all three, and in doing so he saved lives in London and set a standard for how to investigate a disease.
