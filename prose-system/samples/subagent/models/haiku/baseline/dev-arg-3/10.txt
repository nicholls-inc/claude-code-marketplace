**Keep Teaching the Join**

Each autumn, a new group of six-year-olds sits down with a pencil and lined paper and is asked to make the letters that will carry them through the rest of their schooling. For many of them, it is the first time they have been asked to make a deliberate, physical mark for the purpose of communicating. What those marks should look like has become a quiet argument. Some education systems have removed joined-up writing from their core expectations, and many teachers have stopped teaching it without much fuss. I argue that primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and should teach it early and on purpose. The case rests on three points: what the join does for the hand and the mind, what its absence costs pupils later, and why the arguments for dropping it are weaker than they first appear.

The first point is that joined writing is a different motor skill, and a useful one. In joined handwriting, letters flow into one another, so the pen rarely leaves the page. Each pen lift is a small interruption. The hand has to reposition, the eye has to find the next starting point, and the mind has to switch from forming a letter to planning the next. Joined writing removes many of these breaks. Teachers who have watched children move from print to cursive often describe a change in rhythm: the writing becomes less a series of separate constructions and more a continuous motion. That rhythm matters because handwriting is rarely the end goal. It is the means by which a child gets thoughts onto paper, and a pupil whose hand can keep pace with their thinking can write longer, more developed sentences without the effort swallowing the content.

There is also evidence that the act of forming letters by hand supports learning in ways that typing does not. Researchers including Virginia Berninger have linked handwriting fluency to the quality of young children's composition, and studies of letter learning have found that children who form letters by hand recall and recognise them more readily than those who only tap them on a keyboard. Neuroimaging work has suggested that handwriting engages reading networks more strongly than typing does. The evidence is not settled, and the studies are often small. But the direction is consistent enough that dismissing handwriting as a relic is premature. Joined writing, in particular, asks the hand to produce a letter and then carry directly into the next one, which seems to reinforce the sequence of letters that makes up a word. Teachers who use joined handwriting often notice that spelling of common patterns becomes more automatic, though we should be careful about attributing too much to the join alone.

The second point concerns cost, and specifically who bears it. Children who leave primary school without a fluent, joined hand do not stop writing. They write their exams, their notes, their job applications, their letters and their forms, and many of them do so in a slow, effortful print that is hard to sustain across an hour-long paper. A pupil who has to think about every letter has less capacity left for what they are trying to say. The problem compounds in secondary school, where note-taking speed and the volume of written work both rise sharply. Those students then face the choice of struggling on or trying to learn a new script at fourteen, which is far harder than learning it at seven.

The equity implications are sharper still. Handwriting is one of the skills that families can teach at home, and many do. Parents who write neatly, who have time to sit with a child, or who can afford private tuition will often ensure their children learn a join. Children whose families cannot do this rely entirely on school. If primary schools stop teaching joined writing, the advantage shifts toward children with more support at home, and the gap widens without anyone deciding that it should. A skill that schools choose not to teach is not a neutral choice. It is a decision that some children will learn it elsewhere and others will not.

The third point is that the arguments for dropping the join are weaker than they first appear, and I want to address the strongest of them directly.

The most common objection is that keyboards have made handwriting obsolete. Most adult writing is now typed, and it is true that a child who never picks up a pen will not be badly disadvantaged in most office jobs. But this argument confuses the output with the method. Handwriting is taught not only so that children can produce handwritten text but because forming letters by hand builds the letter knowledge and motor memory that support reading and spelling. And handwriting has not disappeared from high-stakes life. Many written examinations are still completed by hand. Signatures, notes in meetings, labels, cards and forms still require a pen. A keyboard does not replace the pen in these cases, and a child who cannot write fluently in any of them has a gap.

A second objection is that print is more legible than cursive, and that legibility should be the goal. This is a fair concern, and some cursive taught in schools has been fussy, with elaborate letter forms that are hard to read and hard to produce. But legibility and the join are not opposites. The aim should be a simple, unfussy joined hand that children can read and produce quickly, and many schools now teach exactly that. Abandoning the join because some children's joined writing is messy is like abandoning reading instruction because some children read slowly. The answer is better teaching, not removal.

A third objection concerns time. The primary curriculum is crowded, and handwriting competes with reading, mathematics, science and everything else. This is a real constraint, but the time cost is modest. Handwriting practice that takes ten to fifteen minutes a few times a week over several years is a small share of a primary education. Set against the hours that secondary schools and families later spend remediating slow, laborious writing, it is a sound investment.

A fourth objection, and the most morally serious, is that joined writing is especially hard for children with dyslexia or dyspraxia. These pupils often find handwriting difficult, and their needs must be taken seriously. But the right response is to provide flexibility and support for them, such as extra practice time, alternative tools, assistive technology, or permission to use a keyboard for extended tasks. It is not a reason to remove the taught skill from everyone. Some teachers report that the continuous flow of a join helps certain children who struggle with reversals or with starting letters at different points, though the evidence for this is mostly anecdotal and deserves more study. Accommodation and instruction can coexist.

Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting because it supports the physical and cognitive work of writing, because its absence costs the children who can least afford it, and because the reasons offered for dropping it do not hold up against the purposes handwriting actually serves. A child who learns a clear, fluent join at seven is equipped with a tool they will use, in some form, for the rest of their life. Schools have a responsibility to give every child that tool, and to do it well.
