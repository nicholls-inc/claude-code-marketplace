**Apollo 13: Home on a Crippled Ship**

On the evening of April 13, 1970, a little under fifty-six hours after leaving Florida, the crew of Apollo 13 reported a bang and a sudden drop in electrical power. Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, was speaking to Mission Control in Houston when it happened. The spacecraft was nearly 200,000 miles from Earth, on its way to a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands of the Moon. The line most people remember, "Houston, we've had a problem," is a close paraphrase of what the crew actually said. Within minutes, it was clear that there would be no landing. Something had gone badly wrong with the service module, the large section of the spacecraft that held the oxygen, electricity, water, and main engine.

The crew was experienced. Jim Lovell, the commander, had flown on Gemini and Apollo 8, the first crewed flight around the Moon. Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, had been added only months earlier. Swigert was a last-minute replacement. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles, and since he had never had the disease, NASA kept him on the ground. Swigert had been the backup and learned the job in a hurry. He was the only one of the three who had not yet been to space.

The cause of the explosion went back to events on the ground, months earlier. One of the oxygen tanks in the service module had been dropped during testing, and its internal fill tube was damaged. Later, a wiring error allowed the tank's heaters to run at a higher voltage than designed during a preflight test, which damaged the insulation around the wires. When the crew stirred the tank's contents in flight, a spark ignited the damaged wiring. The blast tore a panel off the service module and ruptured the second oxygen tank. The first tank had already been lost in the same event.

That left the command module with almost no reserves. Its three fuel cells produced electricity by combining hydrogen and oxygen, and the fuel cells also made drinking water. Two of the three cells were now useless, and the oxygen supply was draining into space. Lovell looked out the window and saw gas venting from the damaged bay. The Moon landing was canceled, and the priority became getting the three men home alive.

The crew moved into the lunar module, Aquarius, which was designed to support two people for about two days on the surface. Now it would have to carry three men for nearly four days. It was a cramped, unfamiliar lifeboat with its own limits. Its engine was built to slow the module for a landing and then to lift it off the surface, and it had limited power and water. Mission Control had to plan the return trip from scratch, and the crew had to follow procedures they had only ever practiced in simulators.

The first urgent problem was carbon dioxide. Each person exhales carbon dioxide, and the air has to be scrubbed of it. The lunar module used round lithium hydroxide canisters, but the command module used square ones. The square canisters would not fit the lunar module's system. Engineers in Houston, led by a team that included Ed Smylie, had to find a solution with only what was already on board. They designed an adapter from plastic bags, duct tape, a stiff cover from a flight plan, and hoses from the spacesuits. The crew built it following instructions relayed from the ground. The adapter worked, and the crew's carbon dioxide levels stayed within safe limits.

Power and water were the next concern. Aquarius had enough battery power to get the crew home but not enough to run for the entire trip. The team decided to keep the command module largely shut down, since its batteries had to be saved for the reentry phase. Water was rationed, and the crew drank very little. Without the command module's heaters, the temperature inside dropped close to freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls and dripped. The astronauts wore winter clothing and slept in the cold, and they were hungry and dehydrated.

The most dramatic part of the return was the flight path. Rather than turning around immediately, the spacecraft was guided around the far side of the Moon. The crew passed about 140 miles above the surface, out of radio contact for a period. At their furthest point, they were about 248,000 miles from Earth, which set a record for the greatest distance humans had traveled from home. The burn of the lunar module's engine, timed carefully, sent them back toward Earth on a path that would bring them home without needing a large engine firing from the damaged service module.

Preparing for reentry was its own challenge. The command module had been shut down for days, and it had to be restarted with little power and no spare margin. Ken Mattingly, the astronaut who had been removed from the crew, worked in simulators in Houston to design and test the restart procedure. Meanwhile, Flight Director Gene Kranz and his team managed each step of the return, going through shifts with little sleep. Kranz's team had a saying about their work, and the phrase "Failure is not an option" is often attributed to him. In fact, that line came from the 1995 film, not from the mission itself.

On April 15, the crew shut down Aquarius and moved back into the command module, named Odyssey. The team confirmed the module's systems one at a time, and it worked. Two days later, on April 17, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module, which was no longer needed, and the command module reentered Earth's atmosphere. Its heat shield, designed for the heat of reentry, performed as intended. Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific at 12:07 p.m. Eastern time, a few miles from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The mission had lasted almost 143 hours.

The crew was tired, thinner, and dehydrated, but they were alive. The mission did not reach the Moon, but it became one of the most celebrated examples of engineering under pressure. The rescue relied on hundreds of people in Houston and elsewhere who improvised solutions, rehearsed procedures, and made decisions under time pressure. The crew's calm performance and their willingness to follow unfamiliar instructions were just as important. Apollo 13 was later adapted into the 1995 film starring Tom Hanks, but the real story depended less on any single moment of drama than on the patient work of people who refused to accept that the mission was lost.
