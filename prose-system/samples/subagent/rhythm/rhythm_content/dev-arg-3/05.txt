Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it with more confidence than many currently do, because the case for abandoning it rests on a picture of the future that has not arrived and on a misreading of what the research actually shows about writing by hand.

The argument against is not foolish, and it deserves to be stated at full strength. Children now grow up surrounded by screens, most adults write by hand only for shopping lists and birthday cards, and the hours spent in Year 3 drilling loops and exit strokes could, on this view, go to reading, number work or learning to touch-type, which will serve them for the rest of their working lives. Finland made precisely this choice in 2016, when its national curriculum stopped requiring cursive and asked schools to teach print lettering and keyboard skills instead, and a number of American states that once dropped cursive from their standards did so on similar reasoning. Teachers who have watched a nine-year-old cry over the slant of a lower-case g have some sympathy with the idea that the whole business is a Victorian survival kept alive by nostalgia and by adults who want children to write the way they themselves were taught.

The first thing to say in reply is that the strongest evidence in this field supports handwriting in general and says much less about cursive in particular, and anyone who claims otherwise is overselling. Brain-imaging work led by Karin James at Indiana University found that young children who practised letters by hand showed activation in regions associated with reading, while children who typed or traced the same letters showed it far less, and the likely explanation is that forming a letter requires the child to generate its shape from memory, which fixes it as a mental object more firmly than hitting a key does. Virginia Berninger and her colleagues have reported that children in the primary years who wrote by hand produced longer and more fluent compositions than those who used a keyboard, and the Mueller and Oppenheimer finding that students who took notes longhand retained concepts better than laptop note-takers, whatever its disputed replication history, points the same way. None of this proves that joining the letters is what does the work. What it does establish is that the physical act of writing is doing cognitive work in the early years, which means that schools cannot treat handwriting as a quaint craft to be dropped once a tablet is available.

Once that is granted, the question becomes which handwriting to teach, and here the case for a joined style rests on practical grounds that are modest but real. A child who prints every letter separately lifts the pen far more often, and every lift costs time and interrupts the rhythm of a sentence; by the upper years of primary school and certainly by secondary school, when a fourteen-year-old has to fill several pages in a timed examination, a fluent joined hand is simply faster than the laborious print that many children are left with when nobody has taught them anything else. In England, GCSE and A-level papers are still written by hand, and examiners cannot reward what they cannot read, so a pupil whose writing slows down, sprawls or collapses under pressure is penalised for a skill the school could have given them at seven. Children who are never shown a joined style do not usually stay with neat print; they invent their own hybrid in the playground years, copying a cool older cousin or a favourite teacher, and the result is often an idiosyncratic scrawl with no consistent letter formation, which is harder to read than either a clean print or a clean joined hand.

The strongest practical argument, to my mind, comes from the children who find writing hardest. Occupational therapists and specialist teachers have long argued that a continuous joined script helps some pupils with dyslexia and dyspraxia, because the flow of the pen makes the word a single movement rather than a string of separate decisions, reduces reversals of letters such as b and d that have different starting points when joined, and makes the spaces between words more obviously a matter of lifting the pen. The research here is thin and I would not want to claim more for it than it will bear, since some specialists recommend continued print for the same pupils and the trials are small. Still, the existence of a respectable body of professional opinion that joined writing helps the children who struggle most should count for something against the proposal to remove it from the curriculum for everyone, especially because the children most likely to be harmed by the removal are those whose families cannot buy them a laptop, a tutor or a private assessment. A well-off child will learn to type at home and probably pick up a decent hand anyway, whereas the child who relies on the school for everything gets exactly what the school decides to teach.

There is also something to be said for the reading side of the skill, which tends to get lost in arguments about output. A generation that cannot read joined handwriting cannot read a grandparent's letter, a note from a teacher, a historical document in an archive, or the annotations in the margin of a family Bible, and while it is true that only a small minority of adults will ever consult a manuscript, the ability to decipher a script that has been in use for centuries is cheap to acquire when children are already learning to write it and surprisingly expensive to acquire later as a specialist subject. Schools in countries that have dropped cursive are now noticing this gap in older pupils who cannot make out the signature on a certificate or the instructions on a worksheet written by a teacher in the old style, and the loss of a shared script has a quiet social cost that no one voted for.

The objection I take most seriously is about time, because the curriculum is full and every minute given to handwriting is a minute taken from something else. That would be a decisive objection if joined-up writing took long to teach, but the evidence from schools that teach it well suggests otherwise; a short daily session of ten or fifteen minutes in the middle years, with the joins introduced after the letter shapes are secure, produces most of the benefit, and the time is recovered later when children write faster and tire less. The real waste in many classrooms comes from the opposite error, in which cursive is introduced late, taught inconsistently by teachers who were never trained in it themselves, and abandoned after a term when the pressure of tests intrudes, leaving children with half-learned habits and a lasting sense that handwriting is something they are bad at. If the cost is the problem, the answer is to teach it properly and briefly, and no one who wanted to improve children's literacy would offer to improve it by removing a tool from the toolbox.

It would be dishonest to pretend that the case rests on certainty, and the strongest honest position acknowledges what remains unknown. We do not have a large, well-controlled trial comparing children taught joined script from the start with children taught print only, followed through to adulthood, and the studies that exist tend to compare handwriting with typing and not print with cursive, so some of the claims made on behalf of joined writing are really claims about handwriting dressed up as claims about loops. A teacher who says that cursive makes children smarter is saying something the research does not support. A teacher who says that a taught, consistent, joined hand gives most children a faster and more legible way to put their thoughts on paper, and gives struggling children a smoother route into writing at all, is saying something that fits everything we know and that costs very little to try.

Keyboards will matter, and primary schools should teach typing as well, because a child leaving at eleven who cannot touch-type will be at a disadvantage in a world that expects it. The choice offered by the abolitionists is a false one, though, since the two skills take different kinds of practice, serve different purposes and can sit side by side in a timetable without either crowding out the other. What I would ask of any school considering dropping joined-up handwriting is to look at what it would be replacing, which is a method of recording thought that needs no battery and no connection, that most children can learn within two years, that carries them through examinations and into any later life in which a pen is the only tool to hand, and that may support the brain's early mastery of letters in ways we are only beginning to measure. Against that, the argument for removing it is mostly that it feels old-fashioned, and a feeling of this kind is poor grounds for taking something away from children who will not be consulted and who cannot easily get it back.
