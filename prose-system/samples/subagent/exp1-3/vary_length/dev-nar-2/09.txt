Apollo 13 was supposed to be the third landing on the Moon. It became the most famous rescue in the history of spaceflight.

The spacecraft left Kennedy Space Center on April 11, 1970, carrying three men: James Lovell, the commander, on his fourth trip into space; Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, a rookie; and Jack Swigert, the command module pilot, who had joined only days earlier. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been grounded after being exposed to German measles. Swigert, a bachelor and a skilled engineer, stepped in with barely a week of joint training.

The launch was nearly flawless. One thing went wrong, and almost nobody noticed: the center engine of the Saturn V's second stage shut down early. The other engines burned longer to compensate, and the crew reached orbit as planned. For two days the flight was routine. The crew sent down a television broadcast, and the networks, by then bored with Moon missions, mostly ignored it.

The trouble began on the evening of April 13, about 200,000 miles from Earth. Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a standard chore that keeps the supercold contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. Fifty-five hours and fifty-five minutes into the flight, a bang shook the spacecraft.

Lovell's famous report, "Houston, we've had a problem," is usually quoted a little wrong. Swigert said it first, and Lovell repeated it. In Houston, flight controllers watched their screens fill with strange readings. Pressure in oxygen tank number two had collapsed. Tank number one was leaking. Electrical power was dropping. Lovell, looking out a window, saw a stream of gas venting into space. It was oxygen, and it was their air and their power.

The cause lay years in the past. The tank's heater thermostats had been built for 28 volts, but the launch pad used 65. During a ground test before the flight, the heaters had run hot enough to melt the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank, and nobody knew. When Swigert stirred the tank, a spark touched that damaged wiring. The oxygen ignited, the tank ruptured, and the blast blew off an entire panel of the service module.

Within minutes, the mission changed. A lunar landing was impossible. The only question was how to get three men home alive.

The command module, Odyssey, was dying. Its fuel cells needed oxygen to make electricity, and the oxygen was gone. Its batteries would be needed for the final descent, so they had to be saved. The crew did the only thing they could. They crawled into the lunar module, Aquarius, and treated it as a lifeboat. It had been designed to support two men for about two days on the lunar surface. Now it would have to sustain three for four.

Mission Control, led by flight director Gene Kranz, did not panic, though the problems stacked up quickly. The first decision was the trajectory. Aborting directly, by turning around, would have required using the damaged service module's main engine, which no one trusted. Instead the controllers chose to swing the ship around the Moon and let lunar gravity sling it back toward Earth. Aquarius's descent engine, meant for landing, would provide the burns. The first came about two hours after the Moon's far side was passed, and it put the ship on a faster path home.

Then came the slow, grinding problems. Power had to be rationed to the bone. The crew shut down nearly everything in Aquarius that wasn't essential, including most of the guidance computer, the heaters, and the lights. The cabin temperature fell toward 38 degrees Fahrenheit. Water was cut to a few ounces a day each, and Haise developed a kidney infection with a fever. They slept little. They were cold, thirsty, and very tired.

Carbon dioxide was the sharpest danger. Aquarius's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrub exhaled carbon dioxide from the air, were rated for two people. The command module had plenty of canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground, working with the same materials the crew had aboard, designed an adapter from cardboard, plastic bags, suction hoses, and duct tape. They read the instructions up to the crew, who built it. It worked. The carbon dioxide level dropped back to safe limits.

A further problem remained. The spacecraft had drifted slightly off course, and a small correction burn was needed. With the guidance platform shut down to save power, the crew could not rely on the usual instruments. Lovell steered by lining up the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, in a window. Two later burns, one of them manual, kept the path steady.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the man who had been taken off the flight, worked in a simulator on the ground. His task was to write a procedure for powering Odyssey back up from a near-dead state, using only the tiny amount of electricity that remained. The usual startup took hours and drew too much power. He found a way to do it with far less. Many people on the ground later pointed to that single procedure as the thing that saved the mission.

On April 17, the crew prepared to come home. First they jettisoned the damaged service module. As it drifted away, they got their first look at the damage: an entire side of the ship was gone, and parts hung loose. Lovell reported it in a calm voice. Then they moved back into Odyssey, powered it up, and let go of Aquarius, the little lifeboat that had kept them alive.

Reentry meant a blackout in radio contact, usually about three minutes. This time it ran longer, and in Houston the controllers waited in silence. The extra delay came from a slightly shallower angle of entry. Four minutes passed, then more. At last, Swigert's voice came through, and on television, three parachutes bloomed over the South Pacific. Odyssey splashed down near the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima at 1:07 p.m. Houston time, on April 17, 1970.

The crew had lost weight, and Haise was ill. But all three were alive.

The investigation that followed, led by a review board, traced the accident to the tank and its heaters, and NASA redesigned the oxygen system. Apollo 14 flew the next year, and the landings went on. Lovell never reached the Moon, though he had flown there twice. The mission, a failure by its own goals, is remembered as a triumph of improvisation, of engineers on the ground and three men in a freezing capsule solving problem after problem, together. Kranz's team had a phrase for what they believed in. They said failure was not an option, and in this case, it was not.
