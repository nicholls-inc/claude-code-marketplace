The Great Molasses Flood

January 15, 1919, was unusually mild in Boston. After a bitter cold spell, the temperature had climbed into the forties. People in the North End, a crowded waterfront neighborhood of Italian immigrants, dockworkers and city laborers, went out without heavy coats. Children gathered scraps of firewood near the harbor, and workmen ate lunch in the sun.

Above Commercial Street stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The molasses was waiting to be turned into industrial alcohol, which went into munitions and, increasingly, into products that would soon be caught up in Prohibition. The tank had been full only a few days. A ship had arrived from the Caribbean shortly before and topped it up.

Just after half past noon, witnesses heard a deep rumble, then a series of sharp cracks that some compared to machine-gun fire. Those were the rivets bursting from the tank's seams. The steel walls tore apart and the molasses came out all at once.

It came down the street as a brown wave roughly twenty-five feet high, moving at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It knocked buildings off their foundations, bent the steel girders of the elevated railway, and threw a train car off its track. A firehouse was pushed toward the water and partly crushed, trapping the firemen inside. Horses, wagons and people were picked up and carried along.

Molasses is thick, and that made it especially deadly. People who were caught in it could not swim through it or pull themselves free. As the afternoon air cooled, it grew thicker still. Rescuers waded in up to their waists. Among the first to arrive were cadets from a training ship docked nearby, followed by police, Red Cross nurses and soldiers. They pulled out survivors coated so heavily that they were hard to recognize. Some victims were not found for days, and one body surfaced in the harbor months later.

Twenty-one people died, among them a ten-year-old boy on his way home from school, and about 150 were injured. Cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed seawater from fireboats to break up the molasses and wash it into the harbor, which stayed brown well into the summer. For decades, longtime residents said the neighborhood smelled sweet on hot days.

The company first blamed anarchists, claiming someone had blown up the tank. The survivors and the victims' families sued, and the case dragged on for years. The court-appointed auditor heard testimony from about three thousand witnesses, and the trial record ran to tens of thousands of pages. In the end he found that the tank had been badly built and never properly tested. It was supposed to be tested by filling it with water, which never happened. It had leaked from the start, and the company's answer was to paint it brown so the leaks would not show. The man who oversaw its construction was a financial officer who could not read a blueprint.

In 1925 the company paid the plaintiffs about $628,000. The case helped change the rules: Massachusetts and other states began requiring that engineers and architects sign off on their calculations before construction could begin. Today the site is a park with ball fields and a bocce court. A small plaque marks what happened there.
