In late August 1854, cholera arrived in Soho, a crowded, working-class district of London, and it did not behave like an ordinary illness. Cholera is a disease that drains the body of fluid through violent diarrhoea and vomiting, and it could kill a healthy adult within a day or two. Within a few days of the first cases, hundreds of people living near Broad Street were dying, residents fled and shops closed, and nearly everyone assumed they knew the cause: foul air, or "miasma," rising from rotting waste and filth. The germ theory of disease had not yet been accepted, and no one had seen the tiny organism that actually causes cholera.

John Snow, a physician who lived a short walk away, was not convinced by the foul-air explanation. Snow was a respected man in his profession, known for his work on anaesthesia, and he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth, but he had also been thinking about cholera for years. During an earlier epidemic in 1848 and 1849, he had concluded that the disease was carried in something people swallowed, probably contaminated water, and that it attacked the gut first. If cholera were a poison inhaled from the air, he reasoned, it should strike the lungs and the whole body at once, yet its symptoms began in the digestive system. He had published this argument in 1849, and most of his colleagues had ignored it.

The Broad Street outbreak gave him a chance to test his idea, and he began by doing something simple and unglamorous: he walked the streets and asked questions. He obtained the list of the dead from the local registrar and went door to door, trying to learn where each victim had lived and what they had drunk, then marked the deaths on a map of the neighbourhood with a small bar for each one, stacked at the address where the person had died. The pattern was striking: the deaths clustered tightly around a water pump on Broad Street, where residents drew their drinking water by hand.

A cluster alone does not prove a cause, so Snow looked harder at the exceptions, which turned out to be the most convincing part of his case. A workhouse nearby housed hundreds of people, yet very few of them died, and Snow discovered that it had its own well and did not rely on the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street also escaped almost untouched, since its workers were given malt liquor to drink and the brewery had its own supply of water too. In the other direction, he heard of a woman who lived far away in Hampstead and died of cholera after her family sent her bottles of water from the Broad Street pump, because she liked the taste, and her niece, who drank the same water, died as well, though neither had any other connection to Soho.

By the first week of September, Snow was ready to present his evidence to the local Board of Guardians, the men who governed the parish, and he asked them to remove the handle of the Broad Street pump so that no one could draw water from it. They agreed, though with little enthusiasm, and the handle came off on 8 September. It is tempting to say that this single act ended the epidemic, but the truth is more modest: the outbreak had already been fading, because many residents had fled and many of the most vulnerable were already dead, and Snow himself acknowledged that the numbers had been falling before the handle was removed.

Even so, his work was not finished, and a local clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who knew the neighbourhood far better than Snow did and who had at first doubted him, began his own inquiries. Whitehead discovered what appears to have been the start of it all: a baby girl who lived at 40 Broad Street had fallen ill with cholera just before the outbreak exploded, and her mother had washed the child's soiled cloths and thrown the dirty water into a cesspool, which lay only a few feet from the pump's well. Later inspection showed that the brickwork lining the well was cracked, allowing waste to seep in.

Remarkably, the authorities did not embrace Snow's conclusions, since official inquiries preferred the foul-air explanation and the parish soon replaced the pump handle. Snow died in 1858, aged only forty-five, without seeing his theory accepted, yet his method outlived him. By counting cases, mapping them, and searching for patterns, he showed that disease could be studied as a problem of evidence rather than of opinion, and today he is often called a founding figure of epidemiology, the science of how illness spreads through populations. The next time you see a map of an outbreak, or hear of investigators tracing an infection back to its source, you are seeing the legacy of a doctor walking the streets of Soho in the summer of 1854.
