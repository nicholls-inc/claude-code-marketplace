Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and who can only talk by shouting across a crowded square. Everyone can hear every word. They want to end up sharing a secret, something an eavesdropper could not work out even after hearing the whole conversation. It sounds impossible, and for thousands of years it was believed to be. Before the 1970s, any two people who wanted to send secret messages first had to meet in private and agree on a code. The idea that changed this is called public-key cryptography, and its core is a trick that uses an everyday fact about arithmetic: some calculations are easy to do forwards and extremely hard to undo.

A good way to picture the trick is with paint, so suppose Alice and Bob publicly agree on a starting colour, say yellow, and each of them privately picks a secret colour and mixes a big batch of it with the yellow. Alice sends her mixture across the square, and Bob sends his. Everyone sees the two mixtures, but nobody can separate a mixed paint back into its ingredients. Now Alice takes Bob's mixture and adds her own secret colour to it, while Bob takes Alice's mixture and adds his secret colour. Both end up with exactly the same final shade, because it contains yellow, Alice's secret and Bob's secret, and the order of mixing makes no difference. The eavesdropper has the yellow and the two half-mixtures, but to finish the job would need one of the secret colours, which never left its owner's hands.

Mathematics can play the same game, and the paint mixing is replaced by something you may remember from school: remainders. Think of a clock, where if it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, it is three o'clock, not fifteen. You have done addition, but you have thrown away whole laps of the clock and kept only the remainder. Mathematicians call this clock arithmetic, and it works with any size of clock. On a clock with 23 marks, say, every number you can reach lies between 0 and 22, and anything bigger wraps around. We write the remainder after dividing by 23 as "mod 23", so fifteen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, divided by 23, leaves a remainder of 8.

Now bring in repeated multiplication: take the number 5 and multiply it by itself six times, which is written 5 to the power 6, comes to 15,625, and on the 23-mark clock lands on 8. The crucial point is that if I tell you the starting number 5, the clock size 23 and the number of multiplications 6, you can find 8 with a pencil and a little patience, but if I only tell you the starting number 5, the clock size 23 and the answer 8, and ask how many times I multiplied, the answer comes out looking scrambled, with no tidy pattern, because the wrapping around the clock keeps jumbling the results. You can still find it by trying 1, 2, 3 and so on until you hit 8, and with a clock this small that takes moments, so the method only fails when the clock is huge.

Here is the whole exchange with these small numbers. Alice and Bob publicly agree on the clock size 23 and the starting number 5, which everyone hears and which is fine. Alice privately picks a secret number, 6, computes 5 multiplied by itself six times on the clock, gets 8, and shouts "8" across the square. Bob privately picks his own secret, 15, computes 5 multiplied by itself fifteen times on the clock, gets 19, and shouts "19".

Now comes the clever step. Alice takes Bob's public number, 19, and multiplies it by itself six times, her secret number, on the 23-mark clock, and the result is 2. Bob takes Alice's public number, 8, and multiplies it by itself fifteen times, his secret number, on the same clock, and the result is also 2. They have arrived at the same number, and neither ever said it aloud.

They must match because multiplying 5 by itself six times and then multiplying the result by itself fifteen times is the same as multiplying 5 by itself ninety times in one go, and it makes no difference whether the six or the fifteen came first. Each person has effectively done all ninety multiplications, but each contributed only their own share privately. It is the paint story again, with the order of mixing not mattering.

The eavesdropper hears only the clock size 23, the starting number 5, and the two shouted numbers 8 and 19, and to get the shared secret they would need Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. With a clock of 23 marks they could simply try every possibility and find them in seconds, which is why this example is only a toy. Real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits, so the number of possible secrets is larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe, and trying them one by one is hopeless. Cleverer methods than guessing exist, but none is known that makes the problem easy for the best-designed clocks, even with the fastest computers in the world. Alice and Bob, by contrast, can still do their forward calculations quickly, thanks to a shortcut: instead of multiplying a number by itself a billion times, they can square it, square the result, and so on, reaching enormous powers in just a few dozen steps, wrapping around the clock each time so the numbers never grow out of hand.

This arrangement is sometimes called key exchange, and the shared number, once agreed, is used as the key for an ordinary lock-and-key code that scrambles the rest of their conversation. Such codes are fast, but they have the problem we started with, which is that both sides need the same key in advance. Public-key methods solve that problem by letting the key be created in the open.

There is a second, closely related use of the same idea, which is where the name "public key" really comes from. Imagine that instead of two people building one shared secret, each person owns a matched pair of keys. One is public, published for anyone to see, and one is private, never shared. The two are linked by arithmetic of the same easy-forwards, hard-backwards kind, so that whatever the public key locks, only the private key can unlock. It works like a letterbox: anyone can post a letter through the slot, but only the owner holds the key that opens the box. A stranger who has never met you can look up your public key and send you a message that no one else, not even the sender, can read afterwards. Banks, websites and messaging apps rely on this all the time, usually just to agree on a fresh shared key for the rest of the session, much as Alice and Bob did.

One caution is worth adding: the scheme as described protects against someone who only listens, but it does not by itself protect against someone who stands in the middle and pretends to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, swapping in their own numbers. Defeating that needs a separate ingredient, a way of checking that a public number really belongs to the person claiming it, and real systems build that in. But the central marvel stands. Two strangers, speaking entirely in the open, can build a secret together, because mixing is easy, unmixing is not, and the order of mixing does not matter.
