Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon, because the rule has spent seventy years forcing everyone to pay for something that only some people use.

Minimum parking rules began as a well-meant guess. Traffic engineers in the postwar decades measured how many cars arrived at the busiest suburban shopping centers on the busiest days of the year, then planners turned those peaks into formulas: so many spaces per thousand square feet of retail, so many per bedroom, so many per restaurant seat. Those numbers were written into zoning codes and have rarely been revisited since, even though the sample they came from was a handful of surveys of places that already had ample free parking. Donald Shoup, the UCLA planning professor whose book The High Cost of Free Parking made this case thoroughly, found that the manuals behind the formulas often rested on a few dozen observations and that the figures varied wildly between cities for the same kind of building, which is hard to square with the idea that they reflect any real science of need.

The cost of that guesswork lands on people who may never benefit from it. A single structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one considerably more, and a developer who must provide it recovers the money through higher rents and higher prices on everyone in the building, whether they own a car or not. A household that gets by without a vehicle, often because it cannot afford one, ends up subsidizing the storage of its neighbors' vehicles. In a city with a housing shortage this is a strange policy to keep, since a requirement that adds a fifth or more to the cost of an apartment block will reliably produce fewer apartments, or more expensive ones, or none at all on lots that cannot physically fit the mandated spaces alongside the homes.

The land arithmetic is just as stark. A surface space with its share of aisle and driveway takes up roughly three hundred square feet, so a modest requirement can cover more of a lot with asphalt than with the building it was supposed to serve. Small sites in older neighborhoods, the corner lots and narrow infill parcels where a duplex or a corner shop would be most welcome, are often rendered unbuildable by the code, which is why so many of them sit vacant or turn into the very parking lots the rule demands. Downtowns that were once dense and walkable have been hollowed out in this way, with the gaps between buildings filled by the storage space that the buildings were obliged to provide.

The objection most people raise is that without requirements, new buildings will have no parking and the surrounding streets will fill with spillover cars. This worry deserves a real answer, and the answer is that removing a mandate is not the same as banning parking. Cities that have repealed their minimums, including Buffalo, which did so citywide in 2017, and Minneapolis, which followed in 2021, have not seen developers abandon parking altogether, because lenders, tenants, and buyers still want it in most places and builders know it. What changes is that the amount becomes a decision someone can make with attention to the specific site and the specific customer. A building near a rail station can offer fewer spaces and lower rents, a suburban one can keep plenty, and a developer who misjudges the market bears the loss rather than the whole city bearing the consequences of a formula.

Where spillover really does become a problem, the better tool is to price the curb rather than to force every new building to hide the issue by overbuilding. Residential permit programs and metered street parking whose rates adjust to demand can keep a block from jamming without requiring any particular building to supply anything, and the revenue can fund local improvements that make residents more willing to accept a few more neighbors. Free curb space is not a natural right so much as a subsidy that the code has taught people to expect, and it is reasonable to ask the people who use it to contribute something toward its upkeep.

There is also a straightforward argument from fairness and from climate. Requirements bake in an assumption that every household will drive, and they make that assumption true by spreading destinations apart and making walking, biking, and transit less practical. A city that wants more people to have the option of living without a car cannot also insist that every building be designed as though they must. Parking minimums push the other way on both counts, raising the cost of homes close to jobs and services while lowering the cost of driving, and they do so quietly, through a line in the code that few residents have ever read.

None of this requires cities to be hostile to drivers or to pretend that cars will vanish. It asks only that the amount of parking in a building be set by the people who will use and pay for it, with the city stepping in at the curb where congestion is real. The evidence from places that have tried it is that development continues, housing gets cheaper to build, and the parking that people truly want still gets built. A rule that cannot show it is doing more good than harm, and that costs money and homes every year it remains on the books, has no business staying in a planning code.
