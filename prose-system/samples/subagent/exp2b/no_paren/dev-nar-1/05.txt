In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness fell upon Soho, a crowded and poor district of London. The illness was cholera, a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhea, drains the body of fluid, and can kill a healthy adult within a day or two. In the span of about ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few streets of one another died. Those who could leave fled. Shops closed, and whole families vanished from their rooms.

At the time, nobody knew what caused cholera. Germs had not yet been shown to cause disease, and the dominant belief among doctors was the miasma theory. This held that illness traveled on foul air, rising from rotting waste, swamps and filth. It was a reasonable guess in a city that stank. London had no proper system for disposing of sewage, and cesspools beneath houses overflowed regularly.

John Snow, a physician living nearby, did not accept this view. He was a careful and curious man, already respected for his work on anesthesia, having given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He had also studied cholera during an earlier epidemic in 1848 and 1849. He noticed that the disease first attacked the bowels, not the lungs. If bad air were the culprit, he reasoned, why would the gut suffer first? It seemed far more likely that people were swallowing something that made them ill. He suspected contaminated water.

When the Soho outbreak began, Snow saw a chance to test his idea. He went door to door in the stricken streets, asking about the dead and about the habits of the living. He obtained lists of deaths from the local register office. Then he did something that proved crucial. He marked each death on a map of the neighborhood, drawing a small bar at the address where each victim had lived. As the bars piled up, a pattern emerged. The deaths clustered thickly around a street pump on Broad Street, where residents drew their drinking water.

A cluster alone was not proof, and Snow knew it. So he looked for exceptions, which are often more revealing than rules. Nearby was a workhouse housing about five hundred people, yet only five of them died. Investigating, he found the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. Likewise, a brewery on Broad Street employed dozens of workers, and none of them died. The men were given malt liquor to drink, and the brewery also had its own well. Snow also found the reverse. A woman living far away, in Hampstead, died of cholera after a carriage brought her bottles of water from the Broad Street pump. She had liked its taste, and her family fetched it regularly. Her niece, who drank the same water, also fell ill and died.

Snow gathered his evidence and took it to the local board of guardians, the officials responsible for the parish. On the evening of September 7, 1854, he urged them to act. Their response was skeptical, but they agreed to a modest step. The next day, the handle of the Broad Street pump was removed, so that no more water could be drawn from it.

It is often said that this single act ended the epidemic. The truth is less tidy. The outbreak had already been fading before the handle came off, perhaps because so many residents had fled or died, and the number of new cases was falling on its own. Even so, Snow believed the removal helped, and his broader case remained powerful.

His next task was to find the source of the contamination. A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, had at first doubted Snow's theory. But Whitehead knew the community intimately and set out to prove or disprove it. His inquiries led him to a baby girl who had fallen ill with cholera shortly before the outbreak exploded. Her family lived at 40 Broad Street, directly beside the pump. Her mother had washed the child's soiled diapers and thrown the water into a cesspool just in front of the house. Examination later showed the cesspool was decayed and leaking, very close to the well that fed the pump. Waste from one sick infant had seeped into the water supply of the whole district.

Whitehead became convinced and joined Snow in presenting the findings. Even so, the authorities were slow to accept them. An official inquiry concluded that the evidence did not prove water was the cause, and the miasma theory persisted for years. Snow died in 1858 at the age of forty-five, without seeing his ideas triumph.

Yet his legacy proved enormous. Later research confirmed that cholera is carried by a microscopic organism in contaminated water, and cities across the world rebuilt their sewers and water systems in response. Snow's method also shaped how we study disease today. By mapping cases, comparing those who got sick with those who did not, and following the evidence wherever it led, he helped found the modern science of epidemiology.
