The Earth does not keep perfect time. It spins a little unevenly, and over the long run it is slowing down, as the pull of the Moon's tides acts like a gentle brake. A leap second is the patch that reconciles the planet with the clock.

Here is the problem it solves. Since 1967, the world's official time has been set by atomic clocks, which count out seconds with astonishing steadiness, drifting by less than a second over many millions of years. Our days, however, are measured by the turning of the Earth, and that turning is messier. Some days run a fraction of a millisecond long. Others run short. Left alone, the two kinds of time would slowly part company, and eventually noon on the clock would no longer match the Sun at its highest point.

To prevent that, international authorities began adding a leap second to the end of a chosen day, usually on 30 June or 31 December. On those occasions the clocks read 23:59:59, then 23:59:60, and only then roll over to midnight. Since the practice began in 1972, 27 leap seconds have been added. The last came at the end of 2016.

So why stop? The trouble is that the modern world runs on computers, and computers dislike a minute with 61 seconds in it. Most software assumes every minute has exactly 60. When a leap second arrives, some systems freeze, some repeat a second, and some record events out of order. In 2012 a leap second helped knock several well-known websites offline. Big technology companies now spread the extra second across a whole day, nudging their clocks slightly slower, but each company does it its own way, which creates new disagreements. For financial markets, power grids, satellite navigation and telecom networks, where timestamps must be exact and consistent, the risk is real.

There is a further twist. In recent years the Earth has begun spinning slightly faster than expected, which raises the prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second is removed. That has never been tried, and engineers fear it would be even more disruptive.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body that oversees the international system of units, voted to abandon leap seconds by 2035. After that date, the gap between atomic time and Earth time will be allowed to grow, to a margin yet to be fixed, perhaps a minute or more. Nobody expects ordinary people to notice. It would take a century or so to build up a single minute of difference.

Astronomers and navigators who need to track the Earth's true orientation will have to make their own corrections. For everyone else, the clock will simply keep ticking, smoothly and without surprises.
