Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case for dropping it is not foolish. Children now spend much of their lives in front of screens, and adults rarely write more than a shopping list or a birthday card by hand. Several countries have already moved on. Finland removed compulsory cursive from its national curriculum in 2016, and many American states have wavered over it for years. If the only question were what skills a ten-year-old will use most in adult life, cursive might well lose to touch typing. But that is not the only question, and I think the schools that have dropped it, or taught it half-heartedly, have given up something more useful than they realised.

The first reason is the plainest: handwriting is still a daily tool for children, and a fluent hand makes it a better one. Pupils in primary school write constantly, in exercise books, on worksheets, in tests, and in the margins of their own thinking. A child who forms letters slowly, one at a time, with the pen lifted after every stroke, is carrying a heavy load during all of that work. Joined writing, taught well, lets the hand move in a continuous rhythm, so that whole words become single motions. Teachers who have watched a class move from laboured print to a confident running hand will tell you the difference in speed and stamina is real. Writing quickly without losing legibility matters most in exam halls, where it still counts for a great deal.

That point deserves some care, because the research is not as tidy as enthusiasts sometimes claim. Studies comparing print and cursive have produced mixed results, and some find no clear advantage for either when both are taught properly. What the better studies agree on is narrower and more interesting. Fluency matters, and fluency comes from a style that is practised until it is automatic. If a school is going to teach handwriting at all, the evidence suggests that a simple, consistent, joined style is among the most efficient to teach, because it removes the repeated decision about where each letter starts and stops. A child who prints in large, separate capitals and block letters can be slow and neat, or fast and illegible, but rarely both fast and neat. The joined style is a reasonable route to the last of these.

The second reason concerns what happens in the brain, and here I want to be modest about what is known. Neuroscientists who study reading and writing, including research groups in Norway and France, have found that forming letters by hand engages the brain differently from pressing keys. Children who learn letters by drawing them tend to recognise them more reliably than children who only type them, and brain imaging suggests that the motor memory of making a shape supports the visual memory of seeing it. Most of this work compares handwriting with typing, not print with cursive, so it cannot settle our question by itself. Still, it gives a strong reason to protect handwriting as a whole, and joined-up writing is the form in which handwriting is most developed. To treat it as a decorative extra is to misunderstand how much practice goes into learning to write at all.

A third reason is access to the written record. A great deal of the world is written in cursive, and not just old manuscripts. Letters from grandparents, notes in a family recipe book, a teacher's comments on a piece of work, a signed card, a diary found in a drawer: these are all written in a joined hand, and a child who has never been taught to read it is shut out from them. This may sound sentimental, but it is a practical form of literacy. Archivists and historians already report students who cannot read primary sources from the last century, let alone earlier ones. A child who can write cursive can almost always read it, and the reverse is much less certain. Reading it is a smaller skill than writing it, but the cheapest way to learn to read it is to learn to write it.

The fourth reason is that a good handwriting lesson teaches something that few other parts of the timetable do. It asks a child to slow down, pay attention to a physical task, and improve through repetition and small corrections. Those are habits that carry over into music, drawing, sport, and careful thinking. Children who struggle with handwriting are often children who struggle to organise their movements, and early practice can help them in ways that typing does not. For children with dyslexia the picture is more complicated, and some teachers find that a joined style helps because it reduces reversals of letters like b and d, while others find it adds difficulty. This is an argument for teaching flexibly and early, with support for those who need it, not for dropping the subject for everyone.

The strongest objection to all of this is about time. The primary curriculum is crowded, and every minute spent on cursive is a minute not spent on reading, mathematics, science, or learning to type. I take this seriously. But the cost of teaching joined-up writing is small when it is done well. Ten or fifteen minutes a day of short, focused practice over a couple of years is enough to give most children a functional running hand, and it can be woven into spelling and writing lessons instead of standing apart. The real waste is in teaching it badly: asking children to copy pages of ornate loops in a style that nobody writes, then ignoring handwriting entirely once they move up the school. A plain, modern, lightly joined script, taught briefly and consistently, gives most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost.

A second objection is that typing is simply more useful, and that children should be taught it first. There is something in this, and I would not argue for handwriting at the expense of keyboard skills. By the end of primary school children should be able to type with reasonable speed, and most schools do not teach it systematically enough. But the two skills do not compete as much as the objection supposes. They serve different purposes, and the evidence on note-taking suggests that writing by hand has its own advantages, since it forces a student to select and summarise rather than record every word. A child who can do both is better equipped than a child who can do only one. It is also worth remembering that devices fail, batteries die, and many exams and everyday forms still require a pen.

A third objection is about fairness. Some say that insisting on joined writing penalises children who find it hard, and that legibility is all that matters. I agree that legibility is the real goal, and that no child should be marked down for a style that is clear but unconventional. This is a reason to be sensible about how we assess handwriting, not a reason to avoid teaching it. We do not stop teaching spelling because some children find it difficult. We teach it with patience, give extra help, and accept that outcomes will vary. The same approach suits handwriting. The aim is not a page of identical copperplate but a hand that is quick, clear, and comfortable, and joined writing is a sound way to get there for most pupils.

It is also worth asking what we lose if we let the skill lapse, because the loss would be hard to reverse. Once a generation of teachers has stopped teaching cursive, the expertise goes with them. Training disappears, materials go out of print, and parents who might have taught it at home assume that schools have decided it no longer matters. Countries that have dropped it have sometimes had second thoughts, and several American states have since passed laws to bring it back. That pattern is a warning. It is easy to remove a subject from the curriculum in a single reform and much harder to rebuild it, and the people who pay for the mistake are children who never had the chance to learn.

None of this requires nostalgia. I do not want to return to the days of rapped knuckles and inkwells, or to the idea that a beautiful hand is a mark of good character. The argument is a modest one, that a well-taught joined hand is faster than print for most writers, supports the early learning of letters, opens up the written past, and trains attention and control in a way few other lessons do. Against that stands a cost of a few minutes a day, which is easily met. Schools should teach a clear, simple, joined style in the middle years of primary school, give children who struggle the support they need, and teach typing alongside it. A child who leaves primary school able to write fluently by hand and type competently has lost nothing and gained two useful skills. A child who leaves with neither has been shortchanged, however modern the curriculum that failed them may have looked.
