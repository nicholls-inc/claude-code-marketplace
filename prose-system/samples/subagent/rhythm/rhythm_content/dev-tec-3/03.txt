A lithium-ion cell is charged with a two-stage protocol called constant-current, constant-voltage, and the reason it has two stages follows from the fact that the voltage you can measure at the terminals is not the voltage of the electrochemistry inside. Lithium ions are shuttled from the cathode, typically a layered metal oxide, through a porous separator soaked in liquid electrolyte, and inserted between the graphene sheets of the graphite anode, while electrons travel through the external circuit to keep charge balanced. The charger first forces a fixed current, commonly around 0.5 to 1 C, meaning a current that would fill the nominal capacity in one to two hours, and lets terminal voltage rise as the anode fills and the cathode empties. The terminal voltage is the sum of the open-circuit voltage, which depends on how much lithium sits in each electrode, and an overpotential equal to the current times the cell's internal resistance, which comprises ohmic drops in the electrolyte and current collectors, charge-transfer resistance at the electrode surfaces, and a concentration term from ions piling up faster than they can diffuse. When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, about 4.2 V for a conventional cobalt-based chemistry, the charger stops regulating current and instead holds the voltage fixed. That limit is a hard ceiling because pushing the cathode much beyond it accelerates electrolyte oxidation and structural damage, and charging the anode too hard at low potential risks plating metallic lithium instead of intercalating it, which is a safety hazard and a source of permanent capacity loss. Once voltage is pinned, the open-circuit voltage keeps climbing as the electrodes fill, so the share of the fixed terminal voltage left over to drive current, the overpotential, shrinks steadily, and by Ohm's law the current must fall with it. In effect the cell behaves like a capacitor charging through a resistor, so the current decays roughly exponentially, though the diffusion of lithium inside solid particles, which becomes slower as the host lattice fills and gradients flatten, stretches the tail beyond what a simple resistor-capacitor model would predict. Because the cell is spending this stage with its state of charge changing only slowly while the current drops toward a cutoff, usually about a tenth or less of the initial rate, the last 20 percent of capacity can take as long as the first 80. Faster charging is therefore limited not by the charger but by how quickly ions can cross the electrolyte and enter particles without driving the anode surface below the plating threshold, and this is why cold cells, aged cells with higher resistance, and thick high-energy electrodes all reach the voltage ceiling sooner and spend more time in the slow phase.
