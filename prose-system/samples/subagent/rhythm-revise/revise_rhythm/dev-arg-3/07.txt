Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it with more confidence than they have lately shown.

The case against it is not foolish and deserves a fair hearing: children now grow up among keyboards and touchscreens, and most of the writing they will do as adults will be typed. Cursive, the argument goes, is a relic like the abacus or the slide rule: charming, historically interesting, and no longer worth the hours it consumes in a crowded curriculum. Several American states dropped it after the Common Core standards left it out, and plenty of teachers elsewhere quietly let it slide in favour of print or early typing. If a child can already form clear letters, why insist that she then learn a second set of shapes and a new way of connecting them, when that time is surely better spent on reading, mathematics, or coding?

The objection rests on a premise I think is mistaken, namely that handwriting is merely a way of getting words onto a surface, and that the keyboard simply does the job faster. If that were all handwriting was, the argument would be hard to resist, but learning to write by hand is a form of training in attention, motor control, and thought rather than the mere acquisition of an output method, and joined-up writing in particular offers things that printing and typing do not.

Start with the physical facts: writing in a joined style is a continuous, flowing movement. Where a child printing letters lifts the pen after every stroke, deciding afresh where to start each one, the child writing in joined letters moves through a word as a single gesture. That fluency matters, because handwriting that is slow and effortful consumes working memory that a young writer needs for the actual business of composing: choosing words, building sentences, holding an idea in mind while finding a way to express it. Researchers who study writing have long observed that children who struggle to form letters automatically tend to write shorter, simpler, and poorer compositions, not because they lack ideas but because the mechanics eat their attention. A well-learned joined hand is quick, and its speed frees the mind. Print, done properly, can be quick too, but in practice many children who stay with print never get past a stiff, laboured, stop-start pace, and the join gives the hand a rhythm that print lacks.

Spelling is another matter, and here too the joined hand helps: a word written in a joined hand is learned as a unit, a pattern of movement as well as a sequence of letters. Teachers of young children often notice that the motor memory of a word helps fix its spelling, and that common letter strings such as the endings of words or the awkward clusters of English become easier because the hand learns them as a shape, which is no trivial benefit in a language as irregular as ours. Typing teaches a different pattern of movement, and one that is far less tied to the identity of individual letters; the finger that strikes a key has no resemblance to the form it produces. The hand that writes a letter has to make the letter.

That point leads to the best-known body of evidence in this debate, which is also the most contested, so I will be careful about what I claim. A number of studies, including work by psychologists and neuroscientists in the United States, Norway and elsewhere, have found that writing by hand engages the brain differently from typing. Children who learn letters by drawing them seem to recognise those letters more readily than children who learn by pressing keys, and brain imaging suggests that the act of forming a letter activates networks involved in reading. Studies of note-taking by university students have found, with some controversy over how well the results replicate, that those writing by hand tend to process material more deeply because they cannot transcribe everything and must select and summarise. I do not want to overstate this, since much of the research compares handwriting with typing, not joined handwriting with print, and the difference between the two handwritten styles is less well documented than the difference between pen and keyboard. But the direction of the evidence is clear enough: whatever is valuable about writing by hand, a school that treats it as optional is gambling with something that appears to matter. And cursive is the form of handwriting that most naturally rewards practice, because it is the form in which a person eventually develops speed and a personal style.

Which brings me to something that is easy to dismiss and unwise to: handwriting is one of the few skills a child can see herself improving. The first wobbling attempts at a looped letter give way over months to something fluent and recognisably her own. For many children, especially those who find reading or arithmetic difficult, this is a visible and honest kind of progress, and the satisfaction of a neat page matters more to their sense of themselves as learners than adults generally admit. It is also a craft, with a modest aesthetic dimension: a child who has learned to make her writing look good has learned that care and practice produce results, and that the way something is made is part of its meaning. We should not be embarrassed to say that this is worth teaching for its own sake.

A practical consideration points the same way, since adults do still write by hand, constantly: on forms, in cards, in margins, on whiteboards, in exams, on shopping lists, in the notebooks where ideas first take shape. Most examinations in most countries are still handwritten, and a student whose hand tires after a page or whose writing is illegible under time pressure is at a real disadvantage. A fluent joined hand is, for most people, faster and less tiring than print over long stretches. Moreover, being unable to read cursive is its own kind of illiteracy. A child who never learns it cannot read the handwriting of her grandmother, the annotations in an old book, or the primary sources that historians rely on, and the number of people able to decipher a handwritten letter shrinks with every cohort that is not taught. Teaching joined-up writing is partly teaching children to read their own cultural inheritance.

Defenders of dropping cursive sometimes reply that it is difficult to teach and that many children never master it. This is true as far as it goes, but it is an argument for teaching it better, not for abandoning it. The common failures come from starting late, teaching it in a rushed way, switching between styles, or treating handwriting as something to be done in odd moments rather than taught explicitly and practised briefly and often. Where schools adopt a single consistent script, with simple joins taught from early on, and give short, regular practice sessions, most children acquire a legible, reasonably quick hand without strain. Some pupils, notably those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties, will always find it hard, and they should be supported with alternatives such as keyboards. But we do not stop teaching reading because dyslexia exists; we adapt. The existence of exceptions does not undermine the rule.

I would also resist the false choice at the heart of the debate. Nobody serious argues that children should not learn to type, and they should learn it early, because keyboard skills are indispensable and are best learned properly rather than picked up as bad habits. The question is not handwriting or keyboards but whether handwriting, and specifically its fluent joined form, deserves a place in a limited school day. The time cost is modest, since a few minutes a day over a few years is not what stands between a child and a modern education, and what it buys is a skill with cognitive, practical, and cultural returns that exceeds its price by a wide margin.

None of this requires nostalgia, or rulers rapped on knuckles, or a belief that a beautiful hand is a sign of good character. It requires only the recognition that the hand and the mind are connected, that learning to write is not the same as learning to produce text, and that a skill which trains attention, speeds composition, supports spelling, and keeps children in touch with their own written past is worth the small effort it takes to teach. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, teach it explicitly and well, and stop apologising for it.
