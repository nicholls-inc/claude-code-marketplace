Keep the Joined Hand in the Classroom

Every few years a headline announces the death of cursive. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, so children should spend their precious classroom hours on something more useful than looping letters together. It is a tidy argument and it sounds modern. I think it is wrong. Primary schools should keep teaching joined up handwriting, and they should teach it with confidence rather than apology. The skill is not a nostalgic ornament. It supports fluent writing, it helps children think, it keeps them able to read the written record of their own families and culture, and it gives them a tool that works when no screen is within reach. Dropping it would save very little time and cost a great deal.

It helps to be clear about what is actually being debated. Nobody serious is proposing that children should stop using keyboards, and nobody sensible wants to return to hours of copying out rows of identical letters under the gaze of a stern teacher. The real question is narrow. Once a child can form print letters, should the school go on to teach how those letters connect into a flowing hand, or should it stop there and let children drift into whatever habits they happen to find? I say the school should teach the joins, and the reasons are practical rather than sentimental.

Start with fluency. Print letters are made one at a time. The pen lifts after every letter, lands again, and starts a new shape. Joined writing lets the hand travel through a whole word in a single movement, which is faster once the pattern is learned and also kinder to the wrist, because the writer is not stopping and starting hundreds of times a page. Speed is not a vanity here. A child who writes slowly must hold a half finished sentence in mind for longer, and every moment spent working out how to make a letter is a moment taken away from deciding what to say. Researchers who study writing development, such as Steve Graham and Virginia Berninger, have long argued that handwriting that has become automatic frees up mental space for composition, and that children who struggle with the mechanics tend to write shorter and simpler texts than they are capable of. Fluent joining is one of the clearest routes to that automatic quality.

Joining also shapes how children understand words. When a child writes the letters of a word in one continuous movement, the word becomes a unit with a rhythm rather than a pile of separate marks. Common endings such as ing, ed and tion become familiar motions in the hand, practised again and again until they arrive without thought. Many teachers report that children who join their writing are better at spotting when a word looks wrong, because their hands have learned what the right version feels like. The evidence on this is thin, but the idea is plausible.

The wider research on writing by hand is more suggestive than conclusive, and an honest case should say so. Studies of young children learning letters have found that forming letters by hand engages the parts of the brain involved in reading more strongly than typing or tracing does. Other work suggests that students who take notes by hand often process the material more deeply than those who type, although that finding has been challenged and should not be treated as settled. None of this proves that cursive in particular is magic. What it does show is that the physical act of producing a letter is not a trivial step on the way to typing. It is part of how children come to know letters at all. A school that takes handwriting seriously is taking seriously one of the main ways young minds absorb the written language, and joined writing is the natural completion of that work rather than an optional extra.

The strongest objection is about time and relevance. The school day is crowded, the objection says, and adults type far more than they write. Why not use the minutes for keyboard skills, coding or anything else that looks like the future? The answer is that these are not rivals. A daily handwriting session in the middle years of primary school takes perhaps ten or fifteen minutes, and much of that can be folded into lessons that are happening anyway, such as copying a poem, writing up science observations or composing a story. Keyboard skills deserve teaching too, and the best schools do both. Examinations in many places are still written by hand. Adults sign forms, jot notes in meetings, write birthday cards, label boxes, leave messages on the fridge and scribble shopping lists. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand is handicapped in all of these small but constant tasks.

A second objection says that print is just as legible and just as quick, so joining adds nothing. There is some truth in this. A neat print hand can be perfectly good, and plenty of adults who were taught to join have drifted back toward print. But notice what that drift really tells us. Most adults settle on a hybrid, a personal mixture of joined and unjoined letters. The only question is whether the school guides children toward an efficient hybrid or leaves them to invent one on their own. Children left alone to find their own joins often arrive at awkward ones, with strange starting points and backward strokes that slow them down and make the page harder to read. Teaching a sound set of joins gives every child a good starting point from which they can later adapt.

There is a fair worry about children with dyslexia, dyspraxia or other difficulties with movement and coordination. I take it seriously. For some of these children, joined writing is a gift, because the continuous flow reduces the reversals of letters such as b and d and makes the gaps between words easier to judge. For others it is a struggle, and forcing the issue would do harm. The sensible response is not to remove the skill from the curriculum for everyone. It is to teach it as the default, watch how each child fares, and allow adaptations for those who need them. We do not stop teaching reading aloud because a few children find it painful. We support those children and keep teaching the rest.

Then there is the matter of reading. Handwriting is not only something we produce. It is something we receive. Letters from grandparents, annotations in a teacher's margin, old family recipes, wartime diaries, parish records, the signatures on historical documents and the notes left by a parent on a kitchen table are all written in joined hands. A generation that cannot read cursive is cut off from a large part of its own past and from the daily writing of the older people around it. It is true that reading a joined hand can be learned without learning to write it, but in practice the best way to read a script is to have formed it with your own fingers. Teaching the writing teaches the reading at no extra cost, and that alone is a strong reason to keep it.

I would add two quieter points. The first is equality. A pencil and a sheet of paper are the cheapest and most reliable writing tools there are. Not every household has a good device, a quiet corner or a dependable connection, and a child who writes comfortably by hand has a skill that works everywhere, for free, with no battery. The second is pride and patience. There is real satisfaction for a seven year old in producing a page that looks like grown up writing, and real value in learning that careful repeated practice makes something look better over time. That lesson is useful far beyond the page.

None of this is an argument for the bad old ways. Handwriting teaching has sometimes been joyless, obsessed with neatness and punishing for children whose hands do not cooperate. A better approach is available. Children should first learn clear print letters, ideally with small exit strokes that prepare the ground for joining. Around the age of six or seven they should begin to link letters, starting with the easiest joins and building up gradually. Practice should be short and frequent rather than long and dull, tied to real writing the children care about, and marked for legibility and fluency rather than for beauty. A child whose writing is clear and comfortable has succeeded, even if it will never be framed.

The case against teaching joined writing rests mostly on the assumption that anything old must be obsolete. But schools exist to give children durable capacities, not to chase the latest tools, and handwriting is such a capacity. It speeds up thinking on the page, it strengthens the link between the hand and the language in the head, it opens the door to a vast archive of written material, and it costs only a few minutes a day. The evidence does not prove that it transforms learning, and I have tried not to claim that it does. But the evidence points the same way again and again, and the downside of keeping it is close to nothing. When the cost is small, the benefits are real and the alternative is to leave children to work it out alone, the decision is easy. Primary schools should go on teaching joined up handwriting, and they should do it well.
