On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusual thaw. After days of bitter cold, the temperature had climbed above forty degrees, and workers, children and horses crowded the waterfront streets. Beside the harbor stood a huge steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company, fifty feet high and ninety feet across. It held more than two million gallons of molasses, shipped up from the Caribbean to be turned into industrial alcohol for munitions.

The tank had never been trusted. It leaked so badly that neighborhood children collected the syrup in tin cans. Rather than fix the seams, the company painted the tank brown to hide the stains. No one had properly tested it, and the engineer who approved it was not an engineer at all.

Shortly after noon, residents heard a low rumble and a sound like machine gun fire as rivets shot out of the steel. Then the tank split open. A wall of molasses roughly twenty five feet high surged into the streets at around thirty five miles an hour. It carried a force that no one had ever imagined from something so sweet.

The wave crushed everything in its path. It tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports and left a train teetering on the edge. It shoved a nearby firehouse off its foundation, trapping the firemen inside as the building collapsed. Houses were smashed to splinters, wagons were swept away, and the air filled with the cries of people stuck fast in the sticky flood. As the syrup cooled in the winter air, it thickened, turning rescue into a slow, desperate struggle. Soldiers, sailors and medical students from nearby ships and schools waded in to dig out survivors.

Twenty one people died and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Some victims were found only days later, buried in the dark syrup. Horses too were lost in great numbers and had to be put down.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats and covered the ground with sand and sawdust. Everything seemed to be coated in a brown film, from the pavement to the trolley cars to the clothes of the people who walked the neighborhood. The harbor ran brown until summer, and locals insisted that on hot days the smell of molasses still lingered for decades.

The company blamed anarchists, claiming the tank had been bombed. But a long legal fight followed, one of the first major class action suits in Massachusetts. Auditor Hugh Ogden spent years hearing hundreds of witnesses and examining the engineering evidence. He concluded that the tank had been poorly built and carelessly managed, and in 1925 the company was held responsible. It paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars to victims and families.

The disaster led to stricter rules for construction and engineering review in the United States. Boston remembers it today as a strange tragedy that began as a thaw and ended in a sticky catastrophe.
