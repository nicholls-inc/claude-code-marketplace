Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and they should do it soon. The rules were adopted with good intentions, but they have quietly shaped American cities into places that serve cars first and people second. Removing them does not ban parking or punish drivers. It simply returns a decision to the people who actually know what a building needs: the owners, the lenders, and the tenants who will use it.

Consider how these rules work in practice. A city council, usually relying on a manual of generic ratios, decides that a restaurant must provide so many spaces per thousand square feet, or that an apartment must come with two spaces per unit. Those numbers are often drawn from surveys of suburban sites at peak demand, which means they describe the busiest day of the year at the most car-dependent location in the sample. Applied to every project in a city, they guarantee a surplus of empty asphalt on nearly every ordinary day. Anyone who has walked past a half-empty lot beside a shopping center has seen the result.

The first problem is cost, and it falls hardest on the people cities say they want to help. A single structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost even more. Developers do not absorb that expense out of generosity. They pass it on in higher rents and higher prices, and they pay for it by building fewer homes on the same lot. Researchers who have studied the effect find that parking mandates add meaningfully to the price of housing, and that they do so for tenants who do not own cars as well as for those who do. A household that gets by without a vehicle ends up subsidizing the storage of other people's vehicles every month.

The second problem is that mandates make good projects impossible. Many small, useful buildings, such as a corner shop with apartments above it or a narrow infill lot near a transit stop, cannot physically fit the required spaces. The project is then abandoned or redesigned into something larger and more expensive, or it never gets proposed at all. Older neighborhoods that we now prize for their walkable charm, with their closely spaced buildings and mixed uses, would be illegal to build today in most places. A rule that forbids the best examples of urban life deserves some suspicion.

The third problem concerns land. Every space required by law, along with the driveways and turning lanes that come with it, takes up ground that could hold homes, shops, or parks. In a downtown, land is the scarcest and most valuable resource a city has, and surface lots are a poor use of it. They also weaken the tax base, since a parking lot generates far less revenue per acre than almost any building would. Cities that carpet their centers in required parking end up spread out, expensive to service, and strangely empty.

The strongest objection is that without mandates, people will crowd the streets looking for somewhere to park. This worry is understandable, but the evidence from places that have already acted is reassuring. Buffalo eliminated its requirements citywide in 2017, and Minneapolis and Anchorage have followed, along with a growing list of others. Developers there still build parking when the market demands it, because most tenants and customers do expect it. What changed is that they now build the amount they believe they can fill, rather than the amount a formula dictates. Projects with fewer spaces tend to be the ones near transit, where fewer residents drive, which is exactly where the old rules did the most needless damage.

Where street parking really does become scarce, the answer is to manage it, not to force every building to hide the problem. Cities can charge fair prices for curb space, which keeps a few spots open on every block and encourages turnover. They can issue neighborhood permits, share the revenue with nearby businesses, and let existing garages rent out unused capacity. These tools treat parking as a resource with a price instead of a free good that must be supplied without limit, and they work far better than a blunt mandate that bakes the same oversupply into every new building for decades.

It is also worth noticing what removal does not require. Nobody is forced to give up a car, and no one is banned from building a garage. A grocery chain that believes its shoppers need a large lot can still build one. A lender that wants spaces for an apartment complex can still insist on them. The reform simply ends the practice of substituting a city official's guess for the judgment of the people with their own money at stake. That is a modest change, and it would strike most people as common sense if it were not so deeply embedded in the zoning code.

Cities face real pressure on housing costs, climate goals, and municipal budgets, and parking minimums make all three harder to manage. Repealing them costs the public treasury nothing, and it unlocks private investment that is currently blocked. The sensible course is to stop requiring parking and let cities build what their residents actually need. If a space is truly worth having, someone will be willing to pay for it, and that is the best test we have.
