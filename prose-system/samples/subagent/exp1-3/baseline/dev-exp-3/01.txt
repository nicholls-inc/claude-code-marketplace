**How two strangers can agree on a secret in public**

**The problem**

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret number, which they can later use as a password to scramble their messages. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Eve, a curious stranger, hears every word they say.

This looks impossible. If a secret is spoken aloud, it is not secret. For centuries the only solution was to meet first, in person, and agree on a code. Then, in the 1970s, researchers showed that meeting is not needed. The trick uses a kind of maths problem that is easy to do in one direction and extremely hard to undo.

**An easy picture first: mixing paint**

Imagine that Alice and Bob each have a tin of paint, and they publicly agree on a shared starting colour, say yellow. Everyone, including Eve, can see this.

Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red. She mixes it with the yellow and sends the mixture to Bob. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, say blue, mixes it with the yellow and sends that to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures travel past.

Now Alice takes Bob's mixture (yellow and blue) and adds her private red. Bob takes Alice's mixture (yellow and red) and adds his private blue. Both end up with the same final colour: yellow, red and blue together. Eve has only the two half-mixed tins. She cannot take the colours back out of them, because unmixing paint is nearly impossible, while mixing it is easy.

Mixing is easy and unmixing is hard. The mathematics below does exactly this, using numbers instead of paint.

**Clock arithmetic**

You have met clock arithmetic at school, even if you did not call it that. On a 12-hour clock, 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock, because you wrap around after 12. We write this as 15 mod 12 = 3. The word "mod" means "the remainder after dividing". In the same way, 100 mod 23 is 8, because 23 goes into 100 four times (92) and leaves 8.

Our clock will have 23 hours instead of 12. Any number we calculate is wrapped back onto this clock by keeping only the remainder after dividing by 23.

Now think about powers. 5 to the power 3 means 5 × 5 × 5 = 125, and on our 23-hour clock that is 10. You can check it: 23 × 5 = 115, and 125 − 115 = 10.

Here is the key fact. Going forward is easy. Give me 5, a power such as 6, and a clock size of 23, and I can find the answer in a few seconds. But going backward is hard. Suppose I tell you that 5 to some power lands on 8 on the 23-hour clock, and I ask you which power it was. You would have to try the powers one by one. With a clock of 23 hours, that is quick enough. But real systems use a clock with hundreds of digits in its size. Then the number of possible answers is far beyond what all the computers on Earth could check in the lifetime of the universe. The powers also jump around the clock in a seemingly random way, so there is no shortcut that tells you "you are getting warmer".

**The exchange, step by step**

Here is the whole procedure, with small numbers so you can follow it.

1. In public, Alice and Bob agree on two numbers: a clock size of 23 and a base number 5. Eve hears both. This is like agreeing on yellow paint.

2. Alice secretly chooses a private number, 6. She tells nobody. Bob secretly chooses 15.

3. Alice calculates 5 to the power 6 on the clock. That is 15,625, which leaves remainder 8 after dividing by 23. She sends **8** to Bob. This is her mixed paint.

4. Bob calculates 5 to the power 15 on the clock. The answer is 19. He sends **19** to Alice.

5. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her private power, 6. On the 23-hour clock, 19 to the power 6 comes to **2**.

6. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his private power, 15. On the 23-hour clock, 8 to the power 15 also comes to **2**.

They both arrive at 2. That is their shared secret, and neither of them ever said it aloud.

**Why do they get the same answer?**

This is the one place where a little school algebra helps. When you raise a power to another power, you multiply the exponents. For example, (5 to the 3) to the 2 is the same as 5 to the 6, because 3 × 2 = 6.

Alice computed (5 to the 15) to the 6, which is 5 to the power 15 × 6, or 5 to the 90. Bob computed (5 to the 6) to the 15, which is 5 to the power 6 × 15, which is also 5 to the 90. Multiplication does not care about order, so 6 × 15 equals 15 × 6. Both end up at the same place on the clock. The clock wrapping does not disturb this, because wrapping can be done at any stage and the final remainder is the same.

**What does Eve see, and why is she stuck?**

Eve heard four numbers: the clock size 23, the base 5, Alice's 8 and Bob's 19. To get the secret, she would need either Alice's private 6 or Bob's private 15. To find Alice's number she must answer: "5 to which power gives 8 on this clock?" That is the hard backward problem. With our tiny clock she can just try powers until she finds 6, and then finish the calculation herself. That is why this example is only a demonstration.

With real clock sizes, a few hundred digits long, trying every power is hopeless. Alice and Bob can still do their own calculations quickly, because there is a fast method of computing huge powers by repeatedly squaring. The gap between "easy to do" and "impossible to undo" is what protects them.

**Why it is called public-key cryptography**

The numbers Alice and Bob send, 8 and 19, are called their public keys. They can be shown to the whole world. The private numbers, 6 and 15, are their private keys, and they never leave the owner's head or device. Each person's public and private numbers are linked by the hard-to-reverse calculation, so publishing one does not give away the other.

Other systems use the same idea differently. In one famous method, called RSA, anybody can use your public number to lock a message, but only you, holding the private number, can unlock it. The foundation there is a different one-way problem: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but taking the result and finding the two primes is very hard. The two approaches have the same spirit: make a lock that anyone can close and only one person can open.

**What this does for you every day**

When your phone connects to a bank's website, the two of you have never met, and your messages cross many machines owned by strangers. Within a fraction of a second, your device and the bank run a calculation very much like the one above. Both end up with a shared secret number. They then use that number as the password for a fast, ordinary scrambling method that protects everything else you send. The padlock in a web browser is a sign that this has happened.

**An honest caveat**

There is one gap. The method as described protects against someone who only listens. It does not stop a more active attacker who sits in the middle and pretends to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, running two separate exchanges. Real systems add proof of identity, using certificates and digital signatures that are checked against trusted authorities. These are also built on public-key mathematics.

**The main idea**

Alice and Bob each mix a private ingredient into something public. They swap the results. Each then adds their own private ingredient to what they received. Because the order of mixing does not matter, they end with the same result. Because mixing cannot be easily undone, Eve, who saw everything except the private ingredients, is left with nothing useful. The secret was never sent. It was built separately at each end.
