A lithium-ion cell is charged by forcing current through it in the direction opposite to discharge, which pushes lithium ions out of the cathode, usually a layered metal oxide, across a porous separator soaked in liquid electrolyte, and into the graphite anode, where they slot between carbon layers in a process called intercalation. Because the cell voltage rises as the anode fills with lithium and the cathode empties, the charger must apply a terminal voltage above the cell's open-circuit voltage, and the gap between the two is what drives the reaction. Practical chargers use constant-current, constant-voltage control. In the first phase the charger holds a fixed current, often between 0.5C and 1C, where 1C means the current that would fill the nominal capacity in one hour, and the terminal voltage climbs steadily until it reaches a ceiling set by the chemistry, typically 4.2 V for a cobalt-based cell, at which point the charger switches to holding that voltage and lets the current fall on its own, ending the charge when it has decayed to something like 3 to 5 percent of the rated current. The slowing is a direct consequence of holding voltage fixed, because the terminal voltage is the sum of the cell's open-circuit voltage, which rises with state of charge, and the voltage lost across internal resistance, which is the current times the cell's impedance. That impedance is not a single ohmic resistor but a combination of electrolyte and contact resistance, a charge-transfer resistance at the electrode surfaces, and a diffusion term that reflects how slowly lithium migrates inside solid particles. As the open-circuit voltage approaches the ceiling, less of the fixed terminal voltage remains to drive current across that impedance, so the current must drop, and it does so roughly exponentially, much like a capacitor charging through a resistor. Diffusion makes the tail longer than a simple resistor-capacitor model would predict, since lithium arriving at the particle surface must spread inward through the graphite, and near full the surface is already crowded while the interior is still filling, so the concentration gradient that drives inward transport shrinks. The ceiling cannot be raised to speed this up, because higher voltage accelerates electrolyte oxidation at the cathode and, more seriously, a fast charge near full pushes the anode surface potential toward that of metallic lithium, where lithium plates as dendritic metal instead of intercalating, which costs capacity, raises the risk of internal short circuits, and is the main reason chargers taper current rather than simply stopping at a voltage. Cold temperatures worsen the problem by slowing diffusion and reaction kinetics, which is why charge acceptance falls sharply below about 10 degrees Celsius and why battery management systems limit current by temperature as well as by voltage.
