Scrap the Parking Mandate

Every city has a rule that shapes its streets more than any zoning map, and almost nobody votes on it. The minimum parking requirement says that before you build an apartment, a cafe or a clinic, you must also build a set number of parking spaces. Two per home. One for every three hundred square feet of retail. The numbers feel like engineering, but they are guesses, and they have bent our cities out of shape for seventy years. Cities should remove them.

Start with where the numbers come from. Many were borrowed from a handbook that surveyed a handful of suburban sites, often with free parking and no transit, and reported peak demand. Planners treated that peak as a floor for everyone. A rule written for a suburban shopping plaza ended up applied to a downtown lot, a corner store and a studio apartment for a student without a car. Nobody tested whether the figures matched how people actually live.

The cost of that guess is enormous. A single structured parking space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and underground spaces cost far more. That cost does not vanish. It is folded into the rent, the menu price and the price of every good sold nearby. Renters who own no car still pay for spaces they never use. Economists who have studied this find that mandates raise housing costs and lower the number of homes built, because a project that cannot fit the required spaces on its lot simply does not happen.

Consider what this does to small projects in particular. A modest lot in an older neighborhood might hold six apartments, but once you add the required spaces and the driveway to reach them, it holds three. The old duplexes and corner shops that gave our best neighborhoods their charm would be illegal to build today, because their lots could never satisfy a modern parking formula. We are forbidden from repeating the things we most admire.

The mandate also destroys the land it claims to serve. Surface lots turn the middle of a city into a gap between destinations. Buildings spread apart, walking becomes unpleasant, and driving becomes the only sensible choice, which then seems to justify even more parking. It is a loop that feeds itself. Cities that fill their centers with asphalt lose tax revenue too. A downtown acre covered in cars often produces a fraction of the property tax of the same acre covered in shops and homes.

Defenders raise a fair worry. If we remove the requirement, will neighborhoods flood with cars and leave residents circling the block? The answer is that removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it wherever customers want it, because buyers and tenants with cars will pay for a space. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision rather than a legislative guess. A suburban apartment complex may still build two spaces per unit. A building next to a train station may build none and charge lower rent. Both outcomes beat a single number imposed on everyone.

The evidence from places that have tried this is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and developers there have since built projects with fewer spaces than the old rules would have demanded, while the city did not descend into gridlock. Minneapolis, Seattle, Sao Paulo and a growing list of others have dropped or sharply reduced their mandates, and in many cases builders reported that they used the freedom to add homes and storefronts rather than empty stalls. California has barred requirements near frequent transit. These are not utopian experiments. They are ordinary cities that decided to stop guessing.

The remaining concern, spillover onto public streets, is real but solvable, and it is solved by pricing, not by mandates. Curb space is valuable, and giving it away free invites overuse. Cities can charge for street parking at rates that keep a space or two open on every block, and send the revenue back to the neighborhood for sidewalks and lighting. Residential permit programs can protect existing residents. This approach treats parking as the scarce resource it is, instead of pretending it is a free entitlement that developers must fund in advance.

There is also a matter of fairness. A parking mandate is a quiet subsidy for driving, paid for by everyone, including those who walk, bike, ride the bus or cannot afford a car. Households with lower incomes own fewer vehicles, yet they carry a share of the cost through higher rents. Removing the mandate does not take anything from drivers who want a space. It simply stops forcing their costs onto their neighbors.

Climate and housing goals push in the same direction. A city that says it wants affordable homes, lower emissions and vibrant streets while requiring every building to devote prime land to storage for cars is working against itself. No other single reform lowers the cost of building homes while also cutting the amount of pavement, and it costs the public treasury nothing.

Cities should not abolish parking. They should abolish the idea that officials know how much of it every building needs. Let developers, lenders and customers decide, let the street price what is truly scarce, and let the next generation of builders put people where we once put cars. The sooner the mandate goes, the sooner we can start building the places we say we want.
