The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Florida carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of four spaceflights and was about to make his fifth. Fred Haise was making his first journey into space and would pilot the lunar module. The third seat belonged to Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons. Swigert had trained hard as his backup, and the launch went smoothly enough that it seemed the substitution had been a minor detail.

The mission was the third planned landing on the Moon. The crew called their command module Odyssey and their lunar module Aquarius. Lovell and Haise were meant to descend to a region called Fra Mauro, a rugged upland, and walk there for hours. For two days the flight was so routine that the television audience on Earth lost interest. The networks did not even carry the crew's broadcast live on the evening of April 13.

About fifty five hours into the flight, the spacecraft was roughly 200,000 miles from home. Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the cryogenic tanks aboard the service module, the cylindrical section behind the command module that held oxygen, hydrogen and the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity. Stirring kept the supercold contents from separating and gave accurate gauge readings. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later there was a bang, and the spacecraft shuddered.

Lovell was floating in the lower equipment bay when he felt the jolt. Swigert said the words that became famous, though the version that history remembers is a little smoothed. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Houston asked for a repeat, and Lovell answered, "Houston, we've had a problem." A warning light told them that a main electrical bus had lost voltage. Soon the oxygen pressure in one tank read zero, and the second tank was falling. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space.

The cause had been planted long before launch. Tank number two had been dropped a short distance during handling at the factory, which probably damaged an internal fill tube. During a countdown rehearsal, ground crews struggled to empty the tank and used heaters to boil off the oxygen. A thermostat switch had been built for a lower voltage than the ground power supply provided, so it fused shut and the temperature inside climbed far beyond what anyone realized. The heat stripped insulation from the wires. When Swigert stirred the tank, a spark ignited the exposed wiring in a pure oxygen environment. The tank ruptured and the blast tore away an entire side panel of the service module.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers spent the first hour searching for a way to save the command module. There was none. Without oxygen the fuel cells would die, and Odyssey would be left with a few hours of battery power. The only safe harbor was Aquarius, a spidery craft built to support two men for about two days on the lunar surface. It would now have to keep three men alive for four. Lovell, Haise and Swigert powered down Odyssey, climbed into the lunar module and used it as a lifeboat.

Next came the question of how to get home. The spacecraft had been on a path that would have missed the Earth had no engine burn been made, but it could swing around the far side of the Moon and be flung back by lunar gravity. Controllers considered turning around immediately, but that required using the damaged service module engine, which no one trusted. Instead they chose to loop around the Moon. A few hours after passing the far side, the crew fired Aquarius's descent engine to speed up the return and aim for a safe splashdown in the Pacific. At the farthest point of the trip the three men were nearly 250,000 miles from Earth, farther than any human beings have ever been.

The journey that followed was an ordeal of cold, thirst and bad air. The lunar module drew very little power on purpose, since every watt had to last. Cabin temperatures dropped to near freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The crew slept little. Water was rationed to a few ounces per day, so all three grew dehydrated, and Haise developed a painful fever and infection. Lovell later recalled that the cold made it nearly impossible to rest.

The most dangerous problem was the air itself. Carbon dioxide exhaled by the crew is removed by canisters of lithium hydroxide. Aquarius had only enough for two men over two days, and the spare canisters in Odyssey were square while the lunar module's sockets were round. As the carbon dioxide reading climbed toward a level that would cause confusion and then death, engineers on the ground gathered the same items the crew had aboard. They had plastic bags, cardboard, spacesuit hoses and a roll of duct tape. A team built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew, who copied the device step by step. The carbon dioxide level fell within hours.

Meanwhile, Mattingly, the man who had been left behind, worked in a simulator on the ground to find a way to restart a dead command module using almost no power. Odyssey would be the only part of the ship able to survive the heat of reentry, so it had to be brought back to life after days of cold. The procedure he and the engineers wrote was tested again and again and then read to the crew. Lovell and Swigert worked through it, powering the systems in a careful order to avoid exceeding the small reserve of battery power.

Navigation brought its own challenges. The crew had to correct their course by hand, using the Earth itself as a guide. With the main guidance computer shut down to save power, Lovell held the line between day and night on the Earth in his window to align the ship while the engine burned. It worked well enough.

On April 17, the crew released the service module, and for the first time they saw its damage. A whole panel was missing, and equipment hung out of the hole. Photographs of the wreck helped investigators piece together what had happened. Then, with the lunar module still attached, the men waited until the last possible moment to leave Aquarius, which had served them so faithfully. They released it too, and it burned up over the Pacific.

Reentry meant a long radio blackout, normally about three minutes. People in Mission Control waited and could not be sure that the heat shield was intact. The blackout stretched past the expected time, and the silence grew heavy. Then the radio crackled, and Swigert's voice came through. Parachutes bloomed on the television screens. Odyssey settled into the sea near Samoa, a few miles from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, almost six days after launch.

Apollo 13 never landed on the Moon, yet it is often called a successful failure. The crew had come home because ordinary people solved problems under pressure, improvising with the materials at hand. The investigation that followed led to a redesign of the oxygen tanks and wiring, and later missions flew safely. Lovell, who lived to be ninety seven, always said that the men on the ground had saved them. For a general reader, the lesson is an old one. In the worst moment, calm minds and honest teamwork can turn a disaster into a homecoming.
