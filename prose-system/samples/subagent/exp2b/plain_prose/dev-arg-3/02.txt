Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case against it is stronger than its defenders usually admit, and I will take it seriously, but it still fails. It fails because it treats handwriting as a question about nostalgia or about the future of paper, when the real question is what a child's hand should be able to do by the age of eleven. A fluent, joined script is the best answer we have, and no keyboard lesson supplies it.

Start with what the sceptics get right. Nobody needs the looped, ornamental cursive of a Victorian copybook. Much of what was once taught in its name was drilled for its own sake, marked for neatness rather than meaning, and used as a proxy for good character. Children with poor fine motor control were shamed for it. Several education systems have quietly dropped cursive requirements, and Finland made keyboard skills a national priority in place of compulsory joined writing. Adults rarely write anything long by hand, and a great deal of what they do write is a shopping list or a signature. If the argument for cursive were that it is beautiful, or traditional, or that grandparents like it, I would not make it, and the sceptics would be right to wave it away.

The argument is about fluency. Writing by hand is a motor skill that children must automate, and the point of automating it is to free their attention. A child who has to think about how to form each letter has less mind left for what the sentence is supposed to say. Researchers who study writing development have long observed that children whose handwriting is slow or effortful tend to write shorter, simpler texts, because the mechanical labour of transcription crowds out composition. This is not a fringe finding. It is one of the more consistent results in the field, and it is the reason handwriting instruction is worth doing at all. The question then is which handwriting is most likely to become automatic and stay fast.

Joined writing has a plausible claim here, and the reason is almost mechanical. When every letter is printed separately, the pen lifts after each stroke and has to find its next starting point. A word of six letters becomes six small, discrete movements, each one a fresh decision about where to begin. In a joined script the word is one continuous gesture, or two or three at most, and the pen stays on the paper. The rhythm is smoother and the hand travels less. I would not pretend the evidence here is overwhelming, because studies comparing print with cursive speed give mixed results and the differences are often modest. But the direction of most of that work, and the experience of almost anyone who has watched a fast writer, is that a practised joined hand outpaces printing. Most adults who write quickly by hand end up with something semi-joined, whatever they were taught. That convergence is telling. If the fast hand is a joined hand, then teaching children to get there deliberately is kinder than leaving each of them to stumble into a messy compromise at fourteen.

There is a second point about legibility that the print side tends to get wrong. Printed letters are easier for a beginner to read because they resemble the type in books. That is a real advantage in the first year, and it is why children should learn to print first. But the claim that print is more legible in the long run confuses the neat print of a seven-year-old with the speeded-up print of a teenager under exam conditions. When printing is rushed, letters collapse into each other without any rule governing how they do so, and the result is often worse than a rushed joined script, where the joins at least impose a consistent structure. Many teachers will tell you the scrappiest handwriting they see comes from children who were never taught a joined style and are improvising at speed. Teaching the joins is teaching a discipline for how letters should behave when they hurry.

Joined writing also has particular value for some children who struggle. Teachers of pupils with dyslexia often favour it, and the reasoning is sensible even if the research base is thin. Because letters in a joined word flow into one another, the child is less likely to reverse or misorder them, and the continuous movement helps fix the spelling pattern in muscle memory, not only in the eye. I would not oversell this. The evidence is far from conclusive, and some children with motor difficulties find joining harder, not easier. But it means the decision to drop cursive is not neutral. It removes a tool that some teachers rely on for the very children who most need help, and the people proposing the change are rarely the ones who have to manage the consequences in a classroom.

Then there is the matter of what handwriting does for thinking. The famous claim that taking notes by hand beats typing came from a 2014 study that has since been challenged, and replications have been less convincing, so I will not lean on it. A firmer and more modest finding is that young children who practise forming letters by hand show different patterns of brain activity, and often learn to recognise letters better, than children who only type or trace them. That work concerns writing letters at all, not joined writing in particular, so it cannot decide this debate. What it does show is that the physical act of making letters is not interchangeable with pressing keys, and that we should be cautious about discarding a technique whose cognitive effects we have only started to understand. When the evidence is incomplete, the burden of proof belongs with whoever wants to remove a long-standing practice, not with the people who want to keep it.

The strongest objection remains the practical one: the curriculum is crowded, and every hour spent on joins is an hour not spent on reading, number, or typing. I take that seriously, and it is the best reason to teach joined handwriting efficiently rather than at length. The skill does not require the thousands of hours of drill that earlier generations endured. Short, regular, well-structured practice, starting once children have secured their printed letters, is enough for most pupils to reach a comfortable joined hand by the upper years of primary school. Fifteen minutes several times a week is a small price. And typing is not really competing for the same time. Children can and should learn both, because they serve different purposes. A keyboard is the right tool for a long essay or a report with revisions. A pen is the right tool for a quick note, a margin, an exam answer, a form filled in at a counter, or a thought worked out on paper with no battery and no distraction. Pretending the second set of occasions has vanished is wishful. Exams in many countries are still handwritten, and a child who cannot write quickly and legibly under time pressure is being quietly disadvantaged on the day it counts.

There is also something to be said for reading. A child who never learns joined writing cannot read it. That sounds trivial until you consider how much of the written record, from family letters to archive documents to the notes teachers scrawl on homework, is joined. Literacy includes being able to decipher the handwriting of other people, and a generation that cannot read cursive has lost access to a good deal of ordinary human correspondence, not only to historical manuscripts. Teaching children to write the script is the most reliable way of ensuring they can read it.

None of this requires treating cursive as sacred. I would drop the insistence on a single prescribed style, the marks for neatness, the moralising about character, and the elaborate capitals that nobody uses after school. What remains is a simple, economical joined hand, introduced after print, practised briefly and often, with room for children to adapt it into their own. That version is cheap, it serves most children well, it gives teachers a useful tool for those who struggle, and it keeps open the ability to read what others have written. Against it stands mostly the observation that we type more than we used to. That is true, and it is a reason to teach keyboarding as well, not a reason to abandon the pen.

Education should be wary of dropping a foundational skill just because a newer one has arrived. We did not stop teaching children to do arithmetic on paper when calculators appeared, because the point was never only to get the answer. The point was to build a capacity the child carries with them. Joined-up handwriting is the same kind of thing. It is a small, learnable, genuinely useful skill that makes the act of putting thoughts on paper smoother and faster, and primary schools are exactly where it should be taught.
