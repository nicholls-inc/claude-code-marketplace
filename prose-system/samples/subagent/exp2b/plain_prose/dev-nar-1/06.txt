In late August 1854, a terrible illness arrived in Soho, a crowded district of London. Cholera is a disease that causes violent diarrhoea and vomiting, draining the body of water so quickly that a healthy person can be dead within a day or two. Within days of the first cases, hundreds of people living near Broad Street were dying. Most of London's doctors, and most educated people, believed they knew why. The prevailing theory held that disease spread through "miasma," a foul-smelling bad air rising from rotting waste, graveyards and sewers. Nobody had yet seen germs as a cause of illness, and the idea would have struck most physicians as odd.

John Snow, a physician who lived nearby, doubted the miasma theory. He had been thinking about cholera since an earlier epidemic in 1848 and 1849. He noticed that the illness struck the gut first, not the lungs, which seemed strange if people were breathing in poison. He suspected instead that sufferers swallowed something, and that it passed from the sick to the healthy through contaminated water. Snow was also a pioneer in anaesthesia, famous for having given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth, so he was no obscure crank. Still, his water theory was only a theory in 1854, and he knew it needed evidence.

The outbreak gave him his chance. Snow walked the streets around Broad Street and began knocking on doors, asking who had fallen sick, who had died, and where those people had got their drinking water. At that time, Londoners did not have taps in their homes. They fetched water from public pumps, many of them drawing from wells dug into the ground. The Broad Street pump was a popular one, prized for water that tasted fresh and clean.

Snow also obtained the official list of deaths from the registrar's office and began marking each one on a map of the neighbourhood, placing a small bar at the address of every victim. As the bars accumulated, a pattern emerged. The deaths clustered thickly around the Broad Street pump, and they thinned out with distance from it. Maps of disease had been drawn before, but Snow used his to make an argument, and the picture was hard to ignore.

Clusters can be misleading, though, so Snow looked hardest at the cases that did not fit. Close to the pump stood a workhouse housing about five hundred people, yet only a handful of them died. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street told a similar story: none of the workers died, and Snow found that they drank beer and water from the brewery's own supply. Then there was a puzzling case far from Soho. A widow living in Hampstead died of cholera, and so did her niece. The widow had once lived in Broad Street and so liked the taste of the pump's water that she had a bottle of it carried to her every day. The niece had drunk some of it too.

By September 7, Snow was ready to speak. He went to the local board of guardians, the officials who managed the parish, and urged them to act. The evidence did not entirely persuade them, but they were willing to try something. The next day they ordered the pump's handle removed, so that nobody could draw water from it.

It would be satisfying to say that this ended the epidemic, but the real story is messier. The outbreak had already been fading by the time the handle came off, probably because many residents had fled the area and the deadliest wave had run its course. Snow himself admitted this and did not claim the removal had saved the district.

What Snow did next mattered more. With help from a local clergyman, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, who knew the neighbourhood and had begun as a sceptic, Snow tracked down the source of the contamination. Whitehead discovered that a baby had fallen ill with diarrhoea in a house at 40 Broad Street just before the outbreak began, and that the family had washed the infant's soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool near the well. When the well was inspected, its brickwork was found to be cracked, allowing waste to seep in.

Snow's work was not accepted at once. The authorities clung to the miasma theory, and a official inquiry concluded that the evidence was insufficient. Snow died in 1858, still not widely believed. Yet over the following years his reasoning, along with his careful method of counting cases and comparing groups, helped establish the case for clean water and proper sewage. Today he is regarded as a founder of modern epidemiology, the science of how diseases spread through populations.
