How Two Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

Imagine Alice and Bob have never met. The only way they can talk is over a line that anyone can listen to. Alice wants to send Bob a message that only Bob can read, and she wants to do it without first meeting him to exchange a password. For most of history this seemed impossible. If the two of them had to agree on a secret, the agreement itself had to be made in private, and that meant someone had already solved the problem.

In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman showed that the problem can be solved. Their method, now called Diffie-Hellman key exchange, lets Alice and Bob end up with the same secret number while everything they say to each other is overheard. An eavesdropper, usually called Eve, hears every message and still cannot work out the secret. This is the core idea behind public-key cryptography, the family of methods that protects most of the secure traffic on the internet today.

Start with a picture. Alice and Bob agree in public on a starting colour, say yellow. Each privately picks a secret colour, Alice red and Bob blue. Each mixes their secret into the yellow, producing a new paint, and sends the mixture to the other. Alice receives Bob's yellow-blue mixture and adds her red. Bob receives Alice's yellow-red mixture and adds his blue. Both end up with the same brown: yellow plus red plus blue. Eve sees the yellow, the yellow-red and the yellow-blue, but to get the brown she would need to separate Alice's red from the mixture, and paint does not unmix.

The picture captures the shape of the trick, but paint is not a mathematical object we can rely on. The real method needs an operation that is easy to do in one direction and very hard to undo. Arithmetic with remainders gives us exactly that.

Think of a clock. If it is 9 o'clock and you wait 5 hours, it is 2 o'clock. We write this as 9 + 5 = 2 (mod 12). The numbers wrap around once they pass the size of the clock. Mathematicians call this arithmetic modulo a number, and it works for any size of clock, including very large ones.

Now add powers. Pick a prime number p, say 23, and a small base g, say 5. Compute 5 to some power, then keep only the remainder after dividing by 23. For example, 5 to the power 6 is 15,625, and its remainder when divided by 23 is 8. So 5^6 mod 23 = 8. Working this out takes a few multiplications, and the trick of reducing after each step keeps the numbers small.

Here is the exchange with these small numbers. The public values are p = 23 and g = 5. Alice picks a secret number a = 6. She computes A = 5^6 mod 23 = 8 and sends 8 to Bob. Bob picks a secret number b = 15. He computes B = 5^15 mod 23 = 19 and sends 19 to Alice.

Alice now takes Bob's number and raises it to her own secret power: 19^6 mod 23 = 2. Bob takes Alice's number and raises it to his secret power: 8^15 mod 23 = 2. They both get 2. Neither ever revealed their secret exponent, yet the two results match.

The reason is that both sides computed the same thing, 5 raised to the power a times b, but each did it in a different order. Alice's secret a combined with Bob's public value B. Bob's secret b combined with Alice's public value A. Because powers multiply in either order, both arrive at g^(a x b), reduced modulo p.

What about Eve? She sees p = 23, g = 5, A = 8 and B = 19. To get the shared secret she needs either Alice's exponent a or Bob's exponent b. Finding an exponent from a result, the problem called the discrete logarithm, is the step that is easy to do forward and hard to reverse. Eve could simply try every exponent from 1 to 22 and see which one gives 8. With a prime of 23 that takes a moment. Real systems use primes with about 600 digits, and the exponents are similarly enormous. Trying every possibility would take longer than the age of the universe with any computer that exists or is likely to exist. No known shortcut solves the problem at that size, although nobody has proven that none exists.

This is the sense in which the method is "public-key." Each person's public value is computed from a private secret, and the public value can be shared freely. The private part, the exponent, never leaves its owner. Knowing the public values does not reveal the secret, because turning the public value back into the private exponent is the hard step.

Real systems add refinements. Many use elliptic curves, a different mathematical setting in which the same idea works with much smaller numbers, so keys are shorter and faster to handle. The exchanged value is also usually fed through a hash function, which turns it into a key of fixed length and removes any structure a mathematician might exploit. The result is then used with a fast symmetric cipher, such as AES, to encrypt the actual conversation. Key exchange is slow for long messages, so it is used only to agree on a key, and the key does the bulk work.

The method has a real weakness, and it is important to understand. Diffie-Hellman by itself does not tell Alice who she is talking to. Suppose Mallory sits in the middle of the line. She can run one exchange with Alice, pretending to be Bob, and another with Bob, pretending to be Alice. Alice and Bob each end up with a secret, but each secret is shared with Mallory, who can read and alter everything that passes. The exchange protects against passive listening, not against an attacker who can change messages. To stop this, the public values must be signed by a key that the parties already trust. In practice this is done with certificates, which bind a public key to a name, and a chain of trust issued by a certificate authority.

Other public-key methods, such as RSA, work differently. In RSA, anyone can encrypt a message with a published public key, but only the holder of the matching private key can decrypt it. RSA is also used to sign messages and certificates. Both families rest on the same kind of assumption: some mathematical operation is easy in one direction and hard in the other.

To summarise. Alice and Bob each keep a secret number and send a public number derived from it. Each combines their own secret with the other's public number, and both arrive at the same shared value. The eavesdropper sees both public numbers but cannot recover the secret without solving a problem believed to be too hard to finish. Once the shared value is agreed, it becomes the key for fast encryption of the actual conversation. The method does not prove who is on the other end, which is why it is always paired with a way of checking identity.

The idea is therefore not magic. It is the discovery that a lopsided piece of arithmetic, easy to compute and hard to reverse, can let two people build a secret out of parts that anyone can see.
