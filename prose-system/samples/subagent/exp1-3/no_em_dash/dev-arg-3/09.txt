Keep the Joined-Up Hand

Every few years, someone declares cursive dead. The argument is tidy: children type, devices dominate, and the hours spent looping an "e" into an "l" could be spent on something useful. It is a tempting case, and I think it is wrong. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting. They should teach it early, briefly and well, and they should treat it as a core skill rather than a nostalgic ornament.

Start with what the skill is for. Handwriting is not a decorative art. It is the way most people, for most of their lives, will produce quick, private, unaided text: a shopping list, a note in a margin, a form filled in at a counter, a name signed on a card. It is also, for the foreseeable future, how children will sit exams. Even in countries moving assessment on screen, handwritten examinations remain the norm in secondary school and beyond. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand is handicapped in the very setting where the stakes are highest. The question is therefore not whether handwriting should be taught, since almost nobody argues it should vanish entirely, but what kind. Print or joined? My answer is joined, and the reasons are practical.

The first reason is speed. Printing requires the pen to leave the page after every letter. Each lift is a small interruption, and across a page of text those interruptions accumulate. A fluent joined script lets the hand travel in a rhythm, with the pen staying down through whole words. Studies of handwriting speed in secondary pupils consistently find that those who write faster tend to do better on timed written work, in part because they can get their thoughts down before they evaporate. Fast handwriting frees the mind. When the mechanics demand little attention, attention is available for content. When every letter is a labour, the sentence suffers.

The second reason is that joining solves problems that printing creates. Children who print are prone to reversals, such as b and d, because the letters are built from the same stick and circle and differ only in orientation. A joined script gives each letter a distinct entry and exit stroke, which makes the shapes harder to confuse. Joining also makes word boundaries clearer: letters within a word are connected, and the gaps between words are therefore easier to see and to keep consistent. Teachers of children with spelling difficulties often report that the continuous movement helps, because a word becomes a single motor pattern rather than a string of separate decisions. Spelling and handwriting are linked in memory, and the muscle memory of a joined word can support the recall of its letters in order.

The third reason is the strongest and the most contested: what the hand does for the brain. Research comparing handwriting with typing has repeatedly found differences in how well people learn and remember. Brain imaging work from Norway found more widespread connectivity in the brains of students who wrote by hand than in those who typed. Experiments with young children have shown that those who learn letters by writing them recognise those letters better than children who learn by typing or tracing. The usual explanation is that forming a letter is a complex, variable motor act, and that the variety and effort of the movement lay down richer memory traces than pressing a key. I should be honest about the limits here. Most of this evidence compares handwriting with typing, not joined with printed script. It does not prove that cursive in particular carries some magic. But it does establish that handwriting as a practice matters for learning, and joined writing is the most efficient form of that practice. If we accept the case for handwriting at all, we should want it to be as fluent as possible, and fluency is what joining delivers.

Now the objections. The most common is that typing is the future and handwriting time is wasted. This confuses two skills that should not compete. Children need keyboard fluency, and schools should teach it. But the claim that one displaces the other is not supported by how children actually spend their days. Handwriting practice, done properly, takes a few short sessions a week. The time cost is small, and the benefits listed above are large. A school that cannot find fifteen minutes for handwriting in a day is not short of time. It is short of priorities.

A second objection is that cursive is hard to teach and hard to learn, and that many adults abandon it anyway. Both observations are true and both miss the point. Most adults do abandon the textbook model, developing a personal hybrid of print and join. But they develop it from a foundation. A child who has learned a clean joined script can speed it up, simplify it and make it their own. A child who has only ever printed has no such base to adapt, and tends to stay slow. As for difficulty, the evidence from schools that begin joining early is that children cope well. Many systems introduce simple exit strokes from the first year, so that joining is never a sudden leap but a natural extension of letter formation. The difficulty is largely an artefact of leaving it late, when habits are set and the pen grip is already awkward.

A third objection is cultural: cursive is old-fashioned, and learning it is like learning to churn butter. Here I would point out that we do not teach joined writing for its own sake, as heritage. We teach it because it works. Nobody argues that arithmetic should be dropped because calculators exist. We understand that the mental skill underlies the tool. In the same way, a fluent hand underlies the ability to take notes in a lecture, to annotate a text while thinking, to scribble a plan on the back of an envelope. These tasks are not performed less often in a digital age. If anything, as screens multiply, the contrast makes the pen more valuable, not less.

A fourth objection is about equity: children with motor difficulties, or dyslexia, or dysgraphia, may struggle with cursive, so why impose it? This is a serious point and deserves a serious answer. The answer is that schools should teach joined writing as the default and support the children who cannot manage it, rather than abolishing it for everyone to spare the few. Some children will need adapted tools, extra time or a printed alternative, and a good school provides these. But a policy designed around the hardest case, applied universally, lowers the ceiling for the majority. Many children with dyslexia, in fact, report that cursive helps, because of the continuous flow noted above. The right response to variation is flexibility, not the removal of a useful standard.

What would good practice look like? Begin in the first year with correct posture and pencil grip, and teach letter formation with the exit strokes built in, so that joining feels like a natural extension rather than a new topic. Keep practice short and frequent: ten minutes, several times a week, is worth far more than an hour once a fortnight. Move from pencil to pen only when the hand is steady, and never turn the pen into a status symbol. Teach children to write with a purpose, such as a letter to a grandparent or a label for a science diagram, so that handwriting is a tool and not a drill. Resist the pressure for perfect copperplate; legibility and fluency are the goals, and a child who writes quickly and clearly has succeeded. By the end of primary school, most children should have a comfortable, joined hand that they can sustain for a page of writing without fatigue.

There is also something to be said for what a handwritten page does to its writer. A person who writes by hand is slower than a typist, and that slowness is not entirely a flaw. It forces choices about what to keep, what to compress and how to phrase an idea. Note-taking studies suggest that students who write notes by hand process the material more, because they cannot transcribe everything and must decide what matters. The pen imposes a kind of discipline that the keyboard removes. Children who learn this early carry a lasting habit of mind.

None of this requires hostility to technology. The best classrooms will use both, and will be clear about which tool suits which task. Typing suits long drafts, editing and sharing. Handwriting suits quick notes, close thinking and examinations. A child equipped with both is better prepared than one given only a keyboard, and the cost of the second skill is a few minutes a day.

So the case for retaining joined-up handwriting rests on solid ground. It is faster than printing, it reduces letter confusion, it supports spelling, it keeps the benefits of handwriting for memory and learning, and it equips children for the examinations and everyday tasks they will actually meet. The objections, when weighed, argue for good teaching and sensible support, not for abandonment. Primary schools should keep teaching it, and teach it properly.
