**How two strangers can agree on a secret in public**

**The problem**

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met. They want to share a secret number, which they can then use as a key to lock and unlock their private messages. The only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. A spy, Eve, hears every word they say.

This sounds impossible. If Eve hears everything Alice and Bob say, she should know everything they know. For most of history, secret codes needed both people to meet first and share a key in private. Public-key cryptography, invented in the 1970s, removed that need. The trick is a kind of arithmetic that is easy to do one way and extremely hard to undo.

**The paint analogy**

Start with colours. Mixing paint is easy, but unmixing it is practically impossible.

Alice and Bob publicly agree on a common colour, say yellow. Each then picks a private colour that they never reveal. Alice picks red and Bob picks blue.

Alice mixes her red into the yellow and sends the resulting orange mixture to Bob. Bob mixes his blue into the yellow and sends the green mixture to Alice. Eve sees the yellow, the orange and the green. She cannot separate the orange back into yellow and red.

Now Alice adds her private red to the green she received from Bob. Bob adds his private blue to the orange he received from Alice. Both end up with yellow, red and blue mixed together. That is the same colour, because the order of mixing does not matter. Eve never had a private colour to add, so she cannot make it.

Maths gives us a version of mixing that works with numbers.

**One-way arithmetic: clock maths**

You already know a one-way operation: multiplication is easy, but factoring is harder. Public-key cryptography uses something similar, built on clock arithmetic.

On a 12-hour clock, 9 + 5 is 14, but the clock shows 2. We wrap around after 12. Mathematicians call this "mod" arithmetic. We write 14 mod 12 = 2, which means "the remainder when 14 is divided by 12."

We can use any number as the wrap-around point, not just 12. Let us use 23. So 30 mod 23 = 7, and 50 mod 23 = 4 (since 23 × 2 = 46, and 50 − 46 = 4).

Now consider powers. 5³ means 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. And 125 mod 23 is 10, since 23 × 5 = 115 and 125 − 115 = 10.

Here is the key fact. Given 5 and a power, such as 5⁶, working out the result mod 23 is quick. But if you are only told the result, say 8, and asked "what power of 5 gives 8 mod 23?", you have to try powers one at a time. With a small clock like 23, that is easy. With a clock of hundreds of digits, it would take longer than the age of the universe, even for the fastest computers. Going forwards is quick, and going backwards is hopeless. That is our "mixing paint."

**The exchange, step by step**

Here is the full recipe, with small numbers so you can check the sums yourself. Real systems use enormous numbers, but the steps are identical.

*Step 1: agree publicly.* Alice and Bob announce two numbers: the clock size, 23, and a base number, 5. Eve hears these. They are the "yellow."

*Step 2: choose privately.* Alice secretly picks 6. Bob secretly picks 15. These are the "red" and "blue." They are never spoken aloud.

*Step 3: mix and send.* Alice computes 5⁶ mod 23. Now 5⁶ = 15,625, and dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 8. So Alice sends Bob the number 8.

Bob computes 5¹⁵ mod 23. This is a big number, but the remainder works out to 19. So Bob sends Alice the number 19.

Eve hears 8 and 19. These are the "orange" and the "green."

*Step 4: mix again with the private number.* Alice takes the 19 she received from Bob and raises it to her private power, 6. That is 19⁶ mod 23, which comes to 2.

Bob takes the 8 he received from Alice and raises it to his private power, 15. That is 8¹⁵ mod 23, which also comes to 2.

Both of them now hold the number 2. That is their shared secret. Nobody ever sent it across the line.

**Why does it match?**

This is the satisfying part. Alice effectively computed 5 raised to the power (15 × 6). Bob effectively computed 5 raised to the power (6 × 15). Since 6 × 15 and 15 × 6 are the same, they land on the same answer. It is the same reason that mixing red and blue into yellow gives the same colour in either order.

In symbols: Alice's public number is 5 to the power a, and Bob's is 5 to the power b. Alice raises Bob's number to the power a, giving 5 to the power (b × a). Bob raises Alice's number to the power b, giving 5 to the power (a × b). Multiplication does not care about order, so the results agree.

**What does Eve see, and why can't she finish the job?**

Eve knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret she needs either Alice's private 6 or Bob's private 15. To find 6, she must work out "what power of 5 gives 8 mod 23?" With these tiny numbers she could simply try 5¹, 5², 5³ and so on until she hits 8, and she would find the secret in minutes. That is why real systems use clock sizes with hundreds of digits. At that scale, trying powers one by one is out of the question, and no known shortcut exists that works quickly for the right kind of numbers.

So the security does not come from hiding the method. The method is public and has been studied by experts for decades. The security comes from the gap in effort: Alice and Bob each do one modest calculation, while Eve faces an impossible one.

**From a shared secret to private messages**

Once Alice and Bob share the number 2 (in real life, a huge number), they use it as the key for an ordinary fast code, such as one that scrambles each letter according to the key. Both can lock and unlock messages, and Eve, who lacks the key, sees only gibberish. This is how your browser sets up a secure connection to a bank's website within a fraction of a second, even though your computer and the bank's have never met.

**A related idea: public and private keys**

You may hear the phrase "public key" and "private key." These come from a close cousin of the method above. In that version, each person has a pair: a public key they hand out freely, and a private key they keep to themselves. The two are linked by one-way maths, such as the fact that multiplying two huge prime numbers is easy but splitting the result back into those primes is not. Anyone can use your public key to lock a message for you, like dropping a letter into a postbox, but only your private key can open it. The idea is the same: easy one way, hopeless the other, with a secret that lets the right person shortcut the hard direction.

**What to take away**

Two strangers can create a shared secret in public because of three ideas working together. First, some operations are easy to perform but practically impossible to reverse. Second, each person keeps a private number that never travels. Third, the order of multiplication does not matter, so both people reach the same answer by different routes. An eavesdropper hears everything and still cannot reconstruct what only the two of them could build.
