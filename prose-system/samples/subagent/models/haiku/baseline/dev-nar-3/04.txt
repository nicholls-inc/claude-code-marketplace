On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, Boston's North End was unusually warm. After a cold spell, the air had climbed near 40 degrees, and children were out in the crowded streets between the harbor and the elevated railway. Beside Commercial Street stood a steel tank nearly 50 feet tall, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, bound for conversion into industrial alcohol, a product the country needed for explosives during the war.

The tank had been put up in a hurry in 1915 and never properly tested. Its walls were thin for its height, its rivets were poorly set, and it had leaked for months. Workers painted it brown to hide the seepage. Neighbors complained. Nobody acted.

Around 12:30, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a rumble, then a sharp crack like artillery, then steel plates peeling apart. A wave of molasses, estimated at 25 feet high and moving perhaps 35 miles per hour, rolled through the streets. It tore at the supports of the elevated railway, knocked buildings off their foundations, and swept up wagons, horses, and people. A firehouse was crushed. The molasses was too thick to swim in and too heavy to escape quickly. Rescuers arrived to find people trapped up to their chests in the sticky mass.

Twenty-one people died, and about 150 were injured. Horses were killed too, and some were never recovered.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews shoveled molasses into barrels and hosed the streets with seawater, and the harbor stayed stained brown for months. On hot days for decades afterward, residents said they could still smell it.

The disaster also became a legal reckoning. Lawsuits against the company dragged on for years. In 1925, a judge found the company liable, ruling that the tank had been negligently built and maintained. Victims' families received modest settlements, and the case became a lesson in corporate responsibility and engineering oversight.

The Great Molasses Flood is remembered as a warning. A sweet, ordinary product turned deadly when cheap construction met indifference.
