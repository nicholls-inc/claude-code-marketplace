Keep Teaching the Join

Every September, hundreds of thousands of children in England sit down with a pencil and lined paper and are asked to join their letters. Some take to it at once. Others spend a year producing a row of wobbly, disconnected shapes that look like a fence after a storm. A growing number of voices now ask whether the effort is worth it. Children type more than they write by hand, the argument goes, so the time spent on joined-up handwriting would be better spent on keyboarding, coding, or reading. I think that argument is wrong. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it early, deliberately and well.

The most practical objection comes first, because people raise it first. Most adults rarely write by hand. Most of the writing they do is typed, and few will ever write a formal letter to a stranger. All of that is true. But it confuses the purpose of handwriting at this stage of life. A six-year-old is not learning a skill for a future job in the way a clerk once did. She is building a motor and cognitive system that supports reading, spelling and thinking. The useful question is not whether an adult needs cursive. It is what happens to a child who has no reliable, fluent way of producing letters.

The research is more interesting than the debate suggests. Karin James, a psychologist at Indiana University, has used brain imaging to show that young children who learn letters by handwriting them activate a wider network of regions associated with reading than children who only look at letters or press keys. Her results are preliminary and the field is still arguing about how much of the effect comes from the movement itself and how much from the attention and repetition that handwriting demands. The honest summary is that the evidence is mixed in its details but consistent in direction. Abandoning handwriting instruction is a bet against that direction, made without much evidence on the other side.

Spelling is the clearest case. Virginia Berninger and colleagues have argued that writing letters in sequence, rather than selecting them from a keyboard, reinforces the motor and visual memory that underpins spelling. A child who forms the word "because" with a pen is rehearsing its shape in a way that tapping a key does not require. Keyboards are also forgiving in a way that pens are not. Autocorrect fixes the mistake before the child has noticed it, so the mistake is never learned. Teachers who have watched pupils move from handwriting to laptops often report that spelling becomes more guesswork, though rigorous comparisons of this are harder to find than the anecdotes.

Then there is speed, which matters more than adults tend to remember. Once it becomes automatic, joined writing is faster than print script, because the pen rarely leaves the paper and each word becomes a single gesture rather than a string of separate marks. A child who writes slowly does not just produce less. She spends her working memory on the mechanics of forming letters instead of on the ideas she is trying to express. Examinations in England are still handwritten, from primary tests through to GCSE and A level. A pupil who must stop every few words to think about where a letter starts will run out of time for the thinking that the exam is supposed to measure.

Consider the most serious counterargument: that joined handwriting is hard, that many children never master it, and that print script is good enough. There is something to this. Some children, including those with dyspraxia or other motor difficulties, find joining genuinely difficult, and schools should support them with occupational therapy, alternative methods and assistive technology where appropriate. But the existence of struggling pupils is an argument for better teaching, not for removing the goal. Print is taught first in England, and joining is introduced in Year 2 as a consolidation step. Dropping the join altogether would not make writing easier for the child who struggles. It would simply guarantee that the child who finds it easy never learns a faster way to write.

A second objection is that keyboards have replaced pens, so schools should replace handwriting with typing. This treats the two as substitutes, when they are complements. Children need to type fluently, and schools should teach touch typing with the same seriousness they bring to handwriting. But a keyboard is not available on a whiteboard, a birthday card, a label, a form at the doctor's surgery, a note in the margin of a book, or a quick sketch of a diagram in a science lesson. Adults handwrite constantly in ordinary life, even if they type for most of their working day. A school that teaches neither well has made a poor trade.

A third objection is more cultural. Cursive, the argument goes, is a relic, a marker of class and gender and an accident of history. There is a grain of truth here. Handwriting styles have always reflected the teaching of their era, and no one should pretend that a particular script is sacred. But the historical argument cuts the other way too. A student who cannot read joined writing cannot read the letters her great-grandparents wrote, the census returns in a county archive, or the handwritten notes in the family Bible. Literacy has always meant being able to read the forms of writing that exist, not only the ones printed for the present day. Teaching the join keeps that door open.

None of this requires a rigid or punitive approach. The aim should be a clear, legible, fluent hand that a child can produce without thinking about it. That means short, regular practice rather than long, tiring sessions; a clear model with consistent letter forms; grouping letters by how they join, so that children learn patterns rather than twenty-six unrelated shapes; and attention to posture and pencil grip before speed. It also means accepting that joined handwriting is a tool, not a moral test. A pupil who writes a neat, fast, joined hand at eleven should be free to develop a personal style at secondary school, and many will. The point of the primary years is to give them the choice.

The join is a small thing. It is a curve at the foot of a letter, a flick at the end of a word. But small motor habits shape large outcomes. Children who learn to write fluently spend their effort on what they want to say rather than on how to make the marks. They spell more accurately, they think more freely in exams, and they can read the handwriting of the past as well as the present. Those are good reasons to keep teaching the join, and to teach it well.
