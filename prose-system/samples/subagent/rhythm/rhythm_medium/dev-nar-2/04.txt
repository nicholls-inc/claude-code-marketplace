On the evening of April 13, 1970, some 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew of Apollo 13 were finishing a routine television broadcast when their mission turned into a struggle for survival. Commander Jim Lovell, command module pilot Jack Swigert and lunar module pilot Fred Haise had launched from Florida two days earlier, bound for the Fretas Mauro highlands of the Moon. Few Americans were paying close attention. Apollo 11 had landed the first men on the Moon the previous summer, Apollo 12 had followed in November, and a third lunar trip seemed almost ordinary. The broadcast that night went largely unwatched by the networks, which declined to carry it.

About an hour after it ended, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine step that keeps the supercooled oxygen and hydrogen from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later there was a loud bang, the spacecraft shuddered, and warning lights lit across the instrument panel. Swigert reported a problem, and Lovell repeated it in the now famous words, "Houston, we've had a problem." Within minutes it was clear that something had gone badly wrong with the service module, the cylinder behind the cone-shaped command module that held the ship's power, water and oxygen supplies.

The cause lay in a tank that had been damaged years earlier. During ground testing before launch, a heater in oxygen tank number two had been left running far longer than intended, and its wiring insulation was damaged in the process. No one realized it at the time. When Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark in the bare wiring ignited the insulation inside the pure oxygen, and the resulting pressure burst the tank and blew off a side panel of the service module. The explosion also damaged the second oxygen tank, and the crew watched their oxygen pressure fall. Because the fuel cells that produced the ship's electricity depended on that oxygen, power began failing as well.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers in Houston worked through the first hours with a mixture of disbelief and discipline. Within a couple of hours it was evident that the command module, Odyssey, was dying and that the mission to land on the Moon was over. The only lifeboat available was the lunar module, Aquarius, which was built to support two men for about two days on the surface, not three men for four days in deep space. The crew powered down Odyssey to save what little battery power remained, moved into the lunar module, and began a very different mission, which was simply to get home.

Getting home was not straightforward. The spacecraft was on a trajectory that would carry it around the Moon and out into space, and engineers had to decide how to bring it back. Using the lunar module's descent engine, the crew made a burn shortly after passing behind the Moon, which sped up their return and aimed them at the Pacific Ocean. Later they would need to fire the engine again to correct their course. These maneuvers had never been planned for a spacecraft in such a condition, and the guidance had to be done by hand with help from the stars and the Sun, since the computer systems were powered down.

Conditions aboard grew harsh. With the power off, the cabin temperature dropped to just above freezing, and condensation formed on the walls. The crew had little to drink, as water was rationed to protect the cooling systems, and Haise developed a kidney infection with a fever. They slept little and ate cold food. Lovell later recalled the discomfort of the cold, but the more pressing threat was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrub the gas from the air, were designed for two people and were filling up. The command module carried plenty of canisters, but they were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round.

This was solved on the ground. Engineers at Houston gathered the same items the astronauts had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, suit hoses and duct tape, and devised an adapter that would let a square canister work in the round fitting. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the men assembled the contraption in space. The carbon dioxide readings fell back into a safe range, and the adapter became one of the best known improvisations in the history of spaceflight.

There were other problems to solve. Odyssey would have to be brought back to life before reentry, using far less power than any procedure had ever assumed, and no one had a tested checklist for it. A team of engineers and astronauts, led in part by the spacecraft's designers and by the astronaut Ken Mattingly, who had been bumped from the crew just before launch over fears of measles exposure, worked in a simulator to build a startup sequence that would use only a small amount of electricity. They wrote it out and read it up to the crew. A second challenge arose when the astronauts had to realign the ship's guidance after the trajectory corrections, again with limited help from the computer.

As the spacecraft neared Earth on April 17, the crew prepared for the final stages. About four and a half hours before landing they jettisoned the damaged service module, and for the first time they saw the extent of the harm. A whole side panel was gone, and wires and insulation trailed from the opening. Lovell described the sight to Houston, and it confirmed to everyone on the ground how severe the explosion had been. Then the crew released the lunar module, which had kept them alive, and said goodbye to it before it burned up in the atmosphere.

Reentry brought one more anxious stretch. Spacecraft entering the atmosphere experience a communications blackout of about three minutes as the surrounding plasma blocks radio signals. Because of the unusual trajectory, and the cold, the blackout lasted longer than expected. Controllers watched the clock and called repeatedly, receiving only silence. When they finally heard Swigert's voice, the control room erupted. Odyssey descended under its three parachutes and splashed down in the South Pacific near the recovery ship Iwo Jima on April 17, 1970, after almost six days in space.

The investigation that followed, led by a review board chaired by Edgar Cortright, traced the failure to the damaged tank and the thermostats that had been designed for a different voltage. NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks, removing the stirring fans and adding a third tank, and put new safeguards in place before flights resumed. Apollo 14 flew the following year and landed on the Moon. Apollo 13 never reached its destination, but it is widely remembered as a triumph of teamwork, in which the crew, the controllers and the engineers solved problem after problem with little time, and returned all three men safely.
