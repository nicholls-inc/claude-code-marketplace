Software teams should stop estimating how long work will take, at least in the form most of them practise it. I mean the habit of asking engineers for a number of days or points for each item before the work starts, then treating that number as a commitment. I think the habit costs more than it returns, and that teams can plan well without it.

The first problem is that the numbers are poor. In a 1994 study by Roger Buehler and colleagues, psychology students predicted that they would finish their theses in an average of 33.9 days. They took 55.5 days, and about 30 per cent finished within the time they had predicted. Software is not a thesis, but the result has been repeated often enough that the planning fallacy is treated as settled. People who know a task well still tend to picture the case where nothing goes wrong. Engineers working in an old codebase are no exception, because the cause of delay is usually something nobody knew about when the estimate was made.

The second problem is what happens to the number afterwards. An estimate given in a planning meeting becomes a date on a roadmap, and the date becomes a target. Engineers learn that the safe answer is a large one, so they pad. Managers learn this too and trim the figure back, and the two sides end up negotiating a number that neither believes. The hours spent in that negotiation are taken from the work. A team that gives one day of a ten-day sprint to refinement and planning is spending a tenth of its capacity on figures that will often be missed.

The third problem is that an estimate lets people treat a guess as a plan. When a task runs over, the team has a ready explanation in the original figure being wrong, and it stops asking whether the work was worth doing at all. The Sydney Opera House was meant to open in 1963 at a cost of A$7 million and opened in 1973 at A$102 million. An honest statement at the start that nobody knew would have prompted better questions than the figure that was given.

What should teams do instead? They can cut work into pieces small enough that each takes a few days at most, and then count finished pieces. After a few months a team has its own record of how many items it completes per week and how long an item takes from start to finish. That record is a better forecast than anyone's guess, because it includes the interruptions, the reviews and the failed deployments that estimates leave out. A manager who is asked for a date can answer with a range drawn from the record, and say that it is a range.

There are fair objections. Some work has a hard deadline, such as a regulatory change or a contract, and the team needs to know early whether it can be met. My view is that the answer there is to fix the date, cut the scope until the forecast fits, and keep cutting as the work goes on. That is a different activity from the one I am arguing against, and it needs no story points and no planning poker.

Teams that try this tend to find that the meetings they drop were not missed. The conversation about scope, which estimation was crowding out, turns out to be the one they needed all along.
