On a mild January afternoon in 1919, Boston's North End was busy with ordinary life. It was January 15, unseasonably warm after a bitter cold snap. Children played near the waterfront. Workers ate lunch. Horses stood in their traces along Commercial Street, and the elevated railway rumbled overhead.

Looming over it all stood a steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had come by ship from the Caribbean. It would be turned into industrial alcohol, and, with Prohibition only days from becoming law, into the munitions and liquor trade's raw material.

The tank was a troubled thing. It had been built quickly in 1915, and nobody had checked its design with an engineer. Its walls were thin. When it was first filled, it leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping molasses in pails. The company's answer was to paint the tank brown, so the stains would not show.

At around 12:30 p.m., it failed.

Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rapid popping of rivets shearing free. Then the tank tore open. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen feet high at its crest, surged into the streets at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed buildings. It lifted a train off its tracks and bent the steel supports of the elevated railway. It swept up wagons, horses and people alike.

Twenty-one people died. About 150 were injured. Some were drowned in the syrup; others were crushed by debris carried inside it. Eleven-year-old Pasquale Iantosca, who had been gathering firewood, was among the dead. Rescuers waded in to their knees and found the work agonizing. Molasses is thick, and as the afternoon cooled, it thickened further, gripping limbs and trapping survivors. Firefighters and sailors from a nearby ship worked for days to reach the buried. Police, Red Cross nurses and soldiers joined them. The cleanup took weeks. Fire crews hosed the streets with salt water, and sand was spread to soak up the rest. For months, the harbor stayed brown, and locals insisted the neighborhood smelled of sweetness on hot summer days for decades.

What followed mattered more than the disaster itself. The company blamed anarchists and claimed that a bomb had destroyed the tank. Residents sued anyway, and the case became one of the first major class-action suits in Massachusetts. A court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, heard testimony from some 3,000 witnesses over nearly three years. The evidence was damning: the tank had been poorly built, inadequately tested and never properly inspected. In 1925 Ogden ruled against the company, which paid out around $600,000 in settlements.

The consequences reached beyond Boston. The case helped push through stricter building codes and the requirement that licensed engineers and architects sign off on large structures. It also changed public attitudes toward corporate responsibility, making it harder for a company to dismiss its victims as a nuisance.

A sweet disaster, then, with a bitter lesson.
