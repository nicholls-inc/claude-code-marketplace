Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and the strongest reason is that a rule written to prevent a shortage of parking has ended up creating a shortage of almost everything else a city needs, including housing, small businesses, and the tax base that pays for roads and schools. A requirement that every new apartment come with one and a half spaces, or that every restaurant provide one space for every hundred square feet of dining room, was never the product of careful study of what people want; most of these numbers were copied from a handbook of suburban traffic counts taken at sites with free parking, and then hardened into law in one city after another until they looked like a fact of nature.

The cost of that habit is easy to underestimate because it is hidden inside the price of everything else. A surface space takes up roughly three hundred square feet once you count the aisles and the driveways, and a space in a structured garage can cost thirty thousand dollars or more to build, with underground spaces running far higher in cities where the soil is wet or the rock is hard. A developer who must build those spaces does not absorb the expense out of generosity; the cost is passed to tenants and buyers, including the many households that own no car, who end up paying for a space they will never use. In a city where a quarter of renters do not drive, a mandate of one space per unit quietly taxes the poorest of them to subsidize the storage of other people's vehicles. Researchers who have studied the effect find that requirements push rents up by amounts that matter, and that they make some projects so expensive that the buildings simply never get built, which is the one outcome that no housing-starved city can afford.

Defenders of the rules usually raise the spillover argument, that without mandates new buildings will flood the surrounding streets with cars and make life miserable for existing residents. The worry is understandable, yet it treats curb parking as something that must be given away for nothing, and that is the real source of the trouble. Where street parking is free and unlimited, any building with no off-street supply will indeed push its cars outward; where cities charge a sensible price for the curb, issue permits to residents, and adjust the price until a space or two is always open, the spillover problem becomes a management problem with a known solution. San Francisco, Pittsburgh and a number of other cities have demonstrated this in practice, and the cities that have actually repealed their mandates, among them Buffalo, Minneapolis, and Auckland in New Zealand, have not seen the catastrophes that were predicted. What they saw instead was that developers kept building parking where buyers wanted it, in amounts that were often a little lower than the old formulas required and sometimes a good deal higher.

That last point deserves emphasis because it answers the charge that abolition amounts to a war on cars. Removing a minimum does not impose a maximum, and nobody is proposing that a suburban grocery store with a large catchment area and no bus line should be forbidden from building a lot. What changes is who decides. A lender who will not finance a building that tenants cannot reach, and a landlord who knows that his residents have cars, will both insist on parking when it matters, and they will do so with real money at stake and real knowledge of the site, which is more than a zoning official applying a ratio from 1975 can claim. Where parking is truly valuable the market will supply it, and where it is not, a lot can become a shop, a cafe, or four more apartments.

The older commercial streets of most cities show what the mandates destroyed. Walk through a neighborhood built before the rules arrived and you will find narrow lots, corner stores, small apartment buildings with a few units each, all of which would be illegal to build today because no one could fit the required spaces on such a site. A modern requirement that a bakery supply eight spaces means that the bakery cannot open in a converted storefront, so it lands in a strip mall with a sea of asphalt in front, and the district that might have grown dense and walkable stays thin and empty. Cities then complain about their lack of vitality and sales tax revenue while enforcing the very rule that guarantees it, and they pave over land that could be producing property taxes at many times the rate of a parking lot.

There is also a plain question of honesty about what a government is entitled to do. A city may reasonably regulate fire safety, structural soundness, and the effect of a building on its neighbors in ways that can be measured, but dictating how many cars a stranger's tenants will own is a forecast, and the forecast has been wrong often enough that cities with detailed minimums routinely find their garages half empty at the busiest hour of the week. A rule whose chief achievement is to make a city spend its land on vacant concrete should be repealed, and the sooner councils do it, the sooner the housing and the neighborhoods they say they want can start to appear.
