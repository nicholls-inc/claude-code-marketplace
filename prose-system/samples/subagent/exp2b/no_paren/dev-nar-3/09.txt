On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter day. The temperature had climbed to about forty degrees, a sharp rise from the bitter cold of the days before. Children played in the streets, dockworkers ate lunch, and horses stood in their harnesses near the waterfront.

Looming over the neighborhood was a huge steel tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It stood fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean. It would be turned into industrial alcohol, a business that was about to become much more urgent with the coming of Prohibition.

The tank had been built quickly and cheaply. Its walls were thinner than they should have been, and no one had carried out proper stress tests. When it was first filled, it leaked so badly that brown rivulets ran down its sides. Rather than repair it, the company painted the tank brown to hide the stains. Neighbors collected the dripping molasses in cups and pails. Workers had also heard the tank groan and rumble, but their warnings were ignored.

Just after noon, a fresh shipment had been pumped in, and the sudden warmth had thinned the cold, heavy liquid inside. Then came a sound witnesses compared to a machine gun, as rivets shot from the tank like bullets. The steel burst open, and a wave of molasses roughly fifteen feet high surged out at around thirty-five miles an hour.

The wave tore through the neighborhood with terrible force. It knocked a firehouse off its foundation. It bent the girders of the elevated railway, lifted a train car, and flattened wagons, buildings, and everything in its path. Twenty-one people were killed and about a hundred and fifty were injured. Some were crushed, and others were trapped and drowned in the thick syrup. Among the dead were children and a number of horses.

Rescuers rushed in, but the molasses made every step a struggle. It was cold enough to thicken as it settled, clinging to rescuers and victims alike. Soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship joined the effort, wading through the sticky mess. It took days to find all the bodies. For months afterward, the harbor ran brown, and the smell of molasses lingered in the area for decades.

The disaster led to one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company at first claimed that anarchists had blown up the tank, but a court-appointed auditor heard testimony from hundreds of witnesses over several years and concluded that the tank was poorly built and unsafe. The company paid out roughly a half million dollars in settlements.

The flood had lasting consequences far beyond Boston. It pushed the state toward stricter building codes and led to the requirement that engineers sign off on construction plans. Today a small plaque marks the site, a quiet reminder of the strange and tragic afternoon when a sea of molasses swept through the city.
