A leap second is a one-second pause added to the world's official clock, usually at the end of June or December. When one is inserted, the clock reads 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Most people never notice it, but it exists because two ways of measuring a day have drifted apart.

Since 1967, the second has been defined by atomic clocks, which tick with extraordinary regularity. Earth is a far less reliable timekeeper. Its rotation slows slightly because of tidal friction from the Moon, and it also speeds up and slows for reasons involving oceans, weather and the planet's molten core. On average, days have been getting longer, though irregularly.

Over time, atomic time and solar time drift apart. To stop midday from slowly sliding toward dawn, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service, which monitors the planet's spin, announces when a second must be added so that Coordinated Universal Time stays within 0.9 seconds of the Earth's position. The first leap second was added in 1972. There have been 27 in total, the most recent at the end of 2016.

Leap seconds have caused real trouble. Many computer systems assume every minute has 60 seconds and every day has 86,400. When an extra second arrives, software can freeze, crash or misbehave. The leap second of 2012 disrupted several large websites, and airlines, telecoms and financial networks must plan around each one. Insertion dates are announced only months ahead, which leaves engineers little time to prepare.

In November 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the intergovernmental body that oversees the International System of Units, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. It did not abandon the link to Earth's rotation altogether. Instead, it will let the gap between atomic and solar time grow, which could eventually amount to a minute or more, and a later decision will settle how to handle that.

Supporters say the change will make digital infrastructure more dependable and remove a recurring source of outages. Critics, including some astronomers and navigators, argue that clock time should stay tied to the sky, and that a future in which noon no longer matches the sun would be strange. The debate comes down to which problem is worse: the occasional headache of a stray second, or a slow, permanent drift that future generations will have to live with.
