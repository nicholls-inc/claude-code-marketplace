Imagine you want to receive a secret message from someone you have never met, and the only way to talk is by shouting across a crowded room where everyone can hear every word. It sounds impossible. If the secret has to be agreed on before anyone speaks, then you and your correspondent would need to meet in private first, and the whole point is that you cannot. For most of history, secret codes worked exactly that way: both sides needed the same key, and getting the key to the other person safely was the weak link. Public-key cryptography, worked out in the 1970s, solved this by using a kind of arithmetic in which some jobs are easy to do and almost impossible to undo.

Start with a simple picture using paint. Suppose you and a stranger, whom we will call Ana and Ben, are standing at opposite ends of a hall, and a crowd of onlookers sits in between. Ana and Ben each have a pot of a secret colour that only they know. There is also a large tub of yellow paint that everybody can see and everybody agrees on. Ana mixes her secret colour into a bucket of the yellow and sends the mixture across the hall to Ben. Ben does the same with his own secret colour and sends his mixture to Ana. The onlookers see both mixtures go past, but here is the catch: nobody can take a mixed paint and separate it back into its ingredients. Now Ana takes the bucket she received from Ben and adds her own secret colour to it. Ben takes the bucket he received from Ana and adds his secret colour. Both end up with exactly the same final shade, because both have combined yellow, Ana's secret and Ben's secret, only in a different order. The onlookers have only the yellow and the two half-mixed buckets, and they cannot produce the final shade without one of the secrets.

Paint is only an analogy, but it captures the shape of the idea. We need something in mathematics that behaves like mixing: easy to do one way, and hopeless to reverse. Fortunately, school-level arithmetic has just such a thing.

The first ingredient is the clock. Think of an ordinary clock with twelve hours. If it is ten o'clock and you wait five hours, you do not say it is fifteen o'clock; you say it is three. You have wrapped around the dial. Mathematicians call this clock arithmetic, and it works with any size of dial, not just twelve. If we use a dial with seven positions, numbered zero to six, then five plus four is nine, which wraps around to two. Multiplying works the same way: three times five is fifteen, and on a seven-hour dial that is one full lap of seven, another full lap of seven, and one left over, so the answer is one. All we have done is keep only the remainder after dividing by the size of the dial.

The second ingredient is repeated multiplication, which is what we mean by a power. Two to the power of three means two times two times two, which is eight. Now put the two ideas together. Pick a dial with a prime number of positions, say seventeen, and pick a starting number, say three. Work out three to the power of some exponent, then wrap the answer around the dial. Three to the power of four is eighty-one, and eighty-one divided by seventeen is four laps with thirteen left over, so on this dial the answer is thirteen. Going forward like this is quick, even when the exponent is large, because there are clever shortcuts that let a computer do it in a handful of steps.

The surprising part is going backward. If I tell you that three raised to some secret exponent lands on thirteen on the seventeen-position dial, can you find the exponent? With a dial that small, yes: you just try them all. But the remainders do not rise in any pattern you can use. They jump around the dial in what looks like a random order, so there is no way of guessing the exponent from the answer, nor of homing in on it. You have to try exponents one after another. If the dial has hundreds of digits rather than two, the number of tries needed is so enormous that all the computers on Earth working for the age of the universe would not finish. That is our unmixable paint.

Now we can replay the paint story with numbers. Ana and Ben publicly agree on two numbers: the size of the dial, which must be a suitable prime, and the starting number. Everyone can see these, just like the yellow paint. Ana secretly chooses a private exponent, which only she knows, and Ben secretly chooses his own. Ana raises the starting number to her private exponent, wraps it around the dial, and sends the result to Ben. That result is her public half of the mixture. Ben does the same with his private exponent and sends his result to Ana.

Next comes the step that makes the two agree. Ana takes the number she received from Ben and raises it to her own private exponent, wrapping around the dial again. Ben takes the number he received from Ana and raises it to his private exponent. This works because of how powers behave. Ben's number is the starting number raised to his exponent, and when Ana raises that to her exponent, the result is the starting number raised to the product of both exponents. Ben's calculation ends up as the starting number raised to the same two exponents multiplied together, only in the other order, and multiplication does not care about order. So they both arrive at the identical number, even though neither of them ever told the other their private exponent, and even though that final number never travelled across the room.

Meanwhile, what does the eavesdropper know? She heard the dial size, the starting number, Ana's public result and Ben's public result. To get the shared secret she would need one of the private exponents. Finding one means running the hard backward step described above: from the public result, work out which exponent produced it. That is the task we said was effectively impossible once the numbers are large. She can watch every message go past and still be no closer to the answer, in the same way that watching the buckets of paint cross the hall tells you nothing about the colours inside.

Once Ana and Ben share this number, they can use it as the key for an ordinary scrambling method that both sides know, because from then on they genuinely have what the old systems lacked: a secret held by two people and nobody else. They can now talk freely across the same crowded room.

It helps to be honest about what is and is not proven. Nobody has shown that the backward step is truly impossible; we only know that, despite generations of clever people trying, nobody has found a fast way to do it. The security rests on that stubborn difficulty. Real systems also need a way to be sure that the person at the far end really is Ben and not an impostor sitting in the middle, passing messages along, which is handled by a separate use of the same mathematical ideas to sign messages. But the central trick stands: some arithmetic is a one-way street, and by building a conversation on that street, two strangers can create a secret in public.

Every time your phone connects to a website over a padlocked link, a version of this exchange takes place in a fraction of a second, with numbers far larger than any in this explanation, and with no one in the room needing to have met beforehand.
