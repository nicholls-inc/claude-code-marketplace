Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met and can only talk by shouting across a crowded room, where everyone can hear every word, yet who want by the end to share a secret that nobody else in the room knows. That sounds impossible, since anything said aloud is known to everyone, but the way round it, found in the 1970s, uses a kind of arithmetic that is easy to do forwards and practically impossible to undo.

The easiest way in is with paint. Suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud on a starting colour, say yellow, which everyone hears and which is therefore not secret. Alice then privately picks a colour of her own, red, and Bob privately picks blue, and each mixes their private colour into a pot of the shared yellow, so that Alice holds orange and Bob holds green. They swap pots across the room, so everyone sees the orange and the green. Now Alice adds her private red to Bob's green pot, and Bob adds his private blue to Alice's orange pot, and both pots end up containing yellow, red and blue in the same proportions, the same muddy brown. That brown is their shared secret. An eavesdropper saw the yellow, the orange and the green, but has no practical way to unmix paint to discover the red or blue. Mixing is easy and unmixing is hopeless, and that one-way quality is the whole idea.

To turn this into numbers, we need an arithmetic operation that behaves like mixing paint, and the one we use is clock arithmetic. On a clock face with twelve hours, if it is ten o'clock and five hours pass, it is three o'clock, not fifteen, because you went past twelve and wrapped around to the start. Mathematicians call this working modulo twelve, and it works for any size of clock. If we use a clock with 23 positions, numbered 0 to 22, then any number is replaced by its remainder after dividing by 23, so 25 becomes 2, 46 becomes 0, and 100 becomes 8, because 23 goes into 100 four times (92) with 8 left over. You already know how to do this from long division at school.

The second ingredient is repeated multiplication, which is what a power means: five to the power of three is 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. We can combine powers with clock arithmetic, so to find 5 to the power of 3 on the 23-position clock, we take 125 and find its remainder after dividing by 23. Since 23 times 5 is 115, the remainder is 10, and so the answer is 10.

Here is the important asymmetry. Given a base such as 5, a power such as 6, and the clock size 23, it is quick to find the answer, which is 8, but suppose someone shows you only the answer 8, the base 5, and the clock size 23, and asks what power was used. On a small clock you could just try 1, 2, 3 and so on until you hit it, but on a clock with hundreds of digits the wrapping around scrambles the results so thoroughly that there is no pattern to follow. The answers jump around apparently at random, and you would have to try an astronomical number of powers, since no one has found a fast shortcut. Going forwards is a few seconds of work for a computer, while going backwards would take longer than the age of the universe.

Now we can follow the whole exchange with the same small numbers. Alice and Bob openly agree on a clock with 23 positions and a base of 5, which are public, like the yellow paint, and then Alice secretly picks the number 6 and Bob secretly picks the number 15, which are their private colours.

Alice computes 5 to the power of 6 on the clock, working it in steps: 5 times 5 is 25, which wraps to 2, and then 5 to the power of 6 is 2 times 2 times 2, which is 8. She shouts 8 across the room, and this is her orange pot. Bob computes 5 to the power of 15 on the clock, where the big number is 30517578125 and its remainder after dividing by 23 is 19, and he shouts 19, his green pot.

Next, each takes the other's public number and applies their own secret power to it. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to the power of 6, which is 19 times itself six times, and after wrapping around the 23-position clock the result is 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to the power of 15, and after wrapping, his result is also 2, so they have both arrived at 2, the shared secret, without ever saying it aloud.

The reason they agree is a plain rule about powers that you may remember from school: raising a power to another power just multiplies the exponents. Alice's answer is 5 to the power of 15, raised to the power of 6, which is 5 to the power of 15 times 6, or 90, and Bob's answer is 5 to the power of 6, raised to the power of 15, which is 5 to the power of 6 times 15, also 90. Since 6 times 15 equals 15 times 6, both end up at exactly the same place, and wrapping around the clock does not disturb this, because the remainder can be taken at any stage of the working without changing the final answer. That is why each of them can do their half of the work privately and still meet at the same number.

Consider what the eavesdropper has. She heard the clock size 23, the base 5, and the two numbers 8 and 19, and to find the secret she would need either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. Recovering either means undoing the clock arithmetic, finding which power of 5 gives 8, and that is exactly the hard backwards problem. With a clock this small she can simply try every possibility, which is why the example is only a demonstration, whereas real systems use clock sizes with hundreds of digits, or a related trick built on curves rather than clocks, and then trying every possibility is out of the question. Meanwhile Alice and Bob's own work remains quick, because computers can compute huge powers on a clock efficiently by repeated squaring, without ever writing out the enormous number in full.

The name public-key cryptography comes from the idea that each person holds a pair of numbers: in the exchange above, Alice's secret 6 is her private key and her shouted 8 is her public key. Anyone may know the public key but only the owner knows the private one, and the difficulty of going from one to the other is what keeps the system safe. Other schemes, such as RSA, use a different hard problem, namely that multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy but splitting the product back into its primes is not, yet the principle is the same, a door that is easy to close and almost impossible to open without the right key.

When your browser shows a padlock, a version of this exchange has just happened: your computer and the website agreed on a shared secret in the open, then used it to scramble everything that follows. Nobody had to meet in advance or hand over a codebook, because all they needed was arithmetic that runs easily in one direction, and a world full of listeners who cannot run it backwards.
