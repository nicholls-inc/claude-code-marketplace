How Strangers Agree on a Secret in Public

The problem

Imagine two people, Alice and Bob, who have never met. They want to share a secret number that they can later use as a password to scramble their messages. The trouble is that the only way they can talk is a line that anyone can listen to. Every word they say, an eavesdropper called Eve hears too.

For most of history this looked impossible. A secret shared between two people had to be agreed in advance, perhaps in a private meeting or by passing a codebook from hand to hand. If you could not meet in secret first, you could not talk in secret later. Then, in the 1970s, mathematicians showed that two strangers can build a shared secret entirely in public, and that Eve, even though she hears everything, is left with a puzzle she cannot solve in any sensible amount of time. This idea is called public key cryptography.

The paint analogy

Start with colours, because the central trick is easy to see there. Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow. Everyone hears it, including Eve.

Alice secretly picks a private colour, say red, and mixes it with the yellow. She sends the mixture to Bob. Bob secretly picks his own private colour, say blue, mixes it with the yellow, and sends that mixture to Alice. Eve sees both mixtures travelling along the line.

Now Alice takes the mixture she received from Bob and adds her own private red. Bob takes the mixture he received from Alice and adds his own private blue. Both of them end up with yellow plus red plus blue. The order of mixing does not matter, so they hold exactly the same colour.

Eve has the yellow and the two mixtures, but she does not have red or blue on their own. Un mixing paint is very hard. That is the whole idea. Mixing is easy, and undoing the mix is brutally difficult. Mathematics has operations that behave in just this way, and we call them one way operations.

A one way operation in numbers

The mathematical version uses something you met at school, which is remainders after division. When you divide 17 by 5 you get 3 with 2 left over. We will say that 17 leaves a remainder of 2 when divided by 5. Clocks work like this too. Four hours after 10 o'clock is 2 o'clock, because the twelve hour clock throws away whole laps of 12 and keeps only the remainder.

Now think about powers. Five to the power 3 means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125. Suppose I tell you that I took 5 to some secret power, divided by 23, and the remainder was 8. Can you tell me the power?

With numbers this small you could just try them all. Five to the power 1 gives remainder 5. Five to the power 2 is 25, which leaves remainder 2. Keep going and eventually you find that the power 6 works. But the remainders jump around in a way that looks random. There is no pattern that lets you home in on the answer, so you have to test the possibilities one at a time. With a modulus of 23 that takes a minute. With a number that has six hundred digits, even the fastest computers on Earth would need longer than the age of the universe.

Going forward is easy. Given the power, working out the remainder is quick, even for gigantic numbers, because there are clever shortcuts. Going backward from the remainder to the power is the hard direction. This is our mixing and unmixing.

The exchange with real numbers

Here is the whole thing with small numbers so you can check it yourself.

Step one. Alice and Bob announce two public numbers. The first is 23, which is the number we divide by. The second is 5, which is the base we raise to powers. Eve hears both. These play the role of the yellow paint.

Step two. Alice secretly chooses the number 6. She never says it aloud. She works out 5 to the power 6, which is 15625. She divides by 23 and keeps the remainder. Since 23 times 679 is 15617, the remainder is 8. She announces 8 publicly. This is her mixed paint.

Step three. Bob secretly chooses 15. He works out 5 to the power 15 and keeps the remainder after dividing by 23. The remainder turns out to be 19. He announces 19 publicly. This is his mixed paint.

Step four. Alice takes Bob's public number 19 and raises it to her secret power 6. The result is 47045881. Dividing by 23 leaves a remainder of 2.

Step five. Bob takes Alice's public number 8 and raises it to his secret power 15. After dividing by 23 the remainder is also 2.

Both of them now hold the number 2. Nobody ever sent it down the line. Here is why it works. Alice's final number is 5 to the power 15, raised to the power 6. Bob's final number is 5 to the power 6, raised to the power 15. Both equal 5 to the power 90, because 6 times 15 is the same as 15 times 6. Multiplication does not care about order, and that tiny fact is the engine inside the whole scheme.

What Eve sees and why she is stuck

Eve heard 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the shared secret she needs either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15. To get them she must work backward from a remainder to a power, which is the hard direction. With our tiny numbers she can do it by trial in moments, which is why this example is only a toy. Real systems use numbers hundreds of digits long, and then the search becomes hopeless.

Notice that Eve is not blocked by a lock or a hidden codebook. She knows the full method. She knows every step Alice and Bob took. The protection comes only from the fact that one direction of the calculation is quick and the other is impractically slow. Security built on public rules and a hard maths problem is much more trustworthy than security built on hoping nobody discovers your method.

From a shared secret to private messages

Once Alice and Bob both hold the same number, they use it as the key for an ordinary fast scrambling method. Every message is scrambled with the key before it goes onto the line and unscrambled with the same key at the other end. Eve can still record all of it, but without the key it looks like noise.

This is what quietly happens when you open a secure website. Your device and the website each choose private numbers, swap mixed values in public, and arrive at a shared key within a fraction of a second. You never meet the website in advance, yet you can send it a password with confidence.

The relation to public and private keys

The name public key cryptography comes from the way each person holds two numbers. The private key is the secret power, like Alice's 6, which she never reveals. The public key is the number derived from it, like Alice's 8, which she can print on a billboard. Anyone can combine their own secret with her public number to reach a secret shared with her alone. Other versions of the idea let you lock a message with someone's public key so that only their private key can open it, or let you sign a message so that everyone can check it came from you. All of them rest on the same foundation of easy forward calculation and hard reverse calculation.

A caution about the weak spot

The method protects against listening, but it does not by itself prove who you are talking to. If Eve could cut the line and pretend to be Bob while talking to Alice, and pretend to be Alice while talking to Bob, she could run two separate exchanges and read everything in the middle. Real systems therefore add a way to check identity, using signed certificates that vouch for a public number. The core trick remains the same. Two strangers mix in secret, show only the mixture, and finish with something nobody else can reproduce.

The lesson is that secrets do not have to be passed along. They can be grown at both ends from public pieces and private choices, and the maths of remainders makes sure that only the two people with private choices can finish the job.
