A heat pump does not make heat so much as move it. Even on a cold day, outside air holds a lot of thermal energy. Air at minus 5 degrees Celsius is still about 268 degrees above absolute zero, and a heat pump extracts part of that energy and delivers it indoors. Heat flows from hot to cold by itself. A heat pump uses a little electricity to push it the other way, the same trick your fridge uses to move heat out of the food and into your kitchen.

Here is how the cycle works. A sealed loop contains a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures, well below zero. Outside, a fan blows air across a coil called the evaporator. The refrigerant in that coil is colder than the outside air, so heat flows into it and it boils into a gas. A compressor then squeezes this gas. Compressing a gas raises its temperature sharply, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor hot, often 50 to 70 degrees. That hot gas passes through a second coil, the condenser, which transfers heat to your radiators, underfloor heating or hot water tank. As it gives up heat, the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid. Finally, an expansion valve drops its pressure, which also drops its temperature far below the outside air, and the loop begins again.

The key figure is the coefficient of performance, or COP. A gas boiler turns each unit of gas into slightly less than one unit of heat, with modern condensing models reaching around 90 to 94 percent efficiency. A heat pump delivers more heat than the electricity it uses, because most of the output was collected from outside. A COP of 3 means three units of heat for every one unit of electricity. In mild weather, a good unit may reach 4 or more. In deep cold, the COP falls because the gap between outside air and the target temperature grows, and the compressor works harder. Many modern units still manage a COP around 2 at minus 10 degrees, and models designed for cold climates keep running well below that.

What does this mean for your decision? Several things matter.

Running costs depend on the price ratio between electricity and gas. In many countries electricity costs three to four times as much per unit as gas. A COP of 3 roughly breaks even at that ratio, and a higher average COP saves money. Time-of-use tariffs, solar panels and a lower electricity price for heat pumps all tilt things in your favor. Check your own tariffs before assuming savings.

Your home's insulation matters a great deal. Heat pumps work best at lower flow temperatures, around 35 to 50 degrees, compared with 70 or more for many boilers. A well-insulated house with larger radiators or underfloor heating suits this well. A draughty house with small radiators may need upgrades, and those costs add up. A heat loss survey from an installer is worth having.

Upfront cost is the biggest barrier. Installation commonly costs several times that of a new boiler, though grants and subsidies in many regions reduce the gap. Ask what is available where you live.

Space and noise are practical considerations. The outdoor unit needs clear airflow and some distance from neighbors' windows, and most homes also need a hot water cylinder.

Finally, think about the long term. Heat pumps keep getting more efficient, the electricity grid is getting cleaner, and gas boilers face growing regulation in some places. If your boiler is near the end of its life and your home is reasonably insulated, a heat pump is a strong candidate. If your boiler is young and your house is leaky, improving insulation first and revisiting the question in a few years may be wiser. Get at least two installer quotes and ask each for the expected seasonal COP in your specific house.
