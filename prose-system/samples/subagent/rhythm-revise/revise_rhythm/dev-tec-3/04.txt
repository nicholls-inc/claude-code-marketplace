A lithium-ion cell is charged with a two-stage protocol called constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV), and to an engineer from another field the cell behaves roughly like a voltage source, whose value depends on its state of charge, in series with an internal resistance. The open-circuit voltage rises from about 3.0 V when empty to about 4.2 V when full, for a typical cobalt-based cell with a graphite anode. Charging means pushing lithium ions out of the cathode, across the electrolyte and separator, and into the graphite, while electrons travel through the external circuit.

In the first stage the charger holds the current constant, commonly between 0.5C and 1C, where 1C is the current that would fill the nominal capacity in one hour. Because the charger must apply a terminal voltage equal to the open-circuit voltage plus the drop across the internal resistance, the terminal voltage climbs steadily as the cell fills. This stage delivers roughly 70 to 80 percent of the capacity and is the fast part of the cycle.

The second stage begins when the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, typically 4.2 V, a hard ceiling because higher voltages accelerate electrolyte decomposition, damage the cathode, and encourage lithium metal plating on the anode, which is both a capacity loss and a safety hazard. The charger therefore switches to regulating voltage and holds it at the limit, so the current is no longer imposed but is whatever the cell accepts. As the cell's open-circuit voltage continues to rise toward the fixed terminal voltage, the difference between them shrinks, and since by Ohm's law the current is that difference divided by the internal resistance, it decays roughly exponentially. Charging is usually terminated when the current falls to about 0.05C to 0.1C.

Several physical effects reinforce this taper beyond the simple resistive picture. The first is that a fixed ceiling on the terminal voltage leaves less and less headroom above the open-circuit voltage. The second is that the voltage drop that appears to be ohmic also contains slower contributions: charge-transfer kinetics at the electrode surfaces and diffusion of lithium within the solid particles. As the graphite fills, the concentration of available vacant sites at the particle surface drops, and lithium must diffuse inward from the surface to make room; that solid-state diffusion is slow, so the surface saturates well before the particle interior does, and the cell reports a high voltage while still holding unfilled capacity. Finally, the open-circuit curve of the graphite anode is nearly flat through the middle of charge and then changes steeply near the end, so small additions of charge produce large voltage changes.

The consequence is that the last 20 percent of capacity can take as long as the first 80. Faster charging protocols try to shorten the taper by adjusting current against temperature and measured voltage, but they remain bounded by the same diffusion and plating limits.
