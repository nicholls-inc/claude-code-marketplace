Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case for dropping it sounds modern and practical, but it rests on a narrow view of what handwriting is for and on a hopeful assumption about what children will do instead. Cursive, or any fluent joined script, remains one of the most efficient tools a young writer can own, and the cost of teaching it is small compared with what it gives back.

The argument against it usually begins with the keyboard. Children will spend their adult lives typing, the reasoning goes, so classroom hours given to looping letters would be better spent on touch typing, coding or anything else that looks more like the future. There is a kernel of truth here, because typing is a necessary skill and schools should teach it properly. But the argument treats the two skills as rivals for the same slot in the timetable, and they are not. Joined-up handwriting takes a modest amount of daily practice over a few years, and it sits comfortably beside keyboard instruction. Nobody proposes to abolish pencils because laptops exist, and so the real question is not whether children should handwrite at all but whether they should be taught to do it well. A child who is left to improvise a script, with no guidance on how to form and connect letters, will write slowly and untidily and will tire quickly. Such a child will also hate the task, and that is the worst outcome of all.

Speed is the first practical reason to teach a joined script. Printed letters require the pen to lift after almost every stroke, and each lift is a small interruption in the flow of thought from head to page. A joined hand reduces those interruptions, because whole words can be written in a single movement. Fluency matters more than it might seem, since a child who must concentrate on forming each letter has less attention left for what the sentence is meant to say. Researchers who study writing have long described this as a bottleneck, in which the mechanical effort of transcription crowds out composition. When handwriting becomes automatic, the mind is freed to think about vocabulary, structure and argument. Children who reach that point write longer and more ambitious pieces, and they are more willing to revise. Those who never reach it tend to write the shortest thing that will satisfy the teacher.

There is also a body of evidence suggesting that the physical act of handwriting does something that typing does not. Studies comparing children who learn letters by hand with those who learn them by pressing keys have found differences in letter recognition and in the brain activity associated with reading. The motion of drawing a letter seems to fix its shape in memory in a way that tapping a key does not, because every letter must be produced from scratch by a controlled movement and no two attempts are identical. Some of this research is still young, and it would be a mistake to claim that it proves more than it does. Yet the direction of the findings is consistent enough to give any cautious educator pause. If there is a reasonable chance that handwriting supports early literacy, then a school should be slow to abandon it on the strength of a hunch that keyboards will do just as well.

Note-taking offers a related lesson. Students who type lecture notes can capture almost every word, and that sounds like an advantage until one considers what happens afterwards. Because they can transcribe without thinking, they often do, and the notes become a record of what was said and not a record of what was understood. Students writing by hand cannot keep up with the speaker, so they are forced to listen, select and compress, which is the beginning of learning. Handwriting at a decent speed is what makes that kind of note-taking possible, and a joined script is the natural way to achieve it. A child who can only print slowly will fall behind and give up, while one who has a fluent hand will have a useful tool for secondary school, university and working life.

The critics are right to point out that many adults no longer write by hand very often. But few adults never do. People fill in forms, sign documents, leave messages, write birthday cards, jot down shopping lists and make notes in meetings where a laptop would be rude. Every one of these moments is easier for someone whose handwriting is quick and legible. Examinations in most countries are still handwritten, and a pupil with a painfully slow or illegible hand is penalised in a way that has nothing to do with knowledge. For children with weaker fine motor control, who are precisely the ones the critics want to spare from the effort, the answer is more careful teaching and not less. A well-taught joined script has a rhythm that can actually help, since the continuous movement is often easier to control than a series of disconnected starts and stops.

Some opponents add that cursive is old-fashioned and tied to a particular national tradition, and that printed letters match the typefaces children see in books. That is a fair observation about letter shapes, but it is a weak argument against joining. Schools are free to choose a simple, modern joined style, and many already do, with a few basic connecting strokes and none of the elaborate capitals that made older copybook scripts so forbidding. The aim is not a calligraphic flourish. It is a legible, quick and personal hand that the writer can sustain for a page without cramp. Nor does a joined script mean that children can no longer read print, since they learn both and move between them without difficulty.

There is a cultural dimension too, though it should not carry the weight of the argument. A society in which no one can read joined handwriting has cut itself off from a large part of its own written record. Letters, diaries, wills, registers and the marginal notes of ordinary people are all written in cursive, and a generation that cannot decipher them depends on transcribers and specialists. Primary school is the cheapest place to prevent that loss, because the skill is far easier to learn at seven than at thirty. It would be odd to describe it as nostalgia to want children to be able to read their grandparents' letters.

The most serious objection concerns opportunity cost, and it deserves a direct answer. Primary teachers have a crowded curriculum and too little time, and every minute spent on handwriting is a minute not spent on reading, mathematics or science. But the amount of time involved is small. Short, frequent sessions of ten minutes or so, built into the ordinary rhythm of the day, are enough for most children to acquire a fluent joined hand by the end of the primary years. And the time is not wasted even in narrow terms, because writing is the means by which children demonstrate and develop understanding in every other subject. An investment that makes all later writing faster and less tiring pays for itself many times over. Dropping it to save a few minutes a day is a false economy, and the cost would fall hardest on children who have few other advantages, since confident parents can always supply the practice that schools stop providing.

Finally, there is the matter of what children gain beyond mere utility. Handwriting is one of the first skills in which a child produces something permanent and visibly their own, and the improvement from clumsy letters to a smooth line of joined script is a real and satisfying achievement. Pride in a neat page is not trivial, and neither is the discipline of taking care over a small task. A school that teaches only keyboards teaches children to produce uniform text instantly, which is useful, but it removes a modest craft from their lives that many of them would have come to enjoy.

None of this requires hostility to technology or a retreat to the copybook. It requires only a sense of proportion. Children should learn to type, and they should learn to do it well. They should also learn a fast, legible, joined hand, because it supports thinking, aids memory, serves them in exams and note-taking, and keeps open the written past. The skill is cheap to teach and hard to acquire later, and the fact that screens are everywhere is no reason to let it lapse. Primary schools should keep teaching it.
