Apollo 13: The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 13, 1970, Apollo 13 was nearly 56 hours into its flight and about 200,000 miles from Earth. The three astronauts aboard had just finished a television broadcast that none of the major American networks bothered to carry. Moon landings, after two successes, had begun to seem routine. Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier spaceflights, was heading for the lunar highlands of Fra Mauro with Fred Haise, who would land with him, and Jack Swigert, who would stay in orbit aboard the command module Odyssey. Swigert had joined the crew only days before launch, replacing Ken Mattingly, who had been exposed to German measles.

After the broadcast, Mission Control in Houston asked Swigert to carry out a housekeeping chore: switching on the fans that stirred the supercold oxygen in the service module's tanks. Inside oxygen tank 2, unknown to anyone, the wiring was damaged. Weeks before launch, a ground procedure had overheated the tank's interior and baked the insulation off the wires. When Swigert flipped the switch, a spark ignited the insulation. Pressure soared, and the tank burst.

The crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights lit up across the instrument panel. "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here," Swigert radioed. Lovell repeated it moments later: "Houston, we've had a problem."

For several minutes, neither the crew nor the controllers understood what had happened. The readings were so bad that some in Houston suspected faulty instruments. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming from the side of the ship. It was oxygen. The explosion had destroyed tank 2 and damaged tank 1, which was now leaking steadily into space.

That oxygen was more than the air the men breathed. It fed the three fuel cells that produced the command module's electricity and water. Two of the fuel cells were already dead, and the third would die when the last oxygen bled away. Odyssey had a small set of batteries, but those were needed for the final plunge through Earth's atmosphere. Within a couple of hours, the ship that was supposed to carry the crew home would be without power.

The landing was off. The only question was how to keep three men alive.

The answer was attached to Odyssey's nose. The lunar module, Aquarius, was a spindly craft built to carry two men to the Moon's surface and support them for about two days. It had its own oxygen, batteries and engine. Under the direction of flight director Gene Kranz and his team, the crew would use it as a lifeboat. Haise and Lovell hurried to power it up while Swigert shut down Odyssey, switching off every system to save its batteries for re-entry. Before the command module went dark, they copied its navigation alignment into the lunar module's computer by hand, with Houston checking Lovell's arithmetic.

Next came the question of the route. Turning around directly would have required the service module's main engine, and no one knew whether the explosion had damaged it. Kranz chose the longer path: continue to the Moon, swing around its far side, and let its gravity sling the spacecraft back toward Earth. But Apollo 13 had earlier left the "free return" path that would bring it home without further action. Five hours after the explosion, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine, an engine designed for landing on the Moon, to put the joined spacecraft back on a homeward course.

On April 14, Apollo 13 passed behind the Moon, out of radio contact, farther from Earth than any humans had ever been. Haise and Swigert took photographs of the surface they would never visit. Lovell, who had orbited the Moon on Apollo 8, had now come this close twice without landing. Two hours after they emerged, the crew fired the descent engine again, this time for more than four minutes. The burn cut about ten hours off the trip and moved the splashdown to the Pacific Ocean, where recovery ships were waiting.

There remained the problem of making a two-man, two-day vehicle support three men for four days. Oxygen was plentiful. Electricity and water were not. The lunar module's equipment was cooled by water, so nearly everything was switched off, including the computer and the heaters, and the crew cut their drinking to about a fifth of normal. The cabin temperature fell close to freezing. Moisture condensed on the walls and instrument panels. The men slept badly and little, and Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever.

A more urgent danger appeared within a day and a half. Each breath the astronauts exhaled added carbon dioxide to the cabin, and the lunar module's filter canisters, meant for two men, were being used up. The command module had plenty of fresh canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers in Houston devised an adapter using only things the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight plan cover, a spacesuit hose and duct tape. They read the instructions up to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the device, and the carbon dioxide level, which had been climbing toward the danger point, fell away.

On the ground, hundreds of engineers and astronauts worked around the clock. Mattingly, who never did catch the measles, spent hours in a simulator helping to work out how to bring the frozen command module back to life on its limited battery power. No such procedure existed. The sequence had to be written and tested in days, then read up to Swigert line by line.

The spacecraft was also drifting slightly off course. With the computer shut down, Lovell and Haise steered a short correcting burn by hand, keeping Earth centered in a window sight while Swigert timed the engine with his watch.

On the morning of April 17, the crew cast off the service module and saw the damage for the first time. An entire panel had been blown off its side, from the engine bell to the base of the command module. Then they climbed back into Odyssey, powered it up successfully despite the damp, and released Aquarius. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," Houston radioed.

One fear remained. The heat shield had sat next to the explosion, and no one could say whether it was cracked. Re-entry normally cuts off radio contact for about three minutes. This time the silence lasted more than four. Then Swigert's voice came through, and television cameras picked up Odyssey descending under three parachutes. It splashed down in the South Pacific, near Samoa, less than four miles from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, 142 hours and 54 minutes after launch.

All three men were alive, tired, dehydrated and cold. Lovell had lost fourteen pounds. NASA classified the mission as a "successful failure": the landing had been lost, but the crew had been saved. A review board traced the explosion to the damaged tank, and later spacecraft were redesigned. None of the three astronauts flew in space again.
