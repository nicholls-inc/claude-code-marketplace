A leap second is a single second added to the world's official clock so that it stays in step with the rotation of the Earth. Since 1972 there have been 27 of them, and the most recent was added at the end of 31 December 2016. On those occasions the clock went from 23:59:59 to 23:59:60 before reaching midnight.

The need for them comes from there being two ways of measuring time. For most of history a day was defined by the turning of the planet, and a second was a fixed fraction of that day. In 1967 the second was redefined using atomic clocks, as 9,192,631,770 oscillations of radiation from a caesium atom. That unit is extremely steady. The Earth is not. Tides drag on its rotation, and the average day has been getting longer by about two milliseconds per century. Earthquakes, melting ice and movements in the planet's core add smaller changes that cannot be predicted far ahead.

Coordinated Universal Time, the basis of civil time almost everywhere, runs on atomic clocks but has been required to stay within 0.9 seconds of the time given by the Earth's rotation. When the gap approaches that limit, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service announces a leap second, usually about six months in advance. The extra second is added on 30 June or 31 December.

The difficulty lies with computers. Most software assumes that every minute has 60 seconds, and a minute with 61 breaks that assumption. Leap seconds have been linked to outages, including one that took down several websites in 2012 and one at the network company Cloudflare after the leap second at the end of 2016. Some large technology firms spread the extra second across a whole day to avoid the problem, but this means their clocks briefly disagree with those of others. Because leap seconds arrive at irregular intervals, they are also hard to test for.

Recent changes in the Earth's spin have added a further complication. Since 2020 the planet has been turning slightly faster, and 29 July 2022 was the shortest day on record, at about 1.59 milliseconds under 24 hours. If that continued, the clock would need a negative leap second, with one second removed. This has never been done, and much software has never been tried against it.

In November 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, meeting at Versailles, voted to stop using leap seconds by 2035. The difference between atomic time and Earth-based time will be allowed to grow beyond 0.9 seconds, and a new limit is to be proposed by 2026. A gap of a full minute might take a century or more to build up. How it would eventually be corrected has not been decided. For most people nothing will change, since the drift is far too small to affect when the sun rises or when a train leaves.
