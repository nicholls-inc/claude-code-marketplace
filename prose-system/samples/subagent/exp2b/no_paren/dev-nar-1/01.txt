In late August 1854, the Soho district of London was a crowded, grimy tangle of streets. Families lived many to a room, and rubbish and human waste collected in cesspools beneath the floors. Then, in the first days of September, people around Broad Street began to die. Within three days, more than one hundred and twenty residents of the neighborhood were dead. Within a week, most of the families on some streets had fled, and the death toll kept climbing. The illness was cholera, a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhea, drains the body of fluid, and can kill a healthy adult in a day or two.

Nobody knew what caused it. The accepted explanation was miasma, the idea that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting matter. London stank, so the theory seemed sensible. Germs had not yet been shown to cause illness, and the microscope had revealed nothing that doctors trusted as an answer.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked a short walk from Broad Street, did not accept the miasma theory. Snow was a quiet, methodical man who did not drink alcohol and ate carefully. He had made his name as a pioneer of anesthesia and had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. During an earlier cholera epidemic, in 1848 and 1849, he had come to a different conclusion. He suspected that cholera was swallowed rather than breathed in, and that it passed from person to person through contaminated water. The disease seemed to strike the gut first, he reasoned, so the cause must enter through the mouth. He had published this idea in 1849, and almost nobody had been persuaded.

The Broad Street outbreak gave him a chance to test it. Snow set out to learn where the dead had lived and what they had drunk. He walked the streets, knocked on doors, and questioned the survivors and the families of the dead. He obtained the official list of deaths from the registrar and marked each address on a map of the district. Using a simple bar for each death, stacked at the house where it occurred, he drew the epidemic onto paper. The bars crowded together around one spot, the water pump on Broad Street.

Snow was careful not to stop at a pattern. A cluster might have other explanations, and he went looking for the exceptions, which are often more revealing. There was a workhouse nearby, home to about five hundred people, where only a handful had died. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. There was also a brewery on the same street, where not a single worker had fallen ill. The men there were given malt liquor to drink, and the brewery had a well of its own. Both cases suggested the pump was involved.

Then he found the opposite kind of evidence. A woman who lived far from Broad Street, in Hampstead, had died of cholera. Snow learned that she had once lived in Soho and that she liked the taste of the water from the Broad Street pump so much that she had a bottle sent to her every day. Her niece, who drank the same water, also fell ill and died. Others nearby had drunk nothing from it and stayed healthy.

On the evening of September 7, Snow took his findings to the local board of guardians, the officials responsible for the parish. He urged them to act. The next day, the handle of the Broad Street pump was removed, so that no one could draw water from it. The authorities were not fully convinced, but they agreed to try.

It is tempting to say the epidemic ended at once. In truth it had already begun to fade, because many residents had fled the area and the outbreak was probably declining by itself. Snow acknowledged this. The removal of the handle was not a clean experiment, and the numbers alone could not prove his case.

The proof that mattered came from a further step. Snow and a local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, continued the investigation. Whitehead began as a skeptic. But he knew the neighborhood and its people, and he helped trace the earliest cases. They found that the first known victim was a baby girl who lived at 40 Broad Street, right beside the pump. Her mother had washed the child's soiled diapers and thrown the water into a cesspool. Inspection showed that the cesspool lay very close to the well, and that its brickwork was decayed, so that waste could have seeped through into the water supply.

Snow also compared water companies across south London, where two companies served houses on the same streets. One drew its water from a stretch of the Thames polluted with the city's sewage, and the other drew from a cleaner stretch upstream. Households using the dirtier supply died at far higher rates. This large natural comparison strengthened his argument.

The authorities still hesitated. A report by the Board of Health in 1855 rejected his theory. Snow died in 1858, still largely unrecognized on this point. Within a few decades, the work of Robert Koch and others identified the cholera bacterium, and Snow's reasoning was vindicated. He is now remembered as a founder of epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations. His method, which was to count the cases, map them, and look hard for the exceptions, still sits at the heart of public health today.
