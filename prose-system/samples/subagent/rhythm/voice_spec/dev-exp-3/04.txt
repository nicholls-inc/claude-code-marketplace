Two people who have never met sometimes need to share a secret, such as the password for a bank session or the key to a private message. The only link between them is a line that anyone can listen to. This looks impossible, because anything one of them says to the other is heard by the listener too. Yet a method for doing it was published in 1976, and your phone and your web browser use a version of it many times a day. The method rests on a kind of arithmetic that is quick to do in one direction and extremely slow to undo, and the rest of this piece explains how that works using numbers small enough to check by hand.

Before 1976 the only way to share a secret code was to share it in advance. Two parties would meet, or a courier would carry a codebook from one to the other, and only then could they send each other messages that an outsider could not read. That works for an army or an embassy but not for millions of strangers who want to buy something online. The answer came from Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman at Stanford, who built on earlier ideas from Ralph Merkle. It later emerged that Malcolm Williamson at GCHQ in Britain had found the same method in 1974, and that his work stayed classified until 1997.

The idea is easiest to see with paint. Suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common colour, say yellow, and the listener hears this as well. Alice secretly picks a colour of her own, for example red, mixes a large tub of red into yellow and sends the orange result to Bob. Bob secretly picks blue, mixes it into yellow in the same way and sends the green result to Alice. Each then adds their own secret colour to the tub they received. Alice adds red to Bob's green mix and Bob adds blue to Alice's orange mix, and both end up with yellow, red and blue in the same amounts, so both hold the same muddy brown. The listener saw yellow, orange and green go past, but cannot take the red out of the orange or the blue out of the green, because mixed paint cannot be separated again. Real systems replace paint with numbers, and the part that cannot be undone is a calculation instead of a mixture.

The arithmetic needed is the remainder left after division. Dividing 30 by 23 gives 1 with 7 left over, so 30 has the remainder 7. Dividing 125 by 23 gives 5 with 10 left over, so 125 has the remainder 10. The remainder always lies between 0 and 22, which is why this is sometimes described as a clock with 23 hours. A power such as 5 to the 3rd means 5 times 5 times 5, which is 125, so 5 to the 3rd leaves the remainder 10 when divided by 23. The remainders of powers jump about in an irregular way. The remainders of 5, 25, 125 and 625 are 5, 2, 10 and 4, and nothing in that sequence suggests a simple pattern.

Now the exchange itself. Alice and Bob announce two public numbers, 23 and 5, and the listener hears them. Alice secretly chooses 6. She works out 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625, and divides by 23, which leaves 8. She sends 8 to Bob. Bob secretly chooses 15. He works out 5 to the power 15, which is 30,517,578,125, divides by 23 and gets the remainder 19. He sends 19 to Alice. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power 6. That is 47,045,881, which leaves 2 when divided by 23. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power 15. That is 35,184,372,088,832, which also leaves 2. Both now hold the number 2, and neither of them ever sent it.

The reason they agree is that both have calculated 5 to the power 90, since 6 times 15 is 90. Alice computed 5 to the 15th and then raised that to the 6th. Bob computed 5 to the 6th and then raised that to the 15th. Multiplying the exponents gives the same result in either order, and taking remainders along the way does not change the final remainder. The numbers 6 and 15 never left the people who chose them. In the usual terms, 6 and 15 are the private keys, 8 and 19 are the public keys, and the shared 2 is the secret.

The listener knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. To find the secret she would need either 6 or 15. With a clock of 23 hours this is easy, because there are only 22 possible exponents, and she can try 5 to the power 1, then 2, then 3, until the remainder comes out as 8. A computer can try every exponent for a ten-digit clock in a matter of minutes. Real exchanges use a prime number with 600 or more digits, and the list of possible exponents then has more entries than there are atoms in the observable universe. Nobody has published a method for finding the exponent that runs fast enough to be of use against numbers of that size on today's computers. The task is called the discrete logarithm problem. It has not been proved to be hard, so this describes what is known so far and is not a guarantee.

The honest parties are not slowed down by the large numbers. Working out 5 to the power of a 600-digit exponent does not mean multiplying 5 by itself that many times. Instead they square a number, take the remainder, square the result, take the remainder again, and continue, so the number of steps is about the number of binary digits in the exponent, which is roughly 2,000. That is the lopsidedness the method depends on. The forward calculation takes a couple of thousand steps, and the reverse has no known shortcut.

Two limits are worth knowing. The first is that the exchange gives Alice a secret but does not tell her who shares it. A person who controls the line could run one exchange with Alice and another with Bob, and pass messages between them while reading everything. Websites deal with this by presenting a certificate, a document signed by an authority that the browser already trusts, which ties the site's public key to its name. The second is that the shared number is not the whole of the protection. It is fed into an ordinary cipher such as AES, which is fast, and that cipher encrypts the conversation itself. A quantum computer of sufficient size could in theory solve the discrete logarithm problem quickly, using an algorithm Peter Shor published in 1994. None exists at that size yet, and standards bodies have begun adopting replacement methods. A related system, RSA, was published in 1977 and uses a different one-way task: multiplying two large primes together is easy, and splitting the product back into them is hard.
