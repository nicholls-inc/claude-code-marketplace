A leap second is a single extra second added to the world's official time, so that clocks stay in step with the turning of the Earth. There are two ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. Atomic clocks count the vibrations of caesium atoms, and since 1967 the second has been defined as 9,192,631,770 of those vibrations. This count is steady to a very high degree. The Earth is a less reliable timekeeper. Tides drag on its spin and slow it, and movements of the oceans, the atmosphere and the molten core make the length of a day vary by a few thousandths of a second.

Coordinated Universal Time, the basis of civil time around the world, was set up in 1972 to follow the atomic clocks while staying within 0.9 of a second of the time given by the Earth's rotation. When the two drifted close to that limit, the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service told the world to insert a leap second, normally at the end of 30 June or 31 December. That day's last minute then had 61 seconds, and the clock read 23:59:60 before moving on to midnight. Twenty-seven leap seconds have been added, the most recent on 31 December 2016.

The practice has caused trouble for computers. Most software assumes that a minute has 60 seconds and has no way to show 23:59:60. Some systems repeat a second, some freeze, and some skip ahead. In 2012 the extra second was followed by crashes at several well-known websites, and a fault in the Linux kernel left servers running at full load. Large technology firms now spread the extra second over many hours to avoid a jump, but they do not all do it in the same way, which creates disagreements of its own. Systems that depend on exact timing, such as financial trading, telecommunications and satellite navigation, have long asked for the practice to end.

In November 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, which meets in Paris and is the body responsible for the definition of the second, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. After that date the gap between atomic time and Earth time will be allowed to grow well beyond one second. The conference has not settled the new limit, and a gap of about a minute is expected to take many decades to build up. The decision also reflects a change in the Earth. Since about 2016 it has spun slightly faster, and the shortest days on record came in 2020 and 2022. If that continued, a second would have to be removed, which has never been done and which engineers regard as more dangerous than adding one. Whether and when a correction is made, perhaps a larger one at long intervals, is left for later generations to decide.
