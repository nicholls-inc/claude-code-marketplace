On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusual winter thaw. The temperature had climbed to about forty degrees after a brutal cold snap, and the streets near the harbor were full of people. Children played, workers took their lunch, and horses and wagons rattled along Commercial Street under the elevated railway.

Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sticky syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a trade that was growing fast as Prohibition approached.

The tank had never been trustworthy. Built in haste in 1915, it had been checked poorly and never properly tested by filling it with water. Seams wept molasses down the sides, and neighborhood children collected the drips in cups. Rather than fix the leaks, the company painted the tank brown to hide them. A fresh shipment had been pumped in the day before, and the sudden warmth had left the contents expanding and fermenting.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, witnesses heard a low rumble, followed by a sound like a machine gun as the rivets shot out of the steel. Then the tank burst. A wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, swept out at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a wall of air before it and a pressure that knocked people off their feet.

The wave crushed everything in its path. It tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports and pushed a train car to the brink of the structure. It flattened wooden houses and warehouses, and it shoved a firehouse off its foundation, trapping the firemen inside. Buildings collapsed into the flood, and the syrup carried away wagons, horses and the people with them.

Rescue was slow and difficult. As the temperature dropped again, the molasses thickened, and anyone caught in it was held fast as if in wet cement. Police, firefighters, sailors from a nearby ship and Red Cross workers waded into the mess. Rescuers cut through the sludge to reach survivors, and the search for bodies continued for days. In the end, twenty-one people were dead and about 150 were injured. Among the dead were children, and a horse-driver and several city workers. The harbor ran brown for months, and locals said the North End smelled of molasses for decades.

The disaster led to one of the first major class action lawsuits in Massachusetts. The company blamed anarchists and claimed sabotage, but an auditor appointed by the court spent years hearing from hundreds of witnesses and engineers. He found that the tank was structurally unsound, and the company was held liable. It paid about 600,000 dollars in damages, which worked out to a few thousand dollars for each family of the dead.

The case helped push states toward requiring that architects and engineers sign off on building plans, and it showed that public safety could not be left to those who stood to profit from cutting corners.
