A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. The first is astronomical: a day is the time the Earth takes to rotate once, so that the sun returns to the same spot in the sky. The second is atomic: since the late 1960s, timekeepers have counted seconds using the vibrations of caesium atoms, which tick with extraordinary regularity. The Earth, by comparison, is a poor clock. Tides, the shifting of the planet's molten core, ice melting and other forces make its rotation irregular, and over the long run it has tended to slow down slightly.

Because of this, a day measured by the Earth has usually been a sliver longer than a day of 86,400 atomic seconds. Left alone, the two systems would drift apart, and eventually noon on the clock would no longer match noon in the sky. In 1972 the world adopted a compromise. Coordinated Universal Time, the basis for civil time everywhere, would run on atomic seconds but be nudged, when needed, to stay within 0.9 seconds of the Earth-based measure. The nudge was the leap second, usually added at the end of June or December, when clocks show 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Twenty-seven have been added, the most recent at the end of 2016.

The trouble is that leap seconds arrive without a fixed schedule. Astronomers announce them only months in advance, because nobody can predict the Earth's wobbles far ahead. That unpredictability is a headache for modern technology. Computers, telecommunications networks, financial exchanges, satellite navigation systems and power grids all depend on precisely synchronised clocks, and many cannot easily cope with a minute that has 61 seconds. Some systems repeat a second, others freeze, and a number of well-known outages and software bugs have been blamed on leap seconds. Large technology firms have developed their own workarounds, such as smearing the extra second across a day, which has led to different clocks disagreeing with one another.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that oversees the definition of units, voted to stop using leap seconds by 2035. Rather than adding a second every year or two, timekeepers will let the gap between atomic and Earth-based time grow, probably for a century or so, and then deal with it in some larger and less disruptive way. Scientists also noted that the Earth has recently been spinning slightly faster, which complicates the picture. For most people the change will be invisible, but for the machines that run daily life, it removes a rare and troublesome surprise.
