# How two strangers can agree on a secret in public

Suppose Alice and Bob have never met and can only talk over a line that an eavesdropper, Eve, hears in full. They want to end up with a number that both of them know and Eve does not. Once they have it, they can use it as a key: the secret ingredient in a scrambling recipe that turns their messages into nonsense for anyone who lacks it.

This sounds impossible. Everything Alice tells Bob, Eve hears too, so it seems that whatever Bob can work out, Eve can work out as well.

The way round this was published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman. Alice and Bob each choose a number that they never send. Each sends only a disguised version of it. The disguise is easy to put on and practically impossible to take off, and the two disguised numbers can be turned into a shared secret only by someone who holds one of the undisguised ones.

That is the core of public-key cryptography: each person has a private number that never leaves home and a matching public number that anyone may see.

## A calculation that is easy in one direction only

The disguise is built from two pieces of school arithmetic: powers and remainders.

Remainder arithmetic works like a clock. On a 12-hour clock, 5 hours after 10 o'clock is 3 o'clock, because 15 divided by 12 leaves remainder 3. We will use a clock with 23 positions instead: after every calculation, divide by 23 and keep only the remainder. So 25 becomes 2, and 50 becomes 4.

Now work out the powers of 5 on this clock. Each one is the previous answer times 5, with the remainder taken:

| Power | Calculation | Result on the clock |
|---|---|---|
| 5¹ | 5 | 5 |
| 5² | 5 × 5 = 25 | 2 |
| 5³ | 2 × 5 = 10 | 10 |
| 5⁴ | 10 × 5 = 50 | 4 |
| 5⁵ | 4 × 5 = 20 | 20 |
| 5⁶ | 20 × 5 = 100 | 8 |

Going forwards is easy: multiply and take the remainder. Notice, though, that the results hop about with no visible pattern: 5, 2, 10, 4, 20, 8.

Going backwards is the hard part. Suppose you are told only that some power of 5 gives 8 on this clock and asked which power it was. With ordinary powers you could home in on the answer, because they grow steadily: too small, try higher. On the clock there is nothing to home in on, and the obvious method is to try the powers one after another.

With 23 positions, that takes a moment. In real use, the clock number is more than 600 digits long, and the private numbers are enormous too. Two facts then matter:

- **Forwards stays fast.** There is a shortcut: square repeatedly to get the 2nd, 4th, 8th, 16th powers and so on, then multiply together the ones you need. Even a gigantic power takes only a few thousand multiplications, which a computer finishes in a fraction of a second.
- **Backwards does not.** Trying the powers one by one is hopeless, and the best methods anyone has found would still keep today's computers busy for many thousands of years at the least.

So raising to a power on a clock is the disguise we wanted. Anyone can apply it, and nobody can undo it in a useful amount of time.

## The exchange, step by step

Here is the whole procedure with our small numbers.

1. **Agree the setup, in the open.** Alice and Bob agree to use the 23-position clock and the starting number 5. Eve hears this, and it does not matter.

2. **Each picks a private number and sends its disguise.** Alice picks 6 and tells nobody. She works out 5⁶ on the clock, gets 8, and sends 8 to Bob. Bob picks 15 and tells nobody. He works out 5¹⁵ on the clock, gets 19, and sends 19 to Alice.

3. **Each raises the other's number to their own private power.** Alice takes Bob's 19 and works out 19⁶ on the clock. The answer is 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and works out 8¹⁵ on the clock. The answer is also 2.

They now share the number 2, and neither of them ever sent it.

The match is not luck. Bob's 19 is really 5¹⁵, so Alice has worked out 5¹⁵ raised to the 6th power. Alice's 8 is really 5⁶, so Bob has worked out 5⁶ raised to the 15th power. Raising a power to a power multiplies the two exponents, and 15 × 6 is the same as 6 × 15, so both have computed 5⁹⁰. Taking remainders along the way does not change the final remainder, so they must land on the same position of the clock.

## Why Eve is stuck

Here is who knows what at the end:

| | Alice | Bob | Eve |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clock 23, starting number 5 | yes | yes | yes |
| Alice's public number, 8 | yes | yes | yes |
| Bob's public number, 19 | yes | yes | yes |
| Alice's private number, 6 | yes | no | no |
| Bob's private number, 15 | no | yes | no |
| Shared secret, 2 | yes | yes | no |

Eve holds 8 and 19, and the natural thing to try is combining them. But multiplying them gives 5⁶ × 5¹⁵, which is 5²¹, because multiplying powers adds the exponents. She needs 5⁹⁰, where the exponents are multiplied.

The only known way to get there is to raise one public number to the other person's private power. That means first recovering 6 from 8, or 15 from 19, which is exactly the backwards problem. On our toy clock she can do it by trial. On a 600-digit clock she cannot.

Alice and Bob each escape that problem because each already holds one private number. That one piece of knowledge is the whole of their advantage over Eve.

## Two limits worth knowing

**The difficulty is observed, not proven.** Nobody has shown that the backwards problem must be hard. The method rests on the fact that decades of effort by mathematicians have not produced a fast way to do it. A large enough quantum computer, a kind of machine that does not yet exist at the needed scale, would be able to, and replacements built on different problems are already being introduced for that reason.

**The exchange hides the secret but does not identify the other person.** We assumed Eve only listens. If she can also intercept and replace messages, she can run one exchange with Alice while posing as Bob, and another with Bob while posing as Alice. She then shares a secret with each and reads everything in between. Real systems prevent this with a second public-key tool, the digital signature, which lets a trusted party vouch that a public number really belongs to the person or website it claims to. This is what your browser is checking when it shows a padlock.

## What happens next

The shared number is not the message itself. It becomes the key for an ordinary, fast scrambling method that both sides then use for the rest of the conversation.

Your phone or computer goes through this whole procedure, with far larger numbers and often a different kind of arithmetic following the same pattern, every time it opens a secure connection to a website. It takes well under a second.
