A heat pump can heat a house on a cold day because it does not make heat. It moves heat that already exists outside, and "cold" is a relative idea. Air at 0°C still holds a large amount of thermal energy. Absolute zero is about -273°C, so a winter day is still far above the point where there is nothing left to take.

**The fridge in reverse**

Your fridge keeps its inside cold by pulling heat out of the food compartment and dumping it into your kitchen through the coils on the back. A heat pump does the same job in the opposite direction. It pulls heat out of the outdoor air, or the ground, and releases it inside your home. Your fridge already moves heat from a cold place to a warm one, so the principle is already in your kitchen.

**How the trick works**

A sealed loop carries a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at very low temperatures. The loop has four main parts.

1. **Evaporator (outside).** The refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, even when that air is -5°C. Heat flows from the air into the refrigerant, which boils into a gas.
2. **Compressor.** This electric pump squeezes the gas. Compressing a gas heats it up sharply, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor much hotter than your house, typically 50 to 70°C.
3. **Condenser (inside).** The hot gas passes through a heat exchanger connected to your radiators, underfloor heating or hot water tank. It is hotter than the water, so it releases its heat and turns back into a liquid.
4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid pressure drops suddenly, which makes it very cold again, and the cycle repeats.

The compressor is what lets heat travel "uphill", from cold to warm. Heat moves that way only when work is put in, and the electricity driving the compressor supplies that work.

**Why this beats a boiler on efficiency**

A gas boiler burns fuel and converts it to heat at around 90 to 95 percent efficiency at best. It can never deliver more heat than the fuel contains. A heat pump delivers more heat than the electricity it uses, because most of the output is heat collected from outside. The measure is the coefficient of performance (COP). A COP of 3 means that each 1 kWh of electricity delivers about 3 kWh of heat, an effective efficiency of 300 percent. Over a whole season, good installations in typical UK-style climates average roughly 2.5 to 4.

**The catch: colder weather lowers performance**

The colder it gets outside, the harder the compressor works to lift heat to the temperature you need, so the COP falls. On a very cold day it might drop to 2 or a little below. Modern units still run at -15°C or lower. They just use more electricity per unit of heat. Defrost cycles are also normal, because frost builds on the outdoor unit and is periodically melted off.

**What this means for your decision**

- **Your house matters most.** Heat pumps work best delivering water at lower temperatures, around 35 to 50°C. That suits well-insulated homes with larger radiators or underfloor heating. A draughty, poorly insulated house may need insulation work or radiator upgrades first, which adds cost.
- **Running cost depends on prices.** Electricity usually costs several times more per kWh than gas. A COP of 3 only breaks even with gas if electricity costs less than about three times as much. Check your tariffs, and consider a time-of-use tariff, as heat pumps combine well with them.
- **Upfront cost is higher.** Installation usually costs more than a new boiler, though grants and subsidies may offset some of it where available.
- **Practicalities.** You need outdoor space for the unit, and usually a hot water cylinder. Noise is modest but worth checking against neighbours.
- **Carbon.** Since the electric grid keeps getting cleaner, a heat pump normally cuts household emissions substantially versus gas.

**Bottom line**

A heat pump works at low temperatures because it is a heat mover rather than a heat maker. If your home is reasonably insulated and your electricity price is not extreme, it can be cheaper to run and much cleaner than a gas boiler. Before committing, ask installers for a heat-loss survey of your home, since that survey, more than the weather, decides whether a heat pump will perform well for you.
