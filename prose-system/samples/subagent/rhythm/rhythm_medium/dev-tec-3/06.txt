A lithium-ion cell is charged by forcing current through it in the direction opposite to the one it delivers during discharge, which drives lithium ions out of the positive electrode, across an electrolyte-soaked separator, and into the graphite of the negative electrode. The charger must push the cell's terminal voltage above its open-circuit voltage to make this happen, and the amount of overvoltage determines how fast the current flows. Because the open-circuit voltage rises with state of charge, from roughly 3.0 volts when empty to about 4.2 volts when full for a common cobalt-based chemistry, the charger has to keep raising its target as the cell fills.

Most chargers therefore use a two-stage method called constant-current, constant-voltage. In the first stage the charger holds the current steady, typically at a rate that would fill the cell in about one hour, and lets the terminal voltage climb on its own. This stage delivers the bulk of the energy, often around seventy to eighty percent of capacity. When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, usually 4.2 volts, the charger switches to the second stage and holds the voltage fixed. The upper limit is a hard safety boundary, because exceeding it accelerates electrolyte decomposition and can drive metallic lithium to plate onto the anode, which shortens life and creates a short-circuit hazard.

The current falls during the constant-voltage stage because of how the voltage is divided inside the cell. The terminal voltage equals the open-circuit voltage plus the current multiplied by the cell's internal resistance, along with additional polarization terms. As charging proceeds, the open-circuit voltage creeps toward the fixed limit, so the remaining headroom shrinks. Since the headroom is what drives current through the internal resistance, the current must decay, roughly exponentially, to keep the terminal voltage from overshooting. A second effect is diffusion. Lithium must migrate into the interior of the graphite particles and fill the available sites, and as the surface layers become saturated the concentration gradient that pulls more lithium inward weakens, so the process slows regardless of the electrical drive.

Charging is considered complete when the current tapers to a small cutoff, commonly a few percent of the initial rate. Pushing the cell to a lower cutoff would add only a sliver of capacity at the cost of considerable time and extra stress. This is also why fast-charging schemes concentrate on the first stage and why a device may show eighty percent in half an hour but need another hour to finish.
