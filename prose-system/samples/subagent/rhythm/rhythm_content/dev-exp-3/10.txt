Imagine that Alice and Bob want to agree on a secret number, so that they can afterwards scramble their messages with it, but the only way they can talk is by shouting across a crowded square in which an eavesdropper, Eve, hears every word. For most of history this looked impossible, because anything agreed in the open is known to everyone who was listening, and the standard remedy was for the two people to meet beforehand and swap a key in private, which is no help to strangers. The surprising discovery of the 1970s was that a shared secret can be built out of entirely public pieces, provided the pieces are combined using an operation that is easy to perform in one direction and hopelessly slow to reverse.

The easiest way to feel the idea is with paint rather than numbers. Suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud on a common starting colour, say yellow, and Eve hears this too. Alice privately picks a colour of her own, perhaps a particular shade of red, and mixes one litre of it into the yellow. Bob does the same with his own private blue. Each then sends the resulting mixture across the square, so that Eve can see both tubs. Now Alice takes Bob's tub and stirs in her private red, while Bob takes Alice's tub and stirs in his private blue, and because mixing paint does not care about the order, both of them end up holding exactly the same final colour, made of yellow plus red plus blue. Eve holds two tubs, one containing yellow and red and the other yellow and blue, and she cannot get to the final colour because she would need to remove one colour from a mixture, and unmixing paint is for practical purposes impossible. What makes the scheme work is that each person keeps one ingredient that never leaves their hands, and that the mixing is easy to do and impossible to undo.

Paint is only an analogy, so we need a mathematical operation with the same character, and the one that serves is arithmetic on a clock. Take a clock with 23 hours instead of 12, and whenever a calculation goes past 23, wrap around and keep only the remainder, exactly as you do when you say that 5 hours after 10 o'clock on a 12-hour clock it is 3 o'clock. Mathematicians call this working modulo 23, and nothing beyond division with remainders is needed to follow it. The operation we care about is repeated multiplication, in which you pick a base number, multiply it by itself a chosen number of times, and keep only the remainder after dividing by 23. With a base of 5, the first few powers are 5, 25 which wraps to 2, then 10, then 50 which wraps to 4, then 20, then 100 which wraps to 8, and so on, and the remainders jump around so erratically that they look random even though each step is perfectly simple.

Now let Alice and Bob do their shouting. In the square they announce the clock size, 23, and the base, 5, which together play the part of the yellow paint. Alice secretly chooses the number 6 and Bob secretly chooses 15, and these are their private colours, never spoken aloud. Alice multiplies 5 by itself 6 times, which gives 15625, and dividing that by 23 leaves a remainder of 8, so she calls out 8. Bob multiplies 5 by itself 15 times, which is a much larger number, but by tracking only remainders as he goes he finds that it leaves 19, so he calls out 19. These two shouted numbers are the tubs of mixed paint, and Eve now knows 23, 5, 8 and 19.

To finish, Alice takes Bob's 19 and multiplies it by itself 6 times, using her private 6, and keeps the remainder, which comes to 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and multiplies it by itself 15 times, using his private 15, and he too ends up with a remainder of 2. The reason they agree is that both calculations are really the base 5 multiplied by itself 6 times 15 lots, which is 90 times, and the order in which the multiplying is grouped does not change the result, just as the order of stirring did not change the paint. The number 2 never crossed the square, yet both of them hold it, and they can now use it as the key for scrambling everything else they say.

You might wonder why Eve cannot simply work backwards from the 8 she overheard to find Alice's private 6. With numbers this small she can, because she can try every possibility in a few seconds, which is why the example is a toy. The real strength comes from scale. In practice the clock has hundreds of digits instead of two, and the private numbers are similarly enormous, and while a computer can still raise a number to a huge power and keep the remainder with remarkable speed, by cleverly squaring as it goes rather than multiplying one step at a time, no one has found a fast way to go the other direction. The remainders leap about so unpredictably that the only known general method is to try candidates one after another, and with a clock of that size the number of candidates exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. The work needed to listen in therefore grows out of all proportion to the work needed to take part, and that lopsided gap is the whole security of the scheme.

This procedure, usually called Diffie-Hellman key exchange after two of its inventors, solves the problem of agreeing a secret, and a close cousin of the same thinking, named RSA after its inventors, solves the neighbouring problem of letting anyone send you a private message. In that version you publish a number, your public key, that anyone can use to lock a message, while you alone hold a companion number that unlocks it, and the one-way difficulty there lies in multiplying two huge prime numbers together, which is trivial, versus recovering the two primes from the product, which is not. Your public key is like an open padlock that you hand out freely, since snapping it shut needs no secret, whereas opening it needs the key that never leaves your pocket.

One weakness remains, and it is a practical one rather than a flaw in the mathematics. Eve can listen but, in the scheme as described, she can also pretend to be Bob, shouting her own number to Alice and a different one to Bob, so that she sits in the middle of two separate secrets and quietly relays everything between them. The defence is to have some trusted third party vouch that a particular public number really belongs to a particular person or website, which is what the certificates behind the padlock icon in your browser do. With that safeguard in place, the same ideas quietly protect nearly everything you do online, from the moment your phone contacts your bank to the card payment at a shop's checkout, allowing strangers who have never met, and who must talk where others can listen, to arrive at a secret known only to the two of them.
