**The Failure of a Successful Failure: Apollo 13**

By April 1970, the United States had already landed men on the Moon twice, and the public had moved on. When Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 1:13 p.m. Houston time on April 11, the television networks gave it little airtime. The crew were Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights, and two first-timers: Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, and Jack Swigert, the command module pilot. Swigert had joined the crew only three days before launch. Ken Mattingly, the original command module pilot, had been exposed to German measles, and flight surgeons would not risk him falling ill in lunar orbit.

The plan was for Lovell and Haise to land in the hilly Fra Mauro region while Swigert circled overhead. The spacecraft had two main parts. The command module, which the crew had named Odyssey, was a cone-shaped capsule joined to a cylindrical service module that held the main engine, the oxygen supply, and the fuel cells that generated electricity. The lunar module, Aquarius, was the spidery lander that would carry two men down to the surface.

For two days the flight went so smoothly that the controllers in Houston joked about being bored. On the evening of April 13, nearly 56 hours into the mission and about 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew finished a television broadcast that none of the networks carried live. A few minutes later, Mission Control asked Swigert to turn on the fans that stirred the supercooled oxygen in the service module's tanks, a routine chore that kept the gauges reading accurately.

He flipped the switch. Ninety-five seconds later the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Warning lights came on. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." When the ground asked him to repeat, Lovell answered: "Houston, we've had a problem."

At first nobody, in space or on the ground, understood what had happened. The readings made so little sense that some controllers suspected faulty instruments. Then Lovell looked out a window and saw a cloud of gas streaming away from the ship. "We are venting something out into space," he reported. It was oxygen. One tank had burst, and the other was leaking. Two of the three fuel cells soon died, and the third was weakening fast. Without oxygen the fuel cells could not make electricity, and without electricity the command module could not keep the crew alive. The Moon landing was off. The question now was whether three men could get home at all.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his team worked from a simple rule: protect what still works. The lunar module was the answer. It had its own oxygen, its own batteries, its own engine, and its own guidance computer. It had been built to keep two men alive for about two days, and now it would have to hold three for nearly four. Before Odyssey's power gave out, Swigert shut it down step by step, saving its batteries for the final hours, since only the command module had a heat shield that could survive the plunge through the atmosphere. Lovell and Haise had already powered up Aquarius and copied the navigation data across by hand. The lander had become a lifeboat.

The ship's path was the next problem. Apollo 13 had left an earlier course that would have looped it around the Moon and back to Earth on its own, a path called a free return, to aim for the landing site. Controllers decided not to fire the service module's big engine, which sat beside the damaged section and might itself explode. They used the lunar module's descent engine instead, designed for landing on the Moon and never meant to push the whole stack. About five and a half hours after the explosion, it fired for half a minute and put the crew back on a course for home.

On April 14 Apollo 13 swung behind the far side of the Moon, out of radio contact. Haise and Swigert, who had never been there, took photographs of the cratered surface passing beneath them. At that point they were about 248,000 miles from Earth, farther than any people had been before or have been since. Two hours after they came around the Moon, the crew fired the descent engine again for over four minutes. The burn cut roughly ten hours off the trip and moved the landing point to the Pacific Ocean, where the Navy's recovery ships were waiting.

Speed did not fix the other problems. Each new one arrived as the last was being dealt with. The first was the air. Three men breathing inside a cabin built for two produced more carbon dioxide than the lunar module's filters could remove, and the gas began to build toward dangerous levels. The command module had plenty of spare filter canisters, but they were square, and the lunar module's sockets were round. On the ground, engineers gathered materials they knew were on board: plastic bags, cardboard from the flight plan covers, suit hoses, and duct tape. They built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew, step by step. The astronauts assembled their version, and the carbon dioxide readings began to fall.

The second problem was cold. To save power, almost everything was switched off, including the heaters. The temperature inside the cabins dropped to around 38 degrees Fahrenheit, and moisture beaded on the walls and windows. The crew could barely sleep. Water was rationed because the lunar module needed it to cool its equipment, and the men drank so little that they became dehydrated. Haise developed a kidney infection and spent the last part of the trip shivering with fever.

The third was steering. The ship kept drifting off course, partly because of gas vented from the lunar module. A correction burn was needed, but the onboard navigation system could not be trusted for it. Lovell lined up the edge of the Earth in the lander's window as a fixed reference, Haise and Swigert handled other controls, and the three of them flew a fourteen-second burn by hand. It worked.

The final danger was restarting Odyssey. No one had ever brought a command module back to life after it had sat frozen and unpowered for days, and its batteries held little to spare. In Houston, Ken Mattingly, the man left behind because of the measles, spent long hours in a simulator with engineers working out a power-up sequence that would not drain them. The procedure was read up to Swigert, who copied pages of it by hand.

On April 17, a few hours before reentry, the crew released the service module. As it drifted away they saw the damage for the first time: an entire side panel had been blown off, and wiring and wreckage hung out of the gap. Lovell reported that a whole side of the spacecraft was missing. Some feared the blast had cracked the heat shield right next to it. Then the crew moved into Odyssey, powered it up, and let Aquarius go. "Farewell, Aquarius, and we thank you," said a voice from the ground.

During reentry the superheated air around the capsule always cut off radio contact for about three minutes. This time the silence lasted longer, and the room in Houston went quiet as the seconds passed. Then Swigert's voice came through, and soon television showed three orange and white parachutes opening above the Pacific. Odyssey splashed down near Samoa at 12:07 p.m. Houston time, and the crew were lifted aboard the USS Iwo Jima.

The investigation that followed traced the explosion to a fault that started years before launch. Safety switches inside the oxygen tank had been designed for an older, lower voltage and were never upgraded. During a ground test before the flight, they fused shut, and the tank's heater cooked its wiring at temperatures that damaged the insulation. When Swigert turned on the fans in space, the bare wires sparked and the tank exploded.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon. NASA later called it a successful failure, because a crew that seemed lost got home through improvisation, discipline, and the combined work of the people in space and on the ground.
