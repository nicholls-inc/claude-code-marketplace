**Let the Market Decide How Many Parking Spots a Building Needs**

Walk through almost any American city and you will find a quiet rule shaping everything around you. Before a developer can build apartments, a restaurant, or a clinic, the zoning code dictates how many parking spaces must come with it. Two per unit. One for every 200 square feet of retail. One for every three seats in a restaurant. These minimum parking requirements look like technical housekeeping. They are among the most expensive and least examined policies in city government, and cities should repeal them.

Start with the cost. A surface parking space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles and ramps, and building one commonly runs several thousand dollars. A structured space can cost $30,000 or more, and an underground one considerably more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, home prices, and menu prices, and everyone pays them, including the household that owns no car. Studies of apartment pricing have estimated that required parking can add hundreds of dollars a month to rent. A city that says it wants affordable housing while mandating a $40,000 garage stall for every unit is working against itself.

The requirements also eat land. In many downtowns, parking occupies more area than the buildings it serves. That is land that could hold homes, shops, or parks, and land that generates no tax revenue. A parking lot yields a tiny fraction of the property tax per acre that a mixed-use building does. Cities then wonder why their budgets strain to maintain sprawling infrastructure. The mandate bakes in a pattern of development that cannot pay for itself.

Defenders of the rules raise a reasonable worry: without mandates, new buildings will have too little parking, and residents will crowd the street spaces outside. But look at what the mandate actually assumes. It assumes a city planner, working from a manual of national averages, knows better than the developer, the lender, and the future tenants how much parking a particular building needs. Those standards often come from a handbook of suburban traffic counts, sometimes drawn from a handful of sites. They routinely overshoot. Visit a mandated lot on an ordinary weekday and you will see rows of empty stalls, built because a formula said so.

Developers and lenders have every incentive to supply parking where people will pay for it. A building aimed at families in a car-dependent neighborhood will include parking because tenants demand it. A building near a train station, aimed at people who do not drive, can skip it and charge less. Removing the minimum does not ban parking. It lets each project choose. Cities that have tried this offer evidence. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017 and saw projects proceed with right-sized parking rather than a wave of parking-free towers. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and Edmonton have since followed with their own reforms. The apocalypse that critics predicted has not arrived.

The street-parking concern is real but solvable, and not by mandates. If curb space is scarce, the answer is to price it. Modern meters and residential permit programs can charge enough to keep a space or two open on every block, and the revenue can fund local improvements. This is far more efficient than forcing every private building to hoard its own supply. A mandate treats parking as a property of each building. Pricing treats it as what it is, a shared resource that responds to demand.

There is also a fairness argument. Minimum requirements make it nearly impossible to build the small, cheap forms of housing that many people want: the fourplex on a modest lot, the converted corner store, the studio over a shop. On a small parcel, the mandated spaces can eat the whole site, killing the project before it starts. Older neighborhoods that were built before these rules, the ones people pay a premium to live in, would often be illegal to build today. We have outlawed the very places we admire.

Environmental and safety goals point the same direction. Abundant, free, mandated parking is an invitation to drive for every trip. When you remove the guarantee of a free space at every destination, more people choose to walk, bike, or ride transit for some trips, and that means fewer emissions and less congestion. Cities spend heavily on transit while their own zoning codes subsidize driving.

None of this requires zealotry. A city can repeal minimums and still allow, and even encourage, parking where it makes sense. It can keep accessible spaces for people with disabilities. It can adopt maximums in places where it wants to discourage driving. The point is to stop pretending that one number, set in advance for every building in every neighborhood, is the right answer.

Parking is a good that people will pay for when they need it. Let those who build, finance, and use buildings make that call. The city's job is to manage the public street fairly, not to dictate the contents of every private lot. Repeal the minimums, price the curb, and let neighborhoods build what their residents actually want.
