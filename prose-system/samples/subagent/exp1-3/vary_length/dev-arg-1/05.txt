**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American suburb and you will find a monument to a rule nobody voted for. It is the empty parking lot. It sits in front of the pharmacy at noon on a Tuesday, ringed by faded white lines, taking up three times the space of the building it serves. It exists because a city ordinance said it must.

Minimum parking requirements are among the most consequential and least debated rules in urban planning. They dictate how many spaces a developer must build per apartment, per restaurant seat, per square foot of office. Cities should repeal them. Not tweak them, not reduce them by a third, but remove them, and let the people who actually pay for parking decide how much to build.

Start with the basic problem: the numbers are made up. Most requirements trace back to a handful of traffic engineering manuals that sampled a small number of suburban sites, often at peak demand, and then treated the results as law. The Institute of Transportation Engineers has itself acknowledged that its data were never meant to set mandatory floors. Yet cities adopted them wholesale. A restaurant needs one space per 100 square feet because a formula said so, not because anyone checked whether the restaurant's customers arrive by car, bus, bike, or on foot.

The cost of this guesswork is enormous. A single surface space can consume 300 square feet once you count the aisles and the ramps. A structured space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build, and an underground one far more. Those costs do not vanish. They are folded into rents, menu prices, and the sticker price of homes. A tenant who owns no car still pays for the garage. Parking minimums are, in effect, a tax on everyone that subsidises driving.

They also make housing harder to build. Consider a small lot downtown where a developer wants to put up eight modest apartments. If the code demands two spaces per unit, the ground floor becomes a garage, the budget balloons, and the project may collapse. Or it shrinks to four larger, pricier units. In a country with a housing shortage, forcing homes to compete with cars for scarce land is a strange choice. Buildings that would have been approved in 1925 are often illegal today, and parking rules are a main reason why.

Then there is the effect on the places we love. The charming main streets people travel to visit, with shops pressed against the sidewalk and apartments above, could not be built under modern codes. Their lots are too small, their buildings too close together. Parking minimums quietly outlaw the form of development that produces walkable, lively, tax-productive neighborhoods. Studies of cities from Strong Towns analyses to municipal audits keep finding the same thing: compact, mixed-use blocks generate far more tax revenue per acre than a big-box store ringed by asphalt.

Critics raise a fair objection. Without requirements, won't developers build too little parking, and won't cars spill onto residential streets? It is a real concern, and it deserves an honest answer.

The answer is that removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it, because tenants and customers want it. Lenders often insist on it. A suburban shopping centre with no spaces would fail, and everyone involved knows it. What changes is that the amount is set by demand rather than by a formula, so a building near a train station can build fewer spaces while a rural strip mall builds plenty. The market is a better judge of local conditions than a number copied from a manual.

Where spillover is a genuine risk, cities have better tools than mandates. Charge for street parking at rates that keep one or two spaces open on every block. Offer residential permits that protect neighbours. Return the revenue to the district so residents see the benefit. Pricing manages scarcity far more precisely than a blanket requirement, which simply guarantees an oversupply in most places and a shortage in a few.

The evidence from places that have tried it is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its minimums citywide in 2017. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing list of others have followed. Developers there have not abandoned parking. They have built it more thoughtfully, shared it between uses, and in some cases built homes that previously would never have penciled out. The sky did not fall, and the cities got more flexible.

None of this requires hostility to cars. People will keep driving, and many must. The point is that a city should not force every project to cater to the most car-dependent version of itself. Choice is the goal. Someone who wants a home with two garage spaces can still find one. Someone who wants a cheaper apartment without a space they will never use should be allowed to rent it.

Land is the scarcest thing a city has. We should stop handing so much of it to empty asphalt by default. Repeal the minimums, price the curb sensibly, and trust builders and neighbours to work out the rest. The lots will be fuller, the rents lower, and the streets better for it.
