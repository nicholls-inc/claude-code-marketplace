The Pump on Broad Street

On the last night of August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, a crowded quarter of London, with a violence that astonished even a city used to the disease. People who were well at breakfast were dead by nightfall, drained by vomiting and diarrhoea so severe that their skin turned blue and their faces shrank. Within ten days about five hundred people living in a few streets around Broad Street had died. Families who could leave loaded carts and fled, and within a week most of the neighbourhood's houses stood empty.

Almost everyone, doctors included, believed they knew the cause. Cholera, like most epidemic disease, was blamed on "miasma": poisonous air rising from rotting filth, cesspools and bad drains. Soho stank, and so Soho sickened. The explanation seemed like plain common sense.

John Snow, a doctor who lived a few minutes' walk away, did not believe it. He was a quiet, methodical man of forty-one, the son of a Yorkshire labourer, who had made his name as a pioneer of anaesthesia; the year before, he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. For several years he had also been arguing an unpopular case about cholera. The disease attacked the gut, not the lungs, so he reasoned that its cause must be swallowed, not breathed. He suspected it travelled in water fouled by the waste of earlier victims. Few of his colleagues were persuaded.

When the Soho outbreak began, Snow saw a chance to test his idea. His suspicion fell quickly on the public pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street, which was popular locally because its water was cool and tasted cleaner than most. He looked at samples and found little to see. So he turned from the water to the people who drank it.

He obtained the official list of deaths from the General Register Office and then walked the streets, knocking on the doors of bereaved households to ask where the dead had fetched their water. The answers fell into a pattern. Nearly all the deaths had happened in houses for which the Broad Street pump was the nearest source of water. Of ten deaths in houses nearer a different pump, five were in families who told him they always sent to Broad Street because they preferred it, and three were children who went to school close by it.

The exceptions were just as telling. The workhouse on Poland Street, surrounded by stricken houses, held more than five hundred inmates and lost only five of them. It had its own well. The seventy-odd men at the brewery on Broad Street itself all survived. Their employer told Snow they were given an allowance of beer and never touched the pump; the brewery also had a well of its own.

Then there was a death that miasma could not explain at all. A widow living in Hampstead, miles away on its airy hill where there was no cholera, had died of it. Snow learned from her son that she had once lived on Broad Street and liked its water so much that a cart brought her a large bottle every day. She drank from the bottle delivered on 31 August and was dead two days later. A niece who was visiting and shared the water went home to Islington and died as well.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow took his findings to the local parish officials. They were doubtful but had no better idea, and the next day they had the pump handle removed so that no one could draw from the well. The deaths, already falling, dwindled away. Snow was honest about this: so many residents had fled that the outbreak was fading in any case, and he never claimed that removing the handle alone had ended it.

The final piece came from an unexpected ally. Henry Whitehead, a young local clergyman, thought Snow was wrong and set out to disprove him. He knew his parishioners far better than Snow did, and the more he asked, the more his evidence pointed to the pump. It was Whitehead who found where it all began. A baby at 40 Broad Street, the house right beside the pump, had fallen ill with diarrhoea days before the outbreak, and her mother had emptied the water used to wash her nappies into a cesspool at the front of the house. When the cesspool was dug out, its brick lining was found to be decayed, and it sat less than three feet from the well.

Snow later drew his evidence onto a street map, marking each death as a small black bar at the house where it occurred. The bars cluster thickly around the pump, and the map is now one of the most famous in medicine.

The authorities were not convinced. An official inquiry dismissed his theory, and Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, without recognition. Opinion shifted only in the following decades, and in 1883 the microbe that causes cholera was identified, living in water, as he had supposed. His patient method of counting cases, mapping them, and chasing down the ones that did not fit is now seen as a foundation of the modern study of how disease spreads.
