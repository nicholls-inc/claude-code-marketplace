In the late summer of 1854, a terror settled over Soho, a crowded district of London where tailors, printers, shopkeepers and the very poor lived packed together in narrow streets. Cholera, a disease that causes violent sickness and drains the body of water until the victim collapses, had visited Britain before. This time it struck with a speed that shocked even those who had seen it. In the first days of September, hundreds of people living within a few streets of Broad Street fell ill, and many died within a day. Families shut their doors and fled. Shops closed. Within about ten days, some five hundred people in the neighbourhood were dead.

Most learned people of the day believed they knew the cause. Disease, they said, travelled on bad air, a foul vapour rising from rot and filth, which they called miasma. It was a sensible idea in a city that stank. London was crowded, its drains were poor, and the smell of waste hung everywhere. But a physician named John Snow doubted it.

Snow lived a few streets from the outbreak. He was already well known as a doctor who had brought ether and chloroform to patients undergoing surgery, and he had even given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He was a quiet man who did not drink alcohol, and he approached problems with a habit of careful counting. Years earlier, during an epidemic in 1848 and 1849, he had reasoned that cholera attacked the bowels, not the lungs. If the illness began in the gut, he thought, then it must be swallowed, not breathed. Something in food or drink carried it from the sick to the healthy. His idea had met with polite indifference.

Now, with the disease raging on his doorstep, Snow had a chance to test it. He began where any good investigator begins, by asking who had died and where they had lived. He collected names and addresses from the local records, and he walked the streets knocking on doors. Soon a pattern emerged. The deaths clustered thickly around the water pump on Broad Street, where residents came to fill their buckets. The farther a house stood from that pump, the fewer deaths it had.

Snow drew all of this on a map. He marked each death with a small black bar at the address of the house, and the bars stacked up like a dark crowd around the pump. This map is now among the most famous images in the history of public health, though it was the careful questioning behind it that mattered most.

Yet a cluster alone proves little, so Snow hunted for the exceptions, which are often where the truth hides. A workhouse on nearby Poland Street housed more than five hundred people, yet only a handful died. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own well and did not use the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street employed about seventy men, and none of them died. The workers were given beer, and the brewery also had its own well. Then there was the strange case of a widow in Hampstead, far from Soho. She died of cholera, as did her niece. Snow discovered that she had once lived in Broad Street, loved the taste of the pump water, and had a servant carry a bottle of it to her every day. No one else in her neighbourhood fell ill.

On the evening of 7 September, Snow went to the local Board of Guardians and laid out his evidence. The members were not fully convinced the water was to blame, but they were willing to try something. The next day, the handle was removed from the Broad Street pump. Without a handle, no one could draw water. The outbreak was already fading, since so many residents had fled, so the removal alone cannot be credited with ending it. Still, the gesture became a symbol.

Snow was helped by a local clergyman named Henry Whitehead, who at first believed in miasma and set out to prove Snow wrong. Whitehead knew the neighbourhood and its people. As he gathered information, he changed his mind. He also found the likely origin. A baby girl living at 40 Broad Street had died of a diarrhoea illness just before the outbreak, and her family's cesspool lay very close to the well. When the cesspool was dug up, its brickwork was found to be decayed, so waste could leak into the water supply.

Officials were slow to accept the finding. The authorities preferred to blame the air, and some wished to avoid the expense of reforming water supplies. Snow died in 1858, still not widely believed. Only later, as microbes were identified and proven, did his reasoning win out. Today he is remembered as a founder of modern epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through communities, and for showing how a map and some patient questions can save lives.
