A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The trouble starts with the fact that there are two ways of measuring time. One is astronomical: a day is the time it takes our planet to rotate once, and the sun is highest in the sky at about the same moment each noon. The other is atomic: since the 1950s, clocks that count the vibrations of atoms such as caesium have kept time with astonishing steadiness, drifting by less than a second over many millions of years. The Earth, by comparison, is a poor timekeeper. Tides, shifting ice and the movement of molten rock inside the planet slow and speed its spin in ways that cannot be predicted far ahead, and over the long run the rotation has tended to lag behind the atomic clocks.

To stop the two from drifting apart, international authorities introduced the leap second in 1972. Whenever the difference between atomic time and the Earth's rotation threatened to exceed nine-tenths of a second, a body in Paris that monitors the planet's spin announced that an extra second would be inserted at the end of June or December. Clocks would show 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. Twenty-seven such seconds were added between 1972 and the end of 2016.

The practice has become a nuisance, however, because modern life depends on computers that expect every minute to have exactly sixty seconds. A leap second can confuse software, and it has been blamed for crashes and outages at major websites and data centres. Financial markets, telecommunications networks, satellite navigation systems and power grids all need timestamps that agree to the millisecond, and engineers often have to improvise, for instance by stretching the last seconds of a day so that the extra one is spread out. These workarounds differ from one company to the next, which creates its own risks. There is also the awkward prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second would have to be removed, something that has never been tried and that few systems are designed to handle.

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the international body that sets the standards for measurement, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. In the meantime the Earth's rotation has been running slightly faster than expected, which has made the case for ending the practice even stronger. Instead, the gap between atomic time and the sun is to be allowed to grow, and officials will decide later how to handle it, perhaps by correcting it in a single step many decades from now. For most people the change will be invisible, since a minute's difference between clock time and the sun would be imperceptible in daily life.
