Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules, and they should do it soon. The case does not rest on hostility to cars. It rests on a simple observation: a city that dictates how much parking every building must contain is making a private business decision on behalf of thousands of property owners, and it is making that decision badly.

Consider what a minimum parking requirement actually does. A developer who wants to build twenty apartments near a train station is told that the city will only approve the project if it includes thirty parking spaces, whether or not the future residents own cars and whether or not the developer believes anyone will pay for the spaces. Those spaces are not free. A surface stall costs thousands of dollars to build, and a space in an underground garage can cost tens of thousands. That expense is folded into the rent or the purchase price, so tenants who own no car end up subsidizing the storage of other people's vehicles. A rule meant to ease a minor inconvenience for drivers becomes a tax on everyone who lives in the building.

The requirements also shape the physical city in ways that few residents ever notice. Land that could hold homes, shops, or small offices is surrendered to asphalt, and the buildings that remain are pushed farther apart. Distances grow, walking becomes less pleasant, and the destinations that once sat close together are separated by seas of empty stalls. In many older neighborhoods, a corner lot that once supported a bakery or a four-unit building cannot be redeveloped today because the required parking would not fit on the site. The rules freeze in place a style of building that the city itself would never have chosen to design.

Defenders of the rules usually make a practical argument: if buildings do not provide enough parking, cars will spill onto the street and neighbors will suffer. This worry deserves a serious answer, but it confuses two separate problems. Crowded curbs are a pricing problem, not a construction problem. Cities that charge nothing for street parking invite shortages, because anything free will be overused. The remedy is to manage the curb with permits, meters, or time limits, not to force every new building to hide its own oversized garage. Where a street is genuinely scarce, a city can price it and use the revenue to improve the neighborhood, which turns a source of resentment into a source of funding.

A second objection holds that developers will build too little parking and leave residents stranded. The evidence from places that have already acted points the other way. When Buffalo eliminated its requirements citywide in 2017, developers did not stop building parking. They built what the market asked for, and in some cases they built more than the old formula would have demanded. Removing a minimum does not forbid parking; it simply returns the decision to the people who bear the cost of getting it wrong. A lender will not finance an apartment building that nobody can reach, and a landlord who loses tenants over a missing garage will learn quickly. The market is not perfect, but it is far more responsive than a ratio copied decades ago from a manual written for suburban shopping centers.

Those ratios deserve a closer look, because their origins are shaky. Many of the numbers in zoning codes trace back to surveys of a handful of suburban sites, often measured at the busiest hour of the year and sometimes with no connection to the type of building in question. Planners then treated those peaks as the baseline for every project. The result is parking designed for the day after Thanksgiving and left empty the other three hundred and sixty-four days. No sensible business would build a warehouse sized for its single busiest afternoon, yet that is exactly what the rules demand of homes and stores.

There is also a matter of fairness and housing affordability. Cities across the country are struggling with shortages that push rents beyond the reach of ordinary workers, and they have few levers that cost the public nothing. Repealing parking minimums is one of them. It does not require a subsidy or a new agency. It lets smaller and cheaper projects pencil out, makes it easier to convert old buildings to new uses, and allows homes to be built on lots that were previously unusable. Households that choose to live without a car can finally find housing that does not charge them for one.

None of this means cities should wage war on driving. Plenty of people will continue to rely on cars, and plenty of developers will keep providing ample parking because their customers demand it. The point is that the choice should be made building by building, by people who have money at stake, and not decreed in advance by a code that cannot know the particulars of any single site. A city that removes its minimums is not abandoning drivers. It is admitting that it never knew how many spaces each building needed, and trusting the people who do.
