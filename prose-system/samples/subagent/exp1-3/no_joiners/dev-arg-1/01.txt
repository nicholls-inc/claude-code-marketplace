Scrap the Parking Minimums

Every city has a quiet rule that shapes its streets more than any mayor ever will. It says that before anyone can build an apartment, a shop or a clinic, they must also build a set number of parking spaces. The number usually comes from a formula written decades ago, and it rarely has anything to do with how people actually live. Cities should repeal these minimum parking requirements entirely.

The first reason is simple honesty about cost. Parking is not free. A surface space costs thousands of dollars to build, and a space in a garage or underground can run to tens of thousands. Those costs do not vanish when a law forces a developer to pay them. They are passed on to tenants, buyers and customers. A renter who owns no car still pays for the space through a higher rent. A minimum requirement is a hidden tax on everyone, and it falls hardest on the poor, who are the least likely to own a car.

The second reason is housing. Cities across the world are short of homes, and many make the shortage worse by demanding that each new unit come with one or two spaces. On a small lot, that demand can decide whether a project is possible at all. Land that could hold six apartments instead holds six cars. Developers drop units, skip the project, or build something larger and pricier to spread the cost. Places that have removed the requirements, such as Buffalo, Minneapolis and Auckland, have seen builders adapt rather than disappear. Buffalo's reform in 2017 was among the first citywide repeals in the United States, and the sky did not fall. Many developers still build parking where buyers want it. They simply build the amount the market asks for, not the amount a formula dictates.

That points to the central argument. Minimums rest on the belief that planners know better than builders and tenants how many spaces a building needs. They do not. The formulas often come from surveys taken at suburban sites with ample free parking, which means they measure what happens when parking is free and then require cities to keep it that way. The logic is circular and it guarantees an oversupply. Anyone who has seen a vast, empty lot beside a half full shopping centre on an ordinary weekday has seen the proof.

Critics raise a fair worry. If developers build less parking, will cars spill onto neighbouring streets and make life miserable for residents? Sometimes, yes, in the short run. But the answer is to manage the street, not to force every building owner to solve the problem with concrete. Cities can price curb parking according to demand, issue permits for residents, and spend the revenue on improving the neighbourhood. When the kerb has a sensible price, spaces turn over, shoppers find a spot, and the shortage people fear mostly disappears. Removing minimums does not ban parking. It returns the decision to the people who will pay for it.

Another objection is that some people truly need to drive, such as older residents, people with disabilities and tradespeople with heavy tools. That is true, and nothing in a repeal stops a developer from providing spaces for them. Many cities that drop minimums keep their rules for accessible bays. A building aimed at older residents will include parking because its customers want it. What a repeal ends is the requirement that a young couple who cycle to work must pay for two spaces they will never use.

Then there is the matter of the places we want to live in. Parking minimums push buildings apart, fill street frontages with asphalt, and make walking unpleasant and often pointless. They make the old, compact neighbourhoods that people love to visit illegal to build today. Many of the most admired streets in any city would fail a modern parking code. Removing minimums lets those forms return, with shops close together, homes above them and a pavement worth walking on.

There is also a climate and fiscal case. Land given to parking yields little tax compared with land given to homes and businesses, so a city rich in asphalt tends to be poor in revenue. Driving encouraged by free parking adds emissions and congestion. A city that wants to meet its own goals cannot also mandate the very thing that works against them.

None of this requires a leap of faith. It requires only that we treat parking like any other good, supplied when people value it and priced when it is scarce. Cities that have tried this report more housing, more varied businesses and no collapse in car access. Cities that cling to the old formulas keep paying for empty spaces.

Repeal the minimums. Let builders, lenders and tenants decide how much parking a building should have. If they want it, it will get built. If they do not, the city will have saved itself a great deal of concrete and a great deal of money.
