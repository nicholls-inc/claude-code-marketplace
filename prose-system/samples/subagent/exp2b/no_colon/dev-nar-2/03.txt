The Long Way Home

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 13 off the pad at Kennedy Space Center in Florida. It was the seventh crewed mission in America's Apollo program and was meant to be the third to land on the Moon. By then the public had grown a little bored with lunar flights. The first landing had been a global event, the second had drawn less attention, and the networks had declined to broadcast the television transmissions that Apollo 13 planned to send home.

The crew was commanded by Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier spaceflights who was about to make his fourth. Fred Haise, a rookie, was to pilot the lunar module. The third seat should have gone to Ken Mattingly, but a few days before launch doctors learned he had been exposed to German measles. Mattingly never fell ill, yet the flight surgeons would not take the chance. His backup, Jack Swigert, took his place with barely two days of joint training.

The spacecraft was really two vehicles joined together. The command module, which Swigert named Odyssey, was the cone-shaped cabin where the men would live and in which they would eventually come home. Attached behind it was the service module, a cylinder holding the engine, fuel cells, water, and the tanks of oxygen and hydrogen that fed them. The lunar module, called Aquarius, rode in a compartment below, waiting to carry two men down to the surface.

The first two days went smoothly. The crew coasted toward the Moon, and the ship was in such good order that controllers in Houston joked about how quiet the shifts had been. On the evening of April 13, the crew finished a television broadcast that few people watched. Mission Control then asked Swigert to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks, a routine step meant to keep their contents from stratifying.

About fifty-six hours into the flight, and roughly two hundred thousand miles from Earth, Swigert flipped the switch. A few seconds later the crew heard a bang and felt the ship shudder. Warning lights lit up across the panel. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it for the controllers, who were already watching the numbers on their consoles go wrong.

The trouble was in oxygen tank number two, inside the service module. Before launch, during a ground test, the tank's heaters had been overworked and had cooked the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside it. No one had noticed. When Swigert turned on the fans, a spark ran along the damaged wires, the insulation ignited, and pressure built until the tank burst. The blast tore away a whole side panel of the service module and knocked the neighboring tank out as well. Within hours both tanks were empty. Without oxygen, the fuel cells could not make electricity or water, and the command module was on its way to becoming a dead machine.

Lovell looked out the window and saw gas streaming into space. He knew what it meant. The landing was gone, and the question now was whether anyone would get home at all.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz gathered his teams and set one rule. The crew would be saved by what the ship could still do, and no one would waste time mourning what it could not. The idea that rescued them was one that had been considered and, in some quarters, dismissed as a stunt. Aquarius had its own power, oxygen, water and engine, all meant to support two men for about two days. It could serve as a lifeboat for three men for four. Odyssey would be shut down almost completely to save its small reserve of battery power for the final descent. The crew moved into the lunar module, and Odyssey went dark.

Next came the route. The ship had been aimed on a path that would not return to Earth on its own, so the first task was to bend the trajectory. Using the lunar module's descent engine, which had never been designed for such a job, the crew fired a burn that put them on a course to swing around the far side of the Moon and fall back toward Earth. Later, with the Moon behind them, they fired the engine again to speed the trip home and bring the landing zone into the Pacific. Passing about 158 miles above the lunar surface, the three men were farther from Earth than any humans had ever been or have been since. They had no time to enjoy the view. Haise and Lovell snapped a few photographs, and then the work resumed.

Life aboard Aquarius soon turned miserable. With the power down, the temperature dropped to near freezing, and moisture ran down the walls. The crew had little water. They rationed it to a few ounces a day, and Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. Sleep was almost impossible in the cold, and the men were exhausted and dehydrated.

The next threat was carbon dioxide. The lunar module's air filters, built for two men over two days, were quickly saturated by three men over four. Levels climbed toward the point at which the crew would grow confused and eventually be poisoned. The command module had plenty of spare filters, but they were square and the lunar module's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the same items the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a spacesuit hose and a roll of duct tape. They built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew step by step. The men copied it, and within hours the carbon dioxide readings dropped. The contraption was ugly and it worked.

Mattingly, the man who had been grounded, spent those days in a simulator on the ground, working out how to bring Odyssey back to life using only the tiny amount of power available. His procedure had to be exact. Turn on one system too early and the batteries would drain before reentry.

Another problem came with a course correction. The ship's navigation system was shut down, and the crew had to align the spacecraft by hand, using the Earth's edge and the Sun as references. Lovell held the ship steady while the engine fired, and the results were good enough to keep them on target.

On April 17, nearing home, the crew powered up Odyssey and cast off the damaged service module. For the first time they saw the extent of the wreckage, a missing panel and trailing wires, and Lovell described it to Houston. Then they said goodbye to Aquarius, the little ship that had saved their lives, and released it to burn up in the atmosphere.

Reentry meant a few minutes of radio blackout while the capsule plowed through the air, and controllers had expected about three minutes of silence. The silence stretched on. Controllers who had survived four days of trouble now watched a blank screen, not knowing whether the heat shield, which had sat cold for days, had cracked. After about a minute and a half past the expected time, Swigert's voice came through. The parachutes opened, and the capsule settled into the South Pacific near Samoa, where the carrier Iwo Jima waited. The flight had lasted almost six days.

The mission failed to reach the Moon, but it is remembered as a success of a different kind. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tanks, the program continued, and Apollo 14 flew the following year. Mattingly never developed the measles and later flew to the Moon on Apollo 16. The crew of Apollo 13 had come home because people on the ground and in the cabin refused to stop solving problems.
