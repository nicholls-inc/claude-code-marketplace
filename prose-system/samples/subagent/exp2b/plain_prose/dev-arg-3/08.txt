Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. The case against it has a surface plausibility that fades once you look at what is actually being proposed, what children actually do with their hands and minds in the early years, and what is lost when a skill is dropped because it is no longer universal rather than because it is no longer useful.

Begin with the argument for abandoning it, which deserves a fair hearing. Adults rarely write by hand for long stretches. We type emails, thumb messages into phones, and dictate notes to software. Examinations in some places have moved to keyboards. Teachers report that cursive is slow to master, that many children never develop a fluent joined hand, and that the hours spent on looped letters could be spent on reading, mathematics or coding. Some adults who were taught a looped script at school abandoned it by their twenties for a printed scrawl, which suggests the effort was wasted. Finland and a number of American states have moved away from mandating cursive. If the future is keyboards, the argument runs, then teach typing and be done.

The trouble is that this reasoning treats handwriting as though its only purpose were producing legible text at the end of a school career. For a seven-year-old, handwriting is not primarily a method of output. It is a way of thinking, and a way of training the hand and eye together. The research here is not unanimous, and anyone who claims it is should be doubted, but the broad direction is consistent. Studies comparing children who learn letters by writing them with those who learn them by typing or tracing on a screen have repeatedly found that the writers recognise and remember letter forms better. Brain imaging work on young children suggests that forming a letter by hand activates the reading circuitry in ways that pressing a key does not. The act of generating a shape from nothing, with the body making a movement that the eye then checks, seems to bind visual and motor memory together. A key press is the same movement for every letter. A handwritten letter is a different movement each time, and that difference is what the brain appears to use.

Joined-up writing is the natural continuation of this. The objection that print is just as good misses what joining does mechanically. When a child prints, the pen lifts after every letter, and each letter is a separate decision about where to start and how to orient. A word becomes a series of discrete stamps. When a child joins letters, the word becomes a single movement with a rhythm, and the hand begins to learn the spelling as a pattern rather than as a chain of separate items. Teachers who work with children who struggle with spelling often notice that the common letter strings, the endings and blends, are far easier to hold in memory when they have a shape the hand knows. Anyone who has watched a child write the word the over and over, and seen it settle into a single flowing gesture, has watched this happen. Spelling and handwriting are not separate subjects that happen to share a lesson. They reinforce each other.

There is also the matter of speed and fluency, which counts for more than it seems. A child whose handwriting is slow and effortful has less attention left for the thing they are trying to say. This is the well-documented bottleneck of early composition: if forming letters consumes working memory, ideas get lost before they reach the page. A fluent joined hand, once secure, is faster than careful print, and it frees the mind. The key words are once secure. The reason cursive has a bad reputation is that it is often taught badly, late, and without enough practice, so that children are left half-fluent in a style they then drop. The remedy for poor teaching is better teaching, not abolition. A child who has been taught a simple, consistent joined style from the start, with exit strokes built into the letters from the first week, does not face a painful conversion in year three. They simply write.

Consider also what typing cannot replace. A keyboard is an excellent tool for producing and editing text, and children should learn to use one properly. But it is not a substitute for the foundational skill, any more than a calculator is a substitute for knowing that seven times eight is fifty-six. The point of learning arithmetic by hand is not that we will compute long division with a pencil all our lives. It is that the understanding built through doing it underlies everything that follows. Handwriting sits in the same position. The child who can write fluently by hand has a skill that works in a power cut, in a field, in an exam hall with no devices, in a margin, on a form at a counter, on a shopping list, on a birthday card. It requires no battery, no login and no update. It is the one writing technology that cannot fail in the ways that technology fails, and it takes up almost nothing in the curriculum compared with what it gives back.

A further point concerns equity, which the case for abandonment tends to overlook. A child at a well-resourced school with a laptop at home will pick up keyboard skills almost without trying. A child without those things relies on school to provide the foundations, and handwriting is the cheapest possible foundation. It needs paper and a pencil. If we remove it from the curriculum on the assumption that everyone will simply type, we widen the gap between those who can supplement school with devices and those who cannot. Moreover, children with certain difficulties, such as dyslexia and dyspraxia, often benefit specifically from the multisensory reinforcement that joined writing provides, and many specialist teachers recommend it precisely because the continuous movement helps with letter reversals and sequencing. Taking it out of the standard curriculum risks taking it away from the pupils who gain most.

Then there is reading. A child who cannot read joined-up writing is cut off from a vast amount of the written record: letters from grandparents, historical documents, annotations in the margins of books, the handwriting of a teacher marking their work. Reading cursive is a distinct skill, and one that is learned most naturally by writing it. If we stop teaching joined writing we do not just lose a way of producing text. We create a generation who cannot read the notes their own families leave them, and who will need to be taught decipherment as a specialist skill, the way we now teach palaeography to historians. That seems an odd trade for the sake of a few saved hours.

It is worth being honest about the limits of the argument. No one should claim that joined handwriting makes children cleverer in some general way, or that the evidence settles every question. The best studies are small, the effects are modest, and some of the more enthusiastic claims made for cursive, such as that it improves general intelligence, have no good support. Nor should anyone insist on a particular style. The elaborate looped scripts of the old copybook, with their flourished capitals, are not what is needed. A plain, economical joined hand, with simple letter forms and sensible joins, is quicker to learn and easier to read, and children should be free to develop it into their own as they grow. What matters is not the exact shape of the letters but the principle: that children learn a fluent, connected way of writing by hand, taught early and practised enough that it becomes automatic.

The deepest reason to keep teaching it is a modest one. Schools exist to pass on the skills a society wants its children to have, and some of those skills are valuable even though they are not universal in adult life. We still teach children to swim though most will never need to save a life, and to read a map though their phones know the way. We do this because the skill is useful, because the practice shapes the learner, and because a person who has it is more capable and less dependent than one who has not. Joined-up handwriting meets each of those tests. It is useful, it is formative, and it is cheap. A school day has room for it, and a child who learns it loses nothing and gains a good deal.

The sensible course, then, is not to choose between the pen and the keyboard but to teach both, in the right order. Start with the hand, because the hand builds the foundation. Teach a simple joined style early and consistently, give it the daily practice it needs to become fluent, and add keyboard skills alongside once the child is ready. The children who emerge will write quickly when they want to, type properly when they need to, read whatever is put in front of them, and think a little more clearly for having learned to make their own marks on a page. That is a better outcome than any that abandoning joined-up writing can offer, and it costs us only the willingness to teach it well.
