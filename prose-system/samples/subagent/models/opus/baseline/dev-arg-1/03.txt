**Let the Market Park the Cars: Why Cities Should Scrap Minimum Parking Requirements**

Walk through almost any American downtown and you will notice something odd. The most common use of land is not housing, shops, or parks. It is storage for idle cars. That is no accident of the market. It is the predictable result of a rule most residents have never heard of: the minimum parking requirement.

For decades, zoning codes have told builders how many parking spaces they must provide. A typical rule might demand one or two spaces per apartment, a set number per 1,000 square feet of retail, or a ratio tied to restaurant seats. These numbers were rarely based on careful study. Planners often copied them from neighboring towns or from manuals built on surveys of suburban sites with free parking and no transit. The result is a one-size-fits-all mandate applied to places that look nothing alike. Cities should get rid of these requirements. Doing so would make housing cheaper, neighborhoods livelier, and local budgets healthier, and it would not take away anyone's right to build parking.

Start with cost. Parking is expensive to build. A surface space uses valuable land. A space in a structured garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars, and an underground space more than that. When the law requires these spaces, developers pass the cost on to everyone, including tenants who do not own a car. Rents go up. Groceries cost more. Small restaurants that could have filled an empty storefront never open because the owner cannot find room for the spaces the code demands. The planning scholar Donald Shoup has spent a career documenting this hidden subsidy, and his conclusion is simple: free parking is never free. We just pay for it in ways we don't see.

The effect on housing is the most serious. Many promising projects, such as a fourplex on a narrow city lot, an apartment building over shops, or the conversion of an old office building, fail because the required parking will not fit or makes the numbers impossible. In the middle of a national housing shortage, we are blocking homes for people to protect the supply of spaces for cars. Cities that have scrapped the mandates offer encouraging evidence. Buffalo removed its citywide minimums in 2017. Minneapolis, San Jose, and Austin have since followed, and California has barred most parking mandates near major transit. Early research and reporting from these places suggest that developers keep building parking where people want it, build less where they don't, and add homes on sites that were not viable before.

Here is the key point opponents often miss: abolishing minimums is not a ban on parking. A grocery store in a car-dependent area will still build a lot, because its customers drive and it wants their business. A luxury tower may still include a garage because its buyers expect one. What changes is who decides. Instead of a decades-old ratio in a zoning book, the decision belongs to the people who know the site, the customers, and the costs. Where transit is good, people walk, or households own fewer cars, builders can provide less. That is how we handle almost every other feature of a building. The city does not require a set number of bathtubs or bike racks for every tenant. It should not require car storage either.

Critics raise a fair worry: if new buildings provide less parking, won't residents fight over curb space on nearby streets? Sometimes, yes. But the answer is to manage the curb, not to force every building to overbuild. Residential permit districts, metered pricing that rises with demand, and better enforcement can keep street parking available. Shoup's argument is that the right price for curb parking is the one that leaves a space or two open on each block. Cities that price their curbs sensibly also gain revenue they can put back into sidewalks, street trees, and transit, which in turn reduces the pressure to drive.

There are environmental and civic stakes as well. Big parking lots spread destinations apart, make walking unpleasant, and lock in car dependence for people who cannot afford a car, cannot drive, or would rather not. They add heat and stormwater runoff. They create dead zones on streets that could hold cafés, clinics, and homes. A city designed around mandatory parking ends up serving cars first and people second.

Removing minimums will not fix housing costs or traffic overnight. It is one reform among many, and it works best alongside zoning that allows more homes, investment in transit, and smart curb management. But it is among the cheapest and quickest reforms a city council can pass. It costs taxpayers nothing, frees up land that is already there, and trusts residents and builders to make reasonable choices.

We would never let a planning code set how many bedrooms every apartment must have, regardless of who lives there. It is time to stop doing the same with parking. Cities should repeal minimum parking requirements and let the land go to what people actually need.
