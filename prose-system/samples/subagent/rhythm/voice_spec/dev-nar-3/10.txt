At about half past noon on 15 January 1919, a large steel tank burst in the North End of Boston and released roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses into the street. The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and stood on Commercial Street near Keany Square. It was about 50 feet high and 90 feet across. The molasses was kept there until it could be turned into industrial alcohol, which was used in the making of munitions.

The day was unusually warm for January. The temperature had risen from near zero to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit over the previous two days, and a fresh shipment of molasses had been pumped into the tank the day before. Residents had long noticed that the tank leaked. The company's response was to paint it brown, which hid the stains. The tank had been built quickly in 1915, and it was never properly tested by being filled with water. The steel was also thinner than the design required.

Witnesses described a rumbling and a sound like a machine gun, which was probably the rivets giving way. The tank then split open, and a wave of molasses between 8 and 15 feet high moved out at an estimated 35 miles an hour. It broke the supports of the elevated railway on Atlantic Avenue and pushed a train car off its track. It crushed several buildings and knocked a firehouse off its foundations. Horses, wagons and people were caught in it. Pieces of the tank and the surrounding debris were carried along by the flow, and many of those who were trapped could not be pulled free.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. Those killed included children, a number of workers from the nearby docks and city yards, and members of the fire department. Rescuers waded in and found the molasses cold and thick, and it clung to everything it touched. Navy cadets from a ship in the harbour were among those who helped. The sticky mass slowed the work and made it difficult to find survivors. It took days to recover the last bodies. Cleaning the streets was a larger task, and crews used salt water from fireboats and spread sand and sawdust to absorb it. The harbour stayed brown into the summer.

A lawsuit followed. United States Industrial Alcohol argued that anarchists had set off a bomb, a claim that fitted the fears of the period, and that the company was therefore not at fault. A court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, heard testimony for about three years. He examined the engineering evidence and concluded that the tank had been built with too little strength to hold what was put into it. The company was found liable in 1925 and paid out about 600,000 dollars. Many families received around 7,000 dollars for a death.

The case is often cited as an early example of a company being held responsible for a failure it tried to blame on others. It also contributed to the practice of requiring engineers to review and sign plans for large structures. On warm days in the North End, some residents say the area still smells faintly sweet.
