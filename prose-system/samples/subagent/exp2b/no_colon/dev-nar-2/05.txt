The Long Way Home

On the evening of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 13 from Florida. Aboard were three men who expected to walk, or at least watch others walk, on the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was the most experienced astronaut alive, a veteran of two earlier flights and the Apollo 8 trip around the Moon. Fred Haise would pilot the lunar module. The command module pilot was Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days earlier. The original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was grounded by flight surgeons, even though he never fell ill.

The public had grown bored with the Moon. Apollo 11 had made history nine months before, and Apollo 12 had followed in November. Television networks did not bother to carry the broadcast from Apollo 13 on its second night in space. The crew nicknamed their command module Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius, and for two days the flight went almost eerily well.

Then, on April 13, about fifty-six hours after liftoff and some 200,000 miles from home, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module. This was routine. The tanks held supercooled liquids that tended to settle, and stirring them kept the gauges honest. Swigert flipped the switch. A few moments later the crew heard a loud bang, and the spacecraft shuddered.

Swigert radioed the famous understatement. "Houston, we've had a problem." Lovell repeated it for the controllers who had missed it. Within minutes the instruments told a grim story. Voltage was dropping. Oxygen pressure in one tank read zero, and the second tank was falling. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space. The service module, the unit behind the command module that supplied power, water and breathable air, was dying.

The cause lay years in the past. Oxygen tank number two had been dropped slightly during handling at the factory. Later, during a ground test, its heaters ran far too hot because a thermostat designed for a lower voltage had been left in place. The heat scorched the Teflon insulation on the wires inside the tank. When Swigert threw the switch, a spark from the damaged wiring ignited that insulation in pure oxygen. The blast tore a panel off the side of the service module and crippled the neighbouring tank.

The crew's first hours were a scramble to understand what had happened. Without the service module's fuel cells, the command module would run on batteries meant only for the final descent. Those batteries could not last. Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams realised that the Moon landing was finished. The only goal now was survival, and the only lifeboat was the lunar module Aquarius, built to carry two men for two days on the lunar surface. It would now have to sustain three men for four.

The crew crowded into the cramped lander and shut down Odyssey to save its remaining power. Then came the question of the route home. The spacecraft was on a path that would loop around the Moon and return toward Earth, but it had been adjusted to bring the crew near the lunar surface, not back to a safe splashdown. Controllers considered turning around immediately, but the damaged service engine was too risky to fire. They chose instead to swing around the Moon and use the lander's descent engine, designed for touching down gently, to speed the return. About two hours after passing the far side, Lovell and Haise fired it for roughly four minutes. The burn shortened the trip and aimed the ship at the Pacific.

At the far point of its loop, Apollo 13 was about 248,000 miles from Earth, farther than any human beings had ever travelled, a record that still stands. Cut off from Earth by the mass of the Moon, the crew could see the far side drifting beneath them. Nobody had time to enjoy it.

The next problem was less dramatic and just as deadly. Three men breathing in a lunar module that was meant for two were exhaling carbon dioxide faster than its filters could absorb. The gas was climbing toward levels that would first dull their thinking and then kill them. Odyssey carried spare filters, but they were square, while the lander's receptacles were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the items the crew had on board, among them cardboard from a flight manual, plastic bags, a spacesuit hose and a roll of duct tape. They worked out an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew step by step. Lovell and Haise built the contraption, fitted it, and watched the gauge fall back to safe levels.

Meanwhile the ship grew cold. With power rationed, the cabin temperature sank toward freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The crew drank little, because water was needed to cool the electronics, and Haise developed a painful infection. Sleep was close to impossible. Swigert, the newcomer, had to hold his own against two seasoned veterans in a space the size of a small closet.

Another hazard waited. The ship's navigation had been shut down, and the course needed a correction before reentry. Without the computer's alignment, Lovell had to line up the ship by hand, using the Earth itself as a reference. The planet's sunlit edge, he found, could be held in the window as a guide, and he steered a burn that kept them on the narrow corridor toward home. Reentering too steeply would burn the craft up, and too shallow would skip it back into space.

On the ground, Ken Mattingly, the astronaut who had been pulled from the crew, worked in a simulator for long hours with engineers to invent a way of powering Odyssey back up from its dead state using only the tiny reserve of battery power available. The procedure he developed had never been tested. It had to work the first time.

On the final day, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module. As it drifted away, Lovell saw that an entire side panel was missing, and that the engine bell and the surrounding equipment were blackened and torn. Pictures from the window showed the extent of the wound, and it gave the engineers on the ground vital evidence. Then, hours before reentry, the men said farewell to Aquarius. The lander that had saved them was cut loose and left to burn in the atmosphere.

Swigert powered up Odyssey following Mattingly's procedure, and it came alive. Reentry brought a nervous pause. The blackout caused by superheated air around the capsule normally lasts about three minutes, and it stretched longer, as controllers waited in silence for a voice from the ship. Then Swigert's call came through, and the room erupted. Three parachutes bloomed, and on April 17 Odyssey dropped into the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted almost six days.

Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, yet it is often called NASA's finest hour. The investigation that followed led to redesigned tanks, removal of the dangerous wiring, and an extra battery and water supply for later flights. Apollo 14 flew the following year and landed successfully. For the public, though, the lasting image was of three men huddled in a freezing capsule while thousands of people on the ground, armed with slide rules, tape and stubbornness, worked out how to bring them home. Lovell later titled his memoir of the flight "Lost Moon." The story deserves another description too, a crew and a ground team who, faced with a wrecked ship, refused to treat the problem as anything but solvable.
