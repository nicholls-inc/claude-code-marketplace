The Long Way Home: Apollo 13

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was making his fourth spaceflight and would have been the fifth person to walk on the lunar surface. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot. The third seat belonged to Jack Swigert, who had joined the crew only days before. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles and was held back by doctors, even though he never fell ill. Swigert, a bachelor and a quick study, took his place.

Apollo 11 and Apollo 12 had already landed on the Moon, and the public had begun to treat such trips as routine. Television networks did not bother to broadcast Apollo 13's early live transmissions. The crew called their command ship Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. For two days, the flight was almost dull.

Then, on the evening of April 13, the spacecraft was about 200,000 miles from home. Mission Control asked the crew to stir the tanks of supercold oxygen and hydrogen that fed the ship's fuel cells. Stirring kept the contents from stratifying and kept the gauges accurate. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later, a bang shook the cabin.

Swigert's calm report became famous: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Houston asked for a repeat, and Lovell answered, "Houston, we've had a problem." Warning lights flashed, and the main electrical power was falling. Lovell looked out a window and saw gas streaming into space. It was oxygen, and it was leaking from the service module, the cylindrical section behind the cone-shaped command module that held the ship's supplies.

The cause had been planted years earlier. Oxygen tank number two had been damaged during handling on the ground, and a heater switch inside it had been fused by an electrical overload during a pre-launch test. When Swigert flipped the stirring switch, bare wire sparked in a tank full of pure oxygen. The tank burst, and the blast tore off a panel of the service module and crippled the neighboring tank. Within a few hours, both tanks were empty. The fuel cells needed oxygen to make electricity and water, so the command module was dying.

The mission to land on the Moon was over in an instant. The new goal was survival. In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his controllers worked through the night, and the crew had to make a decision that no astronaut had faced. The command module's batteries had to be saved for the final minutes of reentry, so the men would have to move into Aquarius and use it as a lifeboat. The lunar module had been built to keep two men alive for about two days on the Moon. Now it had to support three men for four.

Before they could think about returning, the ship's course had to change. Apollo 13 had been set on a path that would not bring it back to Earth by itself. With Aquarius's descent engine, normally used for landing, the crew performed a burn to put the spacecraft on a trajectory that swung around the far side of the Moon and brought it home. Later, after rounding the Moon, they fired the engine again to speed the journey and aim for a Pacific splashdown. During the flyby, they passed about 158 miles above the lunar surface. At the point of greatest distance, they were nearly 249,000 miles from Earth, farther than any humans have ever traveled.

Life aboard the lander was miserable. To conserve power, the crew switched off almost everything, including the heat. The cabin temperature sank to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, and water condensed on the walls. Drinking water was strictly rationed, and each man received only a few ounces a day. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection, and the men slept poorly in the cold. The lack of water also meant they were slightly dehydrated, and all three lost weight.

An even more urgent danger came from the air itself. Carbon dioxide from the astronauts' breath was building up inside Aquarius, and the lander's filters, which were built for two men, were saturating. The command module had spare filters, but they were square, and the lander's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground, working against the clock, designed an adapter using only items the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock, a hose from a spacesuit, and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, and the three men built the contraption. The carbon dioxide level fell. The improvisation became one of the most celebrated problem-solving episodes in the history of spaceflight.

Meanwhile, a second team was working on how to restart the command module. Nobody had ever planned to turn the ship back on after shutting it down in space, and the batteries had to be used with extreme care. Mattingly, who had been kept off the flight, spent long hours in a simulator in Houston helping to devise a startup sequence that would use as little power as possible. He and the engineers wrote the new procedure in days, a job that normally took months.

As the spacecraft approached Earth, the crew also had to correct their course by hand. The guidance computer was mostly shut down, and the ship was drifting slightly off target. Using the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, as a reference, Lovell and Haise held the ship steady for a burn that had to be exactly right. If the angle of reentry was too shallow, the capsule would bounce off the atmosphere and into space. If it was too steep, it would burn up.

On April 17, the crew prepared for reentry. First they discarded the damaged service module, and as it drifted away, they saw the extent of the damage for the first time. A whole panel was missing, and the side of the module was a ruin. Then they jettisoned Aquarius, which had saved their lives, and it fell away to burn up in the atmosphere. Last, Odyssey was on its own, and the crew had to hope that the heat shield was intact.

Reentry produced an anxious silence. A radio blackout normally lasts about three minutes as the plasma around the capsule blocks signals. This time, because of the shallow path the spacecraft had taken, it stretched longer. In Mission Control, the controllers stared at their screens. Then, finally, Swigert's voice came through, and a cheer rose.

Odyssey's parachutes opened, and the capsule settled into the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The flight had lasted just under six days. The three men were exhausted, dehydrated, and unshaven, but they were alive and well. Lovell later said that the pull of the mission had been so strong that for a moment he was almost sorry to have lost the chance to land.

An investigation board traced the explosion to the faulty tank and recommended redesigning the oxygen system, which NASA did. Apollo flights resumed, and four more crews walked on the Moon. Apollo 13 never reached its destination, but it is remembered as a different kind of triumph. It showed what trained people, working together under extreme pressure and with very little, could do when everything had gone wrong. NASA officials later called it a successful failure.
