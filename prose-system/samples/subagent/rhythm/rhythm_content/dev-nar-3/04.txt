On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, a storage tank at the Purity Distilling Company on Boston's Commercial Street, in the crowded North End, burst open. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, which was bound for conversion into industrial alcohol, a trade that the coming of Prohibition would soon make still more urgent. The tank was fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it had been filled to the brim only the day before with a fresh shipment, delivered while the weather was mild enough that the syrup flowed readily. A few hours earlier the temperature had been around two degrees Fahrenheit; by afternoon it stood above forty, and the sudden thaw may have helped to stress a structure that had never been properly tested.

Neighbors had long known that something was wrong with it. The tank leaked so badly that brown streaks ran down its sides and local children collected the drips in tins, and the company's response had been to paint it brown so that the stains would be harder to see, rather than to have an engineer check the work. The steel was too thin for the load, the rivets were poorly set, and nobody had ever filled the tank with water to prove that it would hold.

At about 12:30 the tank gave way with a sound that witnesses compared to a machine gun, as the rivets shot out, and then with a roar as the walls peeled apart. A wave of molasses perhaps twenty-five feet high at its crest, and carrying a weight of many millions of pounds, swept down the street at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It crushed a firehouse, whose men were eating lunch, and knocked a nearby building off its foundation, and it tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports so that a train had to stop short of the gap. Horses, wagons and people were caught and carried along, and a surge of air pushed ahead of the flood and flung some bystanders aside before the syrup reached them.

What made the disaster worse than a simple flood was the nature of the liquid itself. Molasses, cooling quickly in the winter air, thickened as it spread, so that those who were trapped in it could scarcely move their limbs and rescuers sank to their knees as they tried to reach them. Soldiers, sailors from a nearby ship, Red Cross nurses and police officers waded in through the mess, and the injured were treated in a temporary hospital set up close by. Twenty-one people died, and about a hundred and fifty were hurt. Some of the bodies were found only days later, and it took weeks to find them all.

The cleanup went on for months, with crews using salt water from fireboats and sand to scour the streets, and the harbor ran brown until summer. People claimed for years afterward that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled of molasses.

The ensuing lawsuit, one of the first big class actions in Massachusetts, ran for years. The company's lawyers argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court heard hundreds of witnesses and concluded that the structure had simply been too weak. The company was ordered to pay roughly 600,000 dollars in damages, and the case helped bring in stricter rules requiring that engineers review and sign off on building plans.
