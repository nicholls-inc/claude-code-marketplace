On the unseasonably warm midday of January 15, 1919, a storage tank at the Purity Distilling Company on Commercial Street in Boston's North End, holding more than two million gallons of molasses, burst apart. The tank stood fifty feet high and about ninety feet across, and it had been filled only the day before by a shipment that arrived warm from the Caribbean, where the molasses was destined to be fermented into industrial alcohol, much of it for munitions, with Prohibition only days from taking effect. The tank had never been properly tested, and the company's president, Arthur Jell, had left its construction to people with no engineering training; it was painted brown partly so that the leaks, which were obvious to neighbours who collected the syrup that seeped from its seams, would be harder to see.

Witnesses described a deep rumbling and a sound like a machine gun as the rivets popped, and then the steel wall gave way and a wave of molasses perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its front swept outward at something like thirty-five miles an hour, flattening the freight yard, tearing a section of the elevated railway from its supports, and pushing buildings off their foundations. The firehouse nearby was wrecked and some of the firemen inside were trapped in the debris, while a number of people on the street were lifted and carried along before the wave lost speed and spread through the streets in a sticky layer several feet deep. Twenty-one people died, some drowned or crushed and some suffocated, and about 150 were injured, along with a number of horses that had to be shot because they could not be freed. Rescuers, including sailors from the USS Nantucket moored in the harbour, waded in through the thickening mess to reach survivors, and because the cold air soon stiffened the molasses the work was slower and more desperate than anyone had expected, with the last bodies found months afterward, one of them only after the syrup had been pumped away and the harbour had turned brown.

Cleaning up took weeks of work by crews who sprayed the streets with salt water from fireboats and covered the ground with sand and sawdust, and residents of the neighbourhood said for decades afterward that on hot summer days the smell of molasses still rose from the pavement. The lawsuit that followed was among the first big class actions in Massachusetts, and it dragged on for years, during which the United States Industrial Alcohol Company, which owned Purity Distilling, argued that anarchists had blown up the tank. The court-appointed auditor, Hugh Ogden, heard from hundreds of witnesses and expert engineers and concluded that the tank had been too weakly built to hold its contents, so that the company was liable, and it eventually paid out roughly six hundred thousand dollars to families and others, a sum that worked out to a few thousand dollars for each death.

The disaster is often remembered as a curiosity, but its consequences were serious and lasting, since the finding against the company helped bring about stricter building codes and a requirement that architects and engineers sign off on structural plans, so that the flood is now counted among the events that made public safety regulation what it is.
