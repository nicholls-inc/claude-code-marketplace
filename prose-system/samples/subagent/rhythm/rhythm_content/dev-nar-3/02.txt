On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, in Boston's North End, a storage tank belonging to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, burst open and released more than two million gallons of molasses into the streets. The tank stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet across beside Commercial Street, close to the harbor, and it had been built quickly in 1915 to hold molasses that would be fermented into industrial alcohol, a business that had grown with the demand for munitions during the Great War. The construction was careless, since the steel was thinner than the load required, no one had properly tested the tank by filling it with water, and the seams leaked so badly that the company painted the outside brown to hide the stains, while neighborhood children came with cans to collect the syrup that oozed from the walls. Workers and residents had noticed groaning sounds and the occasional shudder, yet complaints were brushed aside, and the tank had been filled the day before with a fresh shipment from the Caribbean that was warmer than the January air and may have fermented and added pressure from within.

Shortly after noon the temperature had risen to the mid-forties after several days of bitter cold, and at about 12:30 the tank gave way with a sound that witnesses compared to a machine gun and then to a rumbling train. A wave of molasses perhaps fifteen feet high at its crest, though accounts vary, surged outward at something like thirty-five miles an hour, flattening a section of the elevated railway, shoving a firehouse off its foundation, and crushing buildings that stood in its path. Men, women and children were swept up, and a number of horses were caught as well, so that rescuers arriving within minutes found a sticky brown lake several feet deep covering the area, in which the victims could scarcely move and from which they could be pulled only with great labor. Soldiers and sailors from a nearby ship and from the Boston Navy Yard joined police, Red Cross nurses and boys from a local training ship in the effort, and salvage went on for days, with the last body recovered months later. Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured.

Cleanup took weeks, because the molasses stiffened as the temperature dropped and clung to everything it touched, and crews spread sand and sawdust and pumped salt water from the harbor to wash it away, leaving the harbor brown into the summer. In the years afterward, the families of the dead sued in one of the first major class action cases in Massachusetts, and an auditor appointed to hear the evidence spent months on engineering testimony before concluding that the tank had not been strong enough, which rejected the company's claim that anarchists had planted a bomb. United States Industrial Alcohol eventually paid out roughly a half million dollars or more in settlements, and the case helped push Massachusetts and other places toward requiring that engineers and architects certify the plans for large structures. Residents of the North End said for decades that on hot summer days the neighborhood still smelled faintly of molasses.
