Imagine that Alice and Bob, who have never met and have no way of arranging a password in advance, want to share a secret while a stranger named Eve sits in the middle of the line copying down every word that passes between them. It sounds impossible, since anything Alice tells Bob, Eve hears too, and for most of history this was taken to be so, because secret communication meant that both sides had to hold the same key beforehand, and carrying that key from one place to the other was the weak point. The idea that dissolved the problem, published in the 1970s, is that some mathematical operations are easy to perform and practically impossible to undo, and that two people can use such an operation to build a shared secret out of pieces that are each harmless to reveal.

The easiest way to see the shape of the idea is with paint rather than numbers. Suppose Alice and Bob agree out loud, in front of Eve, on a common starting colour, say yellow. Alice privately chooses a secret colour, red, and Bob privately chooses his own, blue, and neither ever says what it is. Alice mixes her red into the yellow and sends the resulting orange to Bob, while Bob mixes his blue into the yellow and sends the resulting green to Alice, and Eve can see both jars travel across the line. Now Alice takes the green jar she received and adds her secret red to it, and Bob takes the orange jar he received and adds his secret blue to it, and each ends up with the same muddy brown, since each has combined yellow, red and blue, only in a different order. Eve holds yellow, orange and green, and although she could add more paint to any of these, she has no way to remove paint from a mixture, so she cannot extract the red or the blue and cannot reach the brown. The whole scheme depends on mixing being easy and unmixing being hopeless, and on the fact that the order in which things are mixed does not change the final colour.

Mathematics supplies an operation that behaves like paint, and it needs nothing more than the arithmetic you met at school, with one extra twist borrowed from the way a clock works. On a clock, when you pass twelve you start again from one, so that ten hours after eight o'clock it is six o'clock, because eighteen has wrapped around to leave a remainder of six. Mathematicians call this clock arithmetic and describe it as working modulo a number, here twelve, and in the example that follows we will work modulo 23, which means that after every calculation we divide by 23 and keep only the remainder. The other ingredient is repeated multiplication, which you know as powers, so that 5 to the power 6 means 5 multiplied by itself six times.

Alice and Bob publicly agree on two numbers, which we will take to be 23 and 5, and Eve is welcome to know both. Alice picks a secret number, 6, and Bob picks a secret number, 15, and each keeps their choice entirely to themselves. Alice calculates 5 to the power 6, which is 15,625, divides by 23, and finds that the remainder is 8, and this 8 is her orange jar, which she sends to Bob across the open line. Bob calculates 5 to the power 15, reduces it in the same clock fashion, and arrives at 19, which he sends back to Alice as his green jar. So far everything that has crossed the line is the pair 8 and 19, together with the public 23 and 5.

Now comes the step that corresponds to adding your own secret paint to the other person's mixture. Alice takes Bob's 19 and raises it to her secret power of 6, and after reducing modulo 23 she obtains 2. Bob takes Alice's 8 and raises it to his secret power of 15, and after reducing modulo 23 he also obtains 2. The agreement is no accident, because Alice has in effect computed 5 raised to the power of 15 and then raised to the power of 6, while Bob has computed 5 raised to the power of 6 and then raised to the power of 15, and since multiplying 6 by 15 gives the same answer as multiplying 15 by 6, they have both calculated 5 to the power of 90 in clock arithmetic, merely reaching it by different routes. The shared number 2 is now their secret, which they can use as the key to scramble and unscramble everything else they say to each other, and it never travelled along the line at all.

What stops Eve from doing the same, given that she saw 23, 5, 8 and 19? She would need either Alice's 6 or Bob's 15, and to find Alice's 6 she must ask which power of 5 leaves a remainder of 8 when divided by 23. With numbers this small she can simply try every power in turn and succeed within a minute, which is why the example is a teaching toy and not something to trust with a real secret. The difficulty comes from scale, since real systems use numbers hundreds of digits long, and clock arithmetic with such numbers scrambles the powers so thoroughly that the remainders show no pattern from which to work backwards. Computing the forward direction takes a few hundred multiplications, which a phone does in a blink, whereas trying every possible power would take longer than the age of the universe, and nobody has found a shortcut that makes the backward direction easy, although nobody has proved that none exists either, and the security rests on decades of failed attempts by very capable people.

This is what the phrase public-key cryptography is pointing at. In the scheme above each person ends up with a pair, a private number that never leaves their head, such as 6 or 15, and a public number derived from it, such as 8 or 19, which can be shouted across a crowded room because the derivation works in one direction only. A related family of methods, of which the best known is called RSA after its inventors, uses the same principle for a slightly different job, in which anyone can lock a message using a public key published openly, yet only the owner of the matching private key can unlock it. The lock is made from the product of two enormous prime numbers, and multiplying two primes together is quick whereas recovering them from the product, when each has hundreds of digits, is out of reach of every computer we can build.

In practice, when your browser shows a padlock and connects you to a bank, something very like the paint exchange takes place in a fraction of a second between two machines that have never met, and what they agree on is then used as an ordinary shared key to scramble the rest of the conversation quickly. Eve, listening at the café router or somewhere along the cables, sees only the equivalent of the orange and the green jars, and the secret that the two parties now hold was assembled in a way that neither of them transmitted and that she, for all her patience, cannot reconstruct. A further problem remains, which is that Alice must be sure the jar came from Bob and not from Eve pretending to be him, and real systems solve that with digital signatures and certificates, which lean on the same one-way mathematics, but the central marvel is already visible without them: two strangers can build a secret together in public, because the hard part of the arithmetic is only hard for people who do not hold the private number.
