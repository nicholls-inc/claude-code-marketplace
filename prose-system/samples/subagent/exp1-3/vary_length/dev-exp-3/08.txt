**Sharing a Secret in Public**

Imagine you want to send a friend a secret message, but every letter you post passes through a sorting office full of strangers who might open it. If you and your friend had met beforehand, you could have agreed a code. But you have never met. How do you agree on a secret when everything you say can be overheard?

It sounds impossible. It is not, and the trick is one of the neatest ideas in modern mathematics. It protects your bank card, your messages and nearly everything else you do online.

**The paint-mixing picture**

Start with paint, not numbers. You and your friend, Asha, each pick a private colour. Nobody else sees it. You also agree, out loud, on a shared public colour, say yellow.

You mix your private colour into the yellow. Asha does the same with hers. Now you swap the mixed pots, in full view of everyone. An eavesdropper sees both pots.

Next, you add your private colour to Asha's pot. She adds hers to yours. Both pots now contain yellow plus your colour plus her colour. They match exactly.

The eavesdropper has the yellow and both mixed pots. But unmixing paint is close to impossible. They cannot pull your private colour back out. So they cannot make the final shade. You and Asha share a secret, and it never travelled down the line.

The idea underneath is that some actions are easy to do and very hard to undo. Mixing is easy. Unmixing is not. Cryptographers needed that same one-way property in the language of numbers.

**A one-way street in arithmetic**

School maths already has what we need: clock arithmetic. On a clock with 7 hours, after 6 comes 0, then 1, and so on. Asking "what is 20 on a 7-hour clock?" means dividing by 7 and keeping the remainder. 20 is two full laps (14) plus 6. So the answer is 6. Mathematicians write this as "20 mod 7 = 6".

Now take powers. Suppose the clock has 23 hours and we use the number 5. Raising 5 to a power and then finding where it lands on the clock is easy. Try 5 to the power of 6. That is 15,625. Divide by 23: 23 times 679 is 15,617, leaving 8. So 5^6 lands on 8.

Going backwards is the trouble. Suppose I tell you that 5 raised to some secret power lands on 8, and ask which power I used. With a 23-hour clock you could just try every power in turn. That is a few minutes' work. But real systems use clocks with hundreds of digits in their size. Trying every power would take longer than the age of the universe. The forward step takes a computer a blink. The backward step is a brick wall.

This is our paint mixing. Raising to a power is mixing. Finding the power again is unmixing.

**The exchange, step by step**

Here it is with small numbers, so you can follow it on a calculator. In real life the numbers are enormous.

You and Asha publicly agree on two numbers: a clock size of 23, and a base of 5. Anyone can hear these.

You secretly pick 6. Asha secretly picks 15. Neither is ever said aloud.

You compute 5^6 on the 23-hour clock. We found that is 8. You send "8" to Asha.

Asha computes 5^15 on the same clock. That is 30,517,578,125. Dividing by 23 and keeping the remainder gives 19. She sends "19" to you.

Now the eavesdropper knows 23, 5, 8 and 19. Your secret 6 and Asha's 15 stay hidden.

You take Asha's 19 and raise it to your secret power, 6. You work it on the clock and get 2.

Asha takes your 8 and raises it to her secret power, 15, again on the clock. She also gets 2.

Both of you land on 2. Why? Because both calculations amount to the same thing: 5 raised to the power of 6 times 15, which is 90. The order of multiplication does not matter. You came at it from different directions and arrived at the same door. The eavesdropper, who lacks 6 and 15, cannot get there. With a tiny clock like ours they could guess, but with a clock hundreds of digits wide, there is no realistic way.

The number 2 is now your shared secret. You can use it as the key to lock your later messages with an ordinary scrambling method, one that is fast and that both of you can run.

**The padlock version**

There is a second picture worth knowing, because it is closer to how websites really work. This is the idea of a public key and a private key.

Think of a padlock. Asha makes a thousand identical open padlocks and leaves them in a public place for anyone to take. She keeps the only key. This is her "public key" (the open padlocks) and her "private key" (the one key).

You want to send her something private. You take an open padlock, put your message in a box, and snap the padlock shut. Snapping a padlock needs no key. Now the box can travel through the sorting office. The strangers can see the box and the lock. They cannot open it. Only Asha's private key will do that.

In practice the "padlock" is a pair of numbers linked by maths. One half scrambles a message and cannot easily be used to unscramble it. The other half reverses the scrambling. It works because of another one-way street: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is quick, but taking the result apart again into those two primes is punishingly slow. A prime is a number like 7 or 13 that divides only by 1 and itself. Multiply 7 by 13 and you get 91, easy. Start from 91 and find the pair: doable here, but try it with a number 600 digits long and even the fastest computers give up.

**Why listeners cannot cheat**

Everything depends on two promises. First, the steps you do are easy: a computer can raise a number to a power on a huge clock in a fraction of a second. Second, the steps the eavesdropper would need are hard, with no known shortcut. Nobody has proved that no shortcut exists. But the best mathematicians in the world have tried for decades and failed, and that is the foundation of trust.

There is one weak point worth naming. Suppose someone sits in the middle and pretends to be Asha when talking to you, and pretends to be you when talking to Asha. You would each agree a secret with the impostor, not with each other. Public-key systems guard against this with certificates, which are like passports. A trusted body vouches that a public key really belongs to the person who claims it.

**What it means for you**

Every time your browser shows a padlock symbol, a version of this dance has just happened. Your device and the website agreed a secret key, while anyone on the café Wi-Fi could listen to every word. The key was never sent. It was built, separately, at each end.

The lesson is a deep one. Secrecy does not require that you hide everything. You can say almost all of it in public, provided the one part you hold back is easy for you to use and hopeless for anyone else to recover. Two strangers, a public line, a little clock arithmetic, and a secret that exists only at the two ends.
