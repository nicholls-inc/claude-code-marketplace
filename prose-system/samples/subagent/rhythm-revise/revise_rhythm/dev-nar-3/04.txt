On a mild January afternoon in 1919, the North End of Boston was going about its business: it was just after noon on the fifteenth, unseasonably warm after a bitter cold snap, and children were playing near the waterfront while workers ate lunch and horses pulled carts along Commercial Street. Looming over the neighborhood stood a huge steel tank, fifty feet tall and nearly ninety feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company, which held more than two million gallons of molasses, shipped up from the Caribbean to be fermented into industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions as the First World War had only recently ended.

The tank had been built hastily in 1915 and had never been right, leaking so badly that neighbors collected the syrup in jugs and pails. Instead of fixing the seams properly, the company reportedly painted the tank brown to hide the stains, and the steel was thinner than it should have been, with none of the load testing that might have exposed the flaw. Residents complained for years, but nothing changed.

At about 12:30, a deep rumble was heard, followed by a sound people later compared to a machine gun as the rivets began to shear. Then the tank tore open. A wave of molasses perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high surged out and raced through the streets at an estimated thirty-five miles an hour, heavy, thick and impossible to outrun, crushing buildings, lifting a truck and tossing it aside, and wrenching a section of the elevated railway off its supports. Houses were knocked from their foundations, and a firehouse was swept off its footings, trapping the men inside in the wreckage.

Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured, some drowned in the syrup and others crushed by debris, while rescuers struggled through knee-deep goo that clung to everything and slowed every step. Within days the cold returned and the molasses thickened, making the search for survivors even harder, and many horses died as well. Cleanup took weeks, with crews spraying salt water and spreading sand, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer, while locals said the neighborhood smelled sweet on hot days for decades afterward.

What followed mattered as much as the disaster itself. The families of victims sued, and the case became one of the first large class-action suits in Massachusetts, in which United States Industrial Alcohol, which had taken over Purity Distilling, argued that anarchists had bombed the tank. After years of testimony, an auditor appointed by the court concluded that the structure had simply been unsound, and the company paid roughly six hundred thousand dollars in settlements. The ruling helped push states to require that engineers sign off on building plans, and it shaped the safety standards that govern large structures today.
