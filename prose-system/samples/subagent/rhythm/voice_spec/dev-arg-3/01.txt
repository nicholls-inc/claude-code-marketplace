Primary schools in England teach children to join their letters, and the national curriculum for English, introduced in 2014, says when. In Year 2, pupils begin using the diagonal and horizontal strokes that link one letter to the next. By Years 3 and 4 they are expected to know which letters are best left unjoined. By Years 5 and 6 they are asked to write legibly, fluently and with increasing speed. Some people now regard this as a relic. Children use tablets and keyboards from an early age, they say, and the time spent on looped letters could go to something else. I think schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting. My case rests on writing fluency, on exams, on what the research does and does not show, and on the modest cost of the lessons.

The argument against has some force, so I will begin with it. Finland changed its national core curriculum in 2016 so that cursive was no longer compulsory, and its schools put more weight on keyboard skills. In the United States, the Common Core standards of 2010 left cursive out, and many states let it lapse, though a number have since restored it by law. Nobody disputes that most adult writing is now typed. A child leaving primary school today will send far more messages from a keyboard than with a pen. If the only question were which skill will be used more often in thirty years, the keyboard would win.

That is not the only question. A primary school does not teach only what adults do most often. It also teaches the things that make other learning possible, and handwriting is one of them. A child who has to think about how to form each letter has less attention left for what to say. Researchers such as Steve Graham and Virginia Berninger have found over several decades that children with fluent handwriting tend to write longer and better-organised texts, and that teaching handwriting directly improves the quality of their composition. The mechanism is easy to follow. Writing by hand is a motor task, and a task that has become automatic frees working memory for spelling, grammar and ideas. Joining letters is the usual way schools get children to that point, because a joined script lets the pen stay on the page for the length of a word instead of lifting after every letter.

I should be clear that the evidence for joining specifically is weaker than the evidence for handwriting in general. Studies that compare print with cursive have not found a large and consistent gap in speed or legibility. Some fast writers print and some slow ones join. A fair summary is that fluent handwriting matters, and that a joined style is one good route to it, though not the only one. Many English schools teach a print-based style with short exit strokes, which lets children link most letters with little change to the shapes they learned first. That still counts as joined-up writing, and I think it is a sensible way to teach it. What I oppose is giving up the aim that children should move from separate letters to a connected and quick hand.

Exams are the most practical reason. In England, GCSE and A level papers are written by hand, and a candidate sitting a full set of GCSEs will spend many hours writing across a few weeks in the summer. A small number of pupils are allowed a laptop because of a recognised need, but most are not. A sixteen-year-old who writes slowly loses marks when answers are left unfinished, and one whose script is hard to read loses them because an examiner cannot credit a word they cannot make out. The habit that serves a pupil at sixteen is formed in primary school. If a fast, legible hand is not taught at seven, there is little reason to expect it at fourteen, since teenagers seldom take up a new way of writing without being asked to. In my view this alone is enough to justify the lessons, as long as the exam system stays as it is.

A third reason concerns what happens in the head, and here I want to be careful. In 2014, Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer reported that students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those who typed. Later attempts to repeat the result have found a smaller effect or none, so I do not put much weight on it. Other work is more modest and more reliable. In 2012, Karin James and Laura Engelhardt at Indiana University scanned the brains of children aged four and five who were not yet readers. Those who had practised letters by hand showed more activity in regions linked to reading than those who had typed or traced them. A Norwegian study published in 2024 recorded the brain activity of 36 university students and found more widespread connectivity when they wrote by hand than when they typed. Neither study compares joined script with print, and neither proves that handwriting causes better learning. Together they suggest that the movement of forming letters does something that pressing keys does not. I take that as a reason for caution before removing handwriting from the early years, not as proof.

Reading is a fourth reason. Most adults who write by hand use some form of joined script, so a child taught only print will still meet it in teachers' comments, family letters, old recipes and archive documents. Learning to write a script is the most dependable way to learn to read it. A signature is usually joined as well, and children will be asked to produce one for most of their adult lives, on bank forms, tenancy agreements and delivery slips. These are small matters, but they are the sort of small matters that a person without a settled hand finds awkward and a person with one does not notice.

The cost is low. As I understand it, schools usually teach handwriting in a few short sessions a week, often ten to fifteen minutes each, which is a small share of a timetable dominated by reading, writing, mathematics and a wide set of other subjects. Keyboard skills can be taught alongside it. Children are generally ready for touch typing a few years later than they are ready for joining letters, around the age of eight or nine, so the two do not compete for the same year. The choice presented in most debates, between a pen and a keyboard, is not the choice schools face. They can do both, and the evidence I have described points toward doing both.

Some children find joining hard. Those with motor coordination difficulties may struggle with a continuous script, and for them a laptop or a simpler print style is the right answer. Teachers should be free to make that call early and without stigma. Some teachers also report that a joined hand helps children with dyslexia, because the continuous movement reduces the reversal of letters such as b and d. The research on this is thin and I would not build a policy on it. But the existence of children for whom joined writing is the wrong tool is a reason to teach it flexibly, and does not show that it should be dropped for everyone.

The strongest form of the opposing view is that time is limited and that every hour spent on handwriting is an hour not spent on something with a clearer payoff. I accept the premise and reach a different conclusion. Handwriting is among the few early skills that lets a child get thoughts onto paper at the speed they have them, in every subject, without a device, and in every exam hall. It takes a few years to learn and a few minutes a day to maintain. It is also a skill that adults in most professions use constantly, for notes, forms, labels and lists, even if they rarely write a full page. Dropping it would save a small amount of time and would take away something that a child cannot easily teach themselves later.

My position, then, is that primary schools should keep teaching children to join their letters, in whichever style gets most of them writing quickly and clearly by the age of eleven, and should teach typing as well. The evidence does not show that cursive is magic, and I do not claim it. It shows that fluent handwriting helps children write better, that exams still reward it, and that the cost of teaching it is small. Those three facts are enough for me.
