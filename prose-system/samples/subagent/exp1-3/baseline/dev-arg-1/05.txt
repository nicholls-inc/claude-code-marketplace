**Scrap the Parking Mandate**

Every city has a rule that most residents have never read and that shapes the streets they live on: the minimum parking requirement. It says that if you want to build an apartment, a cafe, or a dentist's office, you must also build a set number of off-street parking spaces. Cities should repeal these rules. Not tweak them, not lower them, but remove them and let builders and tenants decide how much parking a building needs.

Start with what the requirements are. They are not the product of careful study of how many cars a neighbourhood needs. The numbers trace back largely to traffic engineering manuals that tallied peak parking demand at suburban sites with free parking, then turned those observations into law. A restaurant must have one space per 100 square feet of dining room because someone measured a few suburban restaurants whose lots were empty most of the year. Planners then copied the figures from city to city. The standards pretend to precision they do not have.

The first problem is cost. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count aisles and ramps, and a space in a structured garage can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build; underground spaces cost more. That cost does not vanish. It lands in rents and prices. A requirement of one or two spaces per apartment makes every unit more expensive whether or not the tenant owns a car. Households without cars, who tend to be poorer, subsidise the storage of everyone else's vehicles. A rule sold as a public convenience works as a tax on housing, and a regressive one.

The second problem is that the rules decide what gets built at all. On a small downtown lot, a parking mandate can make a project arithmetically impossible, because the spaces eat the buildable area. Small infill apartments, corner shops, and conversions of old buildings are the first casualties. Historic main streets, built before the mandates, would often be illegal to build today. Cities then wonder why new construction looks like a large box ringed by asphalt, and why older neighbourhoods feel more alive.

The third problem is land. Parking mandates turn valuable urban ground into storage for empty cars. Cities spend decades and large sums trying to make places walkable, then require every new building to be set back behind a lot. The more space a city devotes to parking, the farther apart destinations become, and the more necessary a car becomes. The mandate creates the demand it claims to serve.

The standard objection is that without requirements, new buildings will flood the street with parked cars and make life miserable for neighbours. This worry deserves a real answer, and the answer is that the problem is street pricing, not building rules. If curb parking is free, any new demand will spill onto it. But the fix is to manage the curb: permit programmes, metering, and pricing that keeps a few spaces open on every block. Forcing each developer to build spaces, whatever the local conditions, is a blunt substitute for governing a public resource directly.

The second objection is that developers will build too little parking and buyers will suffer. Yet developers and lenders have strong reasons to supply parking where people want it. In a suburban neighbourhood with no transit, a building without parking will struggle to rent, and no lender wants that. Removing the mandate does not forbid parking; it lets the market choose a number. Evidence from cities that have done this is encouraging. Buffalo eliminated its citywide requirements in 2017, and Minneapolis, Austin, and others followed in various forms. Developers there still build plenty of parking where it makes sense, but they also build projects that would not have been feasible before, and the sky has not fallen. Many places also discovered that the old requirements were being waived or reduced case by case anyway, which suggests the numbers were never sacred.

A third objection concerns people who cannot easily do without a car: older residents, people with disabilities, workers on night shifts. These concerns are real, and they argue for accessible parking standards and good transit, not for a blanket mandate that raises the cost of housing for all. Removing minimums does not remove accessible spaces from buildings where tenants need them. It lets the building provide them deliberately instead of by formula.

Climate and public health point the same way. Plentiful, free, mandated parking is a standing subsidy for driving. Taking it away makes other choices, such as walking, cycling, and transit, more competitive without banning anything. A city that wants lower emissions and safer streets should stop requiring the infrastructure of car dependence.

None of this means cars disappear. Plenty of people will keep driving, and plenty of buildings will include garages. The point is that the number should come from the people who pay for it, not from a table written decades ago. Cities that want more housing, more affordable housing, and better streets already have a clear step available. It costs the public treasury nothing. It simply requires lawmakers to delete a few lines from the zoning code and trust that builders, tenants, and neighbourhoods can work out how much parking they need.
