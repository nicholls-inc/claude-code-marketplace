The second that will no longer be added

Every few years, the world's official clocks have paused for a single second. The extra tick, known as a leap second, is slipped in at the end of June or December, so that the last minute of the day lasts 61 seconds instead of 60. Most people never notice. The world's computers do, and that is why the practice is being retired.

The leap second exists because we keep time in two ways that do not quite agree. The older way is the turning of the Earth: one rotation, one day. The newer way is the atomic clock, which counts the vibrations of caesium atoms and loses or gains less than a second over millions of years. The Earth is a far less reliable timekeeper. The pull of the Moon's tides is gradually slowing its spin, and earthquakes, melting ice and movements deep in the planet's core speed it up or slow it down unpredictably.

Since 1972, when the current system began, timekeepers have held the two in step by adding a leap second whenever atomic time drifted nearly a second ahead of the Earth's rotation. They have done so 27 times, most recently at the end of 2016.

The trouble is that the adjustments cannot be scheduled far ahead. Because the Earth's rotation is irregular, each one is announced only about six months in advance, and software that assumes every minute has 60 seconds must be made to cope. It often has not. A leap second in 2012 knocked out websites including Reddit and disrupted airline check-in systems in Australia; another in 2017 caused failures at the internet company Cloudflare. Technology firms have invented their own workarounds, such as "smearing" the extra second across many hours, which means that different systems briefly disagree about what time it is. Satellite navigation systems ignore leap seconds altogether.

A newer worry sharpened the argument. The Earth has lately been spinning slightly faster, raising the prospect of a negative leap second, in which a second would be removed. That has never been tried, and engineers fear it would expose untested software.

In November 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the body of member governments that oversees the world's measurement standards, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. Atomic time and Earth time will be allowed to drift apart by more than a second. How large a gap to tolerate, and how eventually to correct it, is still to be settled, but the difference is expected to reach only about a minute over a century, far too small to shift sunrise in anyone's lifetime.
