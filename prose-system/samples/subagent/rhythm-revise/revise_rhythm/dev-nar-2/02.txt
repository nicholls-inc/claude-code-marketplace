On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise on what was to be the third mission to land men on the Moon. Lovell, the commander, was a veteran of four spaceflights who had already circled the Moon once, on Apollo 8, while Haise was a rookie who would pilot the lunar module. Swigert was a late addition: the original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been grounded days before launch after exposure to German measles, and Swigert, the backup, took his place. By this point Apollo had begun to feel routine to the public, which had watched Americans land on the Moon twice and whose television networks had stopped carrying the crews' broadcasts live.

The first two days passed without trouble, apart from one oddity at launch, when the center engine of the Saturn V's second stage shut down early but the other engines burned longer to compensate and the spacecraft reached its planned trajectory. The crew settled into the coast toward the Moon, flying the combined craft: the conical command module, named Odyssey, attached to its cylindrical service module, with the spidery lunar module, Aquarius, tucked behind them.

On the evening of April 13, about 200,000 miles from Earth, mission control in Houston asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks in the service module, a routine request, since stirring the supercooled oxygen kept it from stratifying and made the quantity gauges accurate. Swigert flipped the switch, and roughly ninety seconds later, at 9:07 p.m. Houston time, the crew heard a loud bang and felt the spacecraft shudder. Swigert reported the now-famous words, "Houston, we've had a problem," and Lovell repeated them a moment later.

The cause lay in a chain of earlier mistakes. The oxygen tank had been dropped slightly during handling at the factory, damaging an internal fill tube, and during a pre-launch test, when technicians had trouble draining the tank, they used heaters to boil off the oxygen at a length ordinarily forbidden. The thermostat switches, designed for the spacecraft's 28-volt supply, were never upgraded when the ground power was raised to 65 volts, and they fused shut. Temperatures inside the tank soared and the Teflon insulation on the wiring was damaged, so that when Swigert turned on the stirring fans, a spark ignited the exposed wires and the tank ruptured, blowing off a panel of the service module and crippling a second oxygen tank.

In the cabin, the crew did not at first understand what had happened, as warning lights flickered, the main electrical buses dropped in voltage, and the thrusters fired on their own. Then Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas venting into space, and it was oxygen, the same oxygen that supplied both their breathable air and the fuel cells that made their electricity. Within about three hours the command module was dying, and the landing on the Moon was abandoned almost at once. The question had become whether the three men could get home at all.

The answer was to use the lunar module as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, power and water, designed to sustain two men for two days on the lunar surface, and now it had to keep three alive for roughly four. The crew powered up the lunar module and shut down nearly everything in Odyssey to save its batteries for the final descent, which could not be skipped. Because the spacecraft was already on a path that would loop around the Moon, flight director Gene Kranz and his team chose not to attempt an immediate abort; instead they used the lunar module's descent engine to burn the spacecraft onto a faster return path, and later to speed it further, so that it would reach Earth sooner and splash down in the Pacific.

The hours that followed were hard in ways the public heard about only in part. The lunar module was never meant to carry that load, and as its systems ran, the cabin grew cold: temperatures fell to near freezing, water condensed on the walls, and the crew, wearing little more than flight coveralls, struggled to sleep. To save water for cooling the electronics, they cut their own ration to a few ounces a day. Haise developed a kidney infection and a fever, and all three lost weight and grew dehydrated.

A more urgent threat was the air. Carbon dioxide exhaled by three men began to build up in the lunar module, whose lithium hydroxide canisters were sized for two, and although the command module had spare canisters, they were square and the lunar module's receptacles were round. On the ground, engineers gathered the same items the crew had on board, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a sock and duct tape, and worked out a way to join them. They read the instructions up to the crew, who built the adapter themselves, and within hours the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

Navigation brought its own difficulty. The spacecraft's course had drifted, and a correction was needed to hit the narrow corridor for atmospheric entry, but with the guidance system shut down to save power and with debris and venting gas obscuring the stars, Lovell had to align the craft by sighting the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, in his window. A burn timed by hand, with only a watch to mark its length, set them on course.

Meanwhile, back on Earth, the question of how to restart the command module took on its own urgency, because it had been shut down and left to cold-soak for days and no one was sure it would come back. Mattingly, the grounded astronaut, spent long hours in a simulator working out a power-up sequence that would draw as little current as possible, and engineers wrote it, tested it, and read it up to the crew.

On April 17, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module and, for the first time, saw how much of it had been torn away: an entire side panel was gone, and the exposed interior was charred. Then they said goodbye to Aquarius, which had saved them, and released it to burn up in the atmosphere, after which Odyssey came back to life on its limited power and the capsule began its descent.

Reentry brought a final, tense wait. The blackout, when the heat of reentry blocks radio contact, normally lasts about three minutes, but this time it lasted longer, and Houston waited in silence. Then, at last, Swigert's voice came through and parachutes appeared on the television screens, and Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific on April 17, within sight of the recovery ship, the USS Iwo Jima, after the crew had been away for nearly six days.

Apollo 13 was a failure in its goal and a triumph in its outcome. A review board traced the accident to the tank and its history, and NASA redesigned the oxygen system, removing the vulnerable heaters and fans, so that the flights that followed went on to land on the Moon. But the rescue of Apollo 13 endures for a different reason: it showed what a team of people could do when the plan failed, working with the tools at hand, under pressure, to bring three men safely home.
