On January 15, 1919, a mild winter day in Boston's North End, a disaster arrived without warning and moved faster than anyone could outrun it.

Looming over the neighborhood near Commercial Street stood a steel tank fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup, shipped up from the Caribbean, was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a key ingredient in munitions. The tank had been built hastily in 1915 and never properly tested. It leaked so badly that residents collected the brown ooze in cups, and the company's response was to paint the tank brown to hide the stains.

The day before, a fresh shipment had been pumped in, and the temperature had risen sharply from the bitter cold of the previous days. Around 12:30 in the afternoon, with the neighborhood at lunch, workers and children and horse teams filled the streets. Then came a low rumble. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the popping of rivets shearing loose, and then a groan as the tank's walls gave way.

A wave of molasses, estimated at fifteen to twenty-five feet high at its crest, burst outward at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a tremendous weight, over twelve thousand tons, and it hit with the force of a collapsing building. It crushed the nearby streets, tore a section of the elevated railway from its supports, and knocked buildings off their foundations. A firehouse was swept aside, and one of its firefighters was killed. Horses, wagons, and people were caught in the flood.

Those swallowed by it found that molasses was nearly impossible to escape. Thick and sticky, it slowed every movement, and as the cold air cooled it, it thickened into something close to glue. Rescuers waded in knee-deep, and the injured lay trapped where they fell. Police, firefighters, soldiers, sailors from a docked ship, and Red Cross workers all hurried to help, but the work was agonizing. Many victims were not found for days.

When it was over, twenty-one people were dead and about 150 were injured. The youngest victims were children. For weeks the harbor ran brown, and the smell of molasses lingered in the neighborhood for years. Cleanup crews used salt water from fire boats, and sand and sawdust, to scrape the sticky mess from streets and buildings.

The aftermath proved as important as the event. Survivors and families sued, and in one of the first major class action cases in Massachusetts, the company insisted that anarchists had bombed its tank. A court-appointed auditor, after years of testimony and hundreds of witnesses, rejected that claim. He found the tank had been structurally unsound, and the company was held responsible. It paid roughly 600,000 dollars in settlements.

The disaster helped change how America builds. Massachusetts and other states strengthened rules requiring that engineers and architects sign off on plans, and that structures be properly inspected. The flood of 1919 remains a strange but sobering lesson in what happens when profit outruns safety.
