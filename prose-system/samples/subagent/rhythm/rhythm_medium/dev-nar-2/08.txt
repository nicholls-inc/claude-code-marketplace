On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted Apollo 13 from Kennedy Space Center in Florida, carrying three men toward what was meant to be the third landing on the Moon. The commander was Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier spaceflights who had already circled the Moon once. Fred Haise was the lunar module pilot and a first-time flyer, and Jack Swigert was the command module pilot. Swigert was a late substitute, because only days earlier doctors had worried that the original pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been exposed to German measles. By then Americans had grown comfortable with lunar missions, and the launch drew little of the attention that had greeted Apollo 11. The first two days passed so smoothly that the crew joked about how little there was to do.

The trouble had been built into the spacecraft years before. An oxygen tank in the service module, the cylindrical section behind the cone-shaped command module, had been damaged during testing on the ground. Later, when technicians drained it, the heaters inside ran far longer than intended, and the thermostats meant to protect them had been fitted for a lower voltage than the ground equipment supplied. The heat stripped some of the Teflon insulation from the wiring inside the tank. Nobody noticed, and the tank was installed and flown with bare wire waiting inside it.

On the evening of April 13, about fifty-six hours into the flight and roughly two hundred thousand miles from Earth, Mission Control in Houston asked the crew to stir the oxygen tanks, a routine step to keep the supercold contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A short circuit sparked in the damaged wiring, the oxygen caught fire, and the pressure inside the tank climbed until it burst. The blast tore a panel off the side of the service module and wrecked the plumbing around it. Aboard the spacecraft the crew heard a bang and felt a shudder. Swigert reported, a little casually, that they had a problem, and Lovell repeated it for the record. In Houston the flight controllers watched pressure readings fall and could not make sense of them at first.

The picture grew worse by the minute. The second oxygen tank began leaking as well, and the fuel cells that depended on that oxygen to make electricity started to die. Those fuel cells also supplied the crew's drinking water, so the loss threatened power, air and water together. Within a couple of hours the command module, Odyssey, was running on its reentry batteries, and those had to be saved for the end of the trip. The landing on the Moon was out of the question. The only goal now was to get the three men home alive, and the flight director, Gene Kranz, told his team that failure was not an option they would entertain.

The answer lay in the lunar module, Aquarius, a fragile craft built to carry two men to the surface and back for a stay of two days. Its batteries, oxygen and water were still intact, and the crew crowded into it, using it as a lifeboat. The plan demanded that Aquarius support three people for about four days, far beyond its design, and that it supply the propulsion for course changes as well. The spacecraft was also on a path that would swing around the Moon and miss the Earth by hundreds of miles, so the first task was to correct it.

Controllers chose to fire the lunar module's descent engine after the craft had swung around the far side of the Moon, which both sped the return and aimed it at the Pacific Ocean. As they passed behind the Moon the crew saw the surface slide beneath them, a sight they had trained to explore and now barely had time to look at. They reached a distance of nearly two hundred and fifty thousand miles from Earth, farther than any human beings have traveled before or since. Then came the long burn that put them on course for home, and after it the work of conserving everything they had.

Conservation meant living in discomfort. Almost everything electrical was shut down in the lunar module to stretch the batteries, and the heat went with it. Temperatures dropped to around forty degrees Fahrenheit, water condensed on the walls, and the men slept badly if at all. Water was rationed to a few ounces a day each, and Haise developed a fever and a kidney infection. Lovell later said he lost about fourteen pounds. They wore their flight suits and tried to rest, while the ground worked out each next step.

The most famous problem came from the air itself. Carbon dioxide from three people's breath was building up faster than the lunar module's canisters of lithium hydroxide could absorb it, and the spare canisters in the command module were the wrong shape for the lunar module's system. Engineers in Houston gathered what the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, hoses from the spacesuits and duct tape, and built an adapter on the ground. They read the instructions up to the astronauts, who copied the device piece by piece. When they fitted it, the carbon dioxide readings dropped, and a crisis that could have ended the mission quietly was resolved.

Meanwhile the spacecraft drifted slightly off course, and a further correction was needed. With the guidance system shut down to save power, the crew had to aim by hand, using the Earth itself as a reference and timing the burn with a watch. Lovell held the craft steady while Haise and Swigert managed the controls, and the short burn worked well enough to keep them on target.

The hardest question left was how to wake Odyssey. Nobody had ever planned to restart a command module that had been left cold and dead for days, and the electrical sequence needed to bring it up on limited battery power had to be invented from scratch. Mattingly, the man who had been grounded, worked in a simulator with engineers for long hours and devised a procedure that would keep within the power budget. The crew then had to follow it exactly, and it worked.

On the final morning the crew separated from the service module, and for the first time they saw the damage: a whole side panel gone and wreckage trailing from the hull. They photographed it, which gave investigators vital evidence, and then released the lunar module that had saved them. Aquarius fell behind, and the men moved into Odyssey, which still held enough power for reentry.

Reentry brought one more silence. Normally radio contact breaks for about three minutes while the heat of the atmosphere surrounds the capsule, and this time the pause stretched past that, because of the shallower angle of their path. In Houston and around the world, people waited and watched the clock, and nobody could say what the capsule had survived. Then the parachutes opened, and the voices of the crew returned over the radio. Odyssey splashed down in the Pacific near Samoa on April 17, close to the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima, almost six days after the launch.

The accident was investigated, the tank design was changed, and the later Apollo missions flew on. Apollo 13 never reached the Moon, but many people consider it one of NASA's finest hours, because ordinary equipment, improvised repairs and a great deal of careful work brought three men back from a place no rescue could reach.
