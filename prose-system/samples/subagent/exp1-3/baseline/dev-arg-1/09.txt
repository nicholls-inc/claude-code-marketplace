**Scrap the Parking Mandate**

Every city that requires a minimum number of parking spaces for new buildings is running a quiet experiment in central planning. A bureaucrat decides, in advance, how many cars a restaurant, apartment block or dental office will attract, and the builder must pour enough concrete to match. The guesses are usually wrong, and the costs are real. Cities should remove minimum parking requirements entirely.

Start with how the numbers get made. Most requirements trace back to the Institute of Transportation Engineers' Parking Generation manual, which compiles observed peak demand at suburban sites, often with free parking, no transit and few pedestrians. Planners then copy a neighbouring town's ratio, round up, and call it a standard. The result is codes that demand, say, one space per 200 square feet of restaurant or two per apartment, regardless of whether the tenants own cars. Donald Shoup, the UCLA scholar whose book The High Cost of Free Parking made this case two decades ago, pointed out that these ratios are rarely tested against how the spaces get used. Surveys of lots serving shopping centres routinely find them mostly empty even on busy days, because the code is tuned to the single busiest hour of the year.

Meanwhile the price is paid by everyone, including people who never drive. A surface space costs thousands of dollars to build; a structured or underground one can run to tens of thousands, and in expensive cities far more. That cost does not vanish. It is folded into rents, home prices and menu prices. A household without a car still pays for the spaces it is forced to subsidise. Requirements function as a regressive tax on housing, falling hardest on the poor, who own fewer cars and are least able to absorb the extra cost.

They also kill buildings before they exist. On a small urban lot, the parking math can make a project impossible: the ramp eats the ground floor, or the required spaces leave no room for the units that would pay for the project. Old main streets are full of beloved narrow buildings that could not legally be built today. Small businesses suffer most, since converting a vacant shopfront into a cafe can trigger a parking requirement the owner cannot meet, leaving the storefront empty. Mandates also lock land into low-value use. Acres of asphalt generate almost no tax revenue per acre compared with the mixed-use blocks they replace, which is a poor bargain for municipal budgets.

The environmental and safety arguments cut the same way. Guaranteed free parking is a subsidy to driving, and subsidies get more of whatever they pay for. Spreading destinations apart to make room for lots makes walking and transit impractical, which in turn makes more parking seem necessary. Breaking that loop does not require banning cars. It only requires ending the compulsion.

The strongest objection is spillover: without mandates, new buildings will clog neighbourhood streets with parked cars. But removing a requirement is not the same as banning parking. Developers will still build it where customers want it, because a building with no parking and a market that demands it will not lease. Lenders and tenants are not fools. What changes is that the amount becomes a business decision based on actual demand, rather than a number in an ordinance. Where street parking is genuinely scarce, the answer is to price it. Permit programmes, metered curbs and performance pricing that keeps a few spaces open on every block address the real problem, which is unpriced curb space, far more precisely than forcing every private lot to oversupply.

Evidence is accumulating that cities can handle this. Buffalo became the first large American city to eliminate its minimums citywide in 2017. Minneapolis, Anchorage and others followed with citywide or near-citywide reforms. Early reviews have found developers continuing to build parking when it makes sense, while also delivering projects that the old rules would have blocked, and no collapse in neighbourhood liveability. Several state legislatures, including California, have now curtailed mandates near transit. Countries from the United Kingdom to Switzerland have used maximums or flexibility for years without chaos.

A fair compromise exists for the nervous: keep accessible spaces required for people with disabilities, which serve a distinct civil-rights purpose, and let everything else float. Cities can also pair repeal with curb management so that residents see the benefit rather than the burden.

The deeper point is about humility. Nobody, least of all a zoning official, knows how many parking spaces a given building needs. Property owners, tenants and lenders have the information and bear the consequences, so they should make the call. A planner's job is to protect the public realm, not to guarantee that every trip can end in a free space. Repeal the minimums, price the curb, and let cities be built for people first and cars second.
