On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm day. After a bitter cold snap, the temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit, and the winter air smelled faintly of thaw. Workers ate lunch near the waterfront. Children played in the streets. Horses, wagons, and elevated trains rumbled past a huge steel tank that loomed over Commercial Street.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It stood fifty feet high and ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sweet syrup had arrived by ship from the Caribbean and was destined to be turned into industrial alcohol, a business that was growing fast as Prohibition approached and as the nation's munitions makers wanted more of it. The tank had been built quickly and cheaply. It leaked so badly that neighbors collected the dripping syrup in pails. Instead of fixing the seams, the company painted the tank brown to hide the stains.

A little after half past noon, a sound like a machine gun rang out as rivets shot from the steel. Then the tank ripped open. A wave of molasses, perhaps twenty-five feet tall at its crest, swept out across the neighborhood at around thirty-five miles an hour. It carried a pressure wave of air ahead of it and a crushing weight behind. Buildings were knocked from their foundations. A section of the elevated railway buckled and sagged. A firehouse was shoved aside, and men inside were trapped in the wreckage.

Cold had thickened the syrup, but not enough to stop it. People were caught, lifted, and thrown. Some drowned in a substance that clung to every limb and filled every breath. Rescuers waded in as best they could. Sailors from the nearby USS Nantucket rushed to help, along with police, Red Cross workers, and Boy Scouts. They found that the molasses grew stickier as it cooled, which made reaching the victims slow and dreadful. Horses struggled and died in the mire. Rescue efforts continued for days before the last bodies were found.

In the end, twenty-one people were killed and about one hundred fifty were injured. Cleanup took weeks. Crews sprayed salt water and sand over the streets, and the harbor ran brown well into the summer. For decades, locals insisted that on hot days the North End still smelled of molasses.

The disaster did not end with the cleanup. Families sued the company, and the case became one of the first major class action suits in Massachusetts. The company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, but an auditor appointed by the court examined the evidence and concluded that the structure had been poorly built and never properly tested. The company paid settlements to the victims' families.

The flood also changed how things are built. In its aftermath, Massachusetts tightened its rules for construction, requiring that engineers and architects review and sign off on major plans. What seemed at first like a strange, almost comic event became a lasting lesson in the cost of cutting corners.
