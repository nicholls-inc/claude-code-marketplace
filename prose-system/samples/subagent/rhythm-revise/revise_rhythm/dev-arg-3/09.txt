Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, although the case against it has a seductive simplicity: children will spend their adult lives typing, so the hours spent looping an "e" into an "l" are hours stolen from something more useful. But this argument rests on a thin picture of what handwriting is for, and a thinner picture of what a primary school is doing when it teaches it. Cursive is not a nostalgic ornament clinging to the curriculum. It is a practical skill, a cognitive tool, and an act of equity, and abandoning it would cost children more than the few hours a week it takes to teach.

Begin with the plainest point, which is that people still write by hand, and will go on doing so. Pupils sit examinations with a pen. Adults fill in forms, sign documents, take notes in meetings that do not welcome a laptop, jot shopping lists, write cards, and leave messages for the people they live with. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand is handicapped in all of these, and above all in the exam hall, where the pace of the hand sets a ceiling on how much a candidate can say. Here the join matters, because printed letters require the pen to leave the page after every stroke, and each lift is a small interruption. Joined writing keeps the pen moving, so a fluent writer can often produce more words per minute with less strain. The evidence on this is not uniform, and defenders of cursive sometimes claim more than the research supports, but the practical logic is hard to dismiss. A script designed around continuous movement is simply a faster way to put words on paper once it is learned well.

It is also worth being honest about what the research does and does not show, because the strongest defence of handwriting does not need exaggeration. Studies comparing handwriting with typing in young children have repeatedly found that children who learn letters by writing them recognise and remember them better than those who learn by pressing keys. Brain imaging work has suggested that handwriting engages networks involved in reading that keyboard use does not engage to the same degree. Studies of note-taking in older students suggest that writing by hand pushes the writer to process and compress ideas rather than transcribe them word for word, which tends to help understanding. None of this proves that joined-up script specifically is superior to print. What it shows is that the motor act of forming letters is bound up with learning to read and to think in language. If that is so, the question for a school is not whether to teach handwriting at all, since nobody serious proposes abandoning it, but which form of handwriting repays the effort best. A child who is taught to join letters from the start learns words as shapes with rhythm and direction, not as strings of separate marks, and this fits well with how spelling patterns are learned. The common endings and clusters of English, the "ing" and the "tion" and the "ough", become single movements of the hand, so that muscle memory reinforces orthographic memory.

The second argument is about equity, and it is the one that I find most persuasive. Typing instruction presupposes a keyboard, and keyboards are not evenly distributed. A school that decides handwriting is obsolete is making a bet that every child will have regular access to a device at home and at school, and that the device will work and be used for learning rather than for distraction. In practice, the children least likely to have a quiet desk, a charged laptop and a reliable connection are the same children who most need the school to deliver basic skills reliably. A pencil and paper cost almost nothing and need no updates, passwords or batteries. Fluent handwriting is therefore one of the cheapest and most durable forms of literacy a school can hand a child, and removing it in the name of modernity tends to widen the gap it claims to close. Wealthier families will simply teach their children to write properly anyway, privately, with tutors and workbooks, and the state school pupil will be left with an informal, inconsistent scrawl. We have seen this pattern with other skills that schools stopped valuing, where the knowledge does not disappear but becomes a marker of privilege.

Third, consider the children for whom handwriting is hard. Dyslexia and dyspraxia, and the wide range of motor and perceptual difficulties that make writing laborious, are often cited as reasons to drop cursive, on the grounds that forcing a difficult script on struggling children is cruel. The worry is sincere, but the conclusion does not follow. Many specialists who work with dyslexic children recommend joined writing precisely because it reduces the problem of reversals. When every letter starts on the line and flows from the one before, a "b" and a "d" are harder to confuse, because they are formed by different movements, and the spaces between words are easier to keep consistent because the word is a single connected unit. The right response to children who find handwriting difficult is to teach it well, patiently and with support, and to allow assistive technology where it is genuinely needed, not to withdraw the instruction from the whole cohort. A school can and should offer a keyboard to the child who truly requires one, which is a reasonable adjustment for an individual but not an argument for removing a skill from every child because some will find it hard, any more than the existence of children who struggle with arithmetic is an argument for dropping long division.

A fourth consideration is what handwriting gives a child beyond function. Handwriting is among the earliest forms of craft a child experiences. It requires attention, patience, and the willingness to repeat something until it improves, in a world that offers instant correction and endless undo. A page of careful writing is a small, visible record of effort, and the pride children take in a neat page is real and good for them. Learning to write well by hand is a rare piece of schooling where improvement is directly visible to the learner without any grade, and where the standard is set by the child's own earlier attempts. This is not a trivial thing in an era when so much of a child's day happens on screens designed to hold their attention for its own sake. Slowing down enough to shape a word deliberately is a quiet discipline, and primary school is the right time to build it.

Then there is the matter of reading itself. A great deal of the written record of the last few centuries is in cursive: letters, diaries, wills, registers, annotations in margins, a grandparent's recipe book. A generation that cannot read joined writing will be cut off from its own family papers and from primary sources that historians, genealogists and ordinary curious people rely on. It would be strange to let a whole literacy lapse within a lifetime. We do not need every child to become a calligrapher, but we do need most of them to be able to read what their grandmother wrote and to sign their own name with confidence.

The strongest objection deserves a fair answer. Curriculum time is finite, and every hour on handwriting is an hour not spent on reading, maths, science or typing. This is true, and it is the reason handwriting should be taught efficiently, not the reason to drop it. The sensible model is short, frequent, well-structured practice, beginning with pre-cursive patterns in the early years and moving to joined letters once children have the basic forms secure, with the aim of automaticity by about the age of nine or ten. Done properly, this takes a modest share of the week, and the payoff is a child who can write with speed and legibility without having to think about it, which frees their attention for the content of what they are saying. Poor handwriting taxes working memory: a child who has to concentrate on forming each letter has less mental room left for composing a sentence. Automatic handwriting is the foundation for fluent composition, and the research linking transcription fluency to the quality and length of children's writing is among the more consistent findings in the field. Time invested here is recovered many times over in everything else the child writes.

Nor does keeping cursive mean rejecting keyboards, since the choice is a false one. Children should learn to type, and should be taught to do it properly, with the same seriousness, in the upper years of primary school and beyond. A modern education should produce people who can move easily between pen and keyboard and who know which suits which task. What it should not do is treat the older skill as already dead and let it wither by neglect before the younger one has proved it can replace it. We do not know what the next fifty years of technology will look like, but we do know that a child who can write fluently by hand loses nothing in a digital future, while a child who cannot may lose a good deal if the device is absent, forbidden or broken.

In the end the argument for teaching joined-up handwriting is an argument about robustness. It is cheap, it is fast once learned, it supports reading and spelling, it helps many children who struggle, it gives every pupil the same tool regardless of what is at home, and it connects them to the written past. The argument against it is only that it is old, which is not, by itself, a reason to stop. Primary schools should keep teaching children to join their letters, and teach it well, because the hand that has learned to write fluently is a resource a child will draw on for the rest of their life.
