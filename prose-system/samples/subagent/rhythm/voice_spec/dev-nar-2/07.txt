Apollo 13 was launched from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida on 11 April 1970 at 2:13 pm local time. It was the third mission planned to land men on the Moon. The crew were James Lovell, the commander, Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, and John Swigert, the command module pilot. Swigert had not been the first choice. Ken Mattingly was taken off the crew three days before launch, after doctors learned he had been exposed to German measles and had no immunity to it. Swigert took his place after a short period of training with Lovell and Haise in the simulators. Lovell had flown in space three times before, on Gemini 7, Gemini 12 and Apollo 8, and Apollo 13 made him the first person to fly four missions.

The climb into orbit had one fault. The centre engine of the second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the other four engines burned longer to make up the speed. The spacecraft reached its planned orbit, and a little over two hours into the flight the crew fired the third stage to leave Earth orbit for the Moon. The command and service module, named Odyssey, then separated from the rocket, turned around and docked with the lunar module, named Aquarius, which had been carried in the top of the stage. The joined craft headed for the Fra Mauro highlands, where the crew were to land.

The first two days passed without trouble. On the evening of 13 April, about 56 hours into the flight, Houston asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine task meant to stop the contents from settling. The spacecraft was then about 200,000 miles, or 320,000 kilometres, from Earth. Swigert switched on the stirring fans in oxygen tank 2. A little under two minutes later the crew heard a bang and felt the spacecraft shake. Swigert reported to Houston that they had had a problem, and Lovell repeated the report. Warning lights showed that the main electrical buses were losing voltage, and the crew saw that the pressure in oxygen tank 1 was falling. Lovell looked out of a window and saw gas venting into space.

The cause was traced later to damage done before the flight. During a ground test at Kennedy, the technicians had trouble emptying tank 2, and they ran its heaters for a long time to boil off the oxygen. The heaters were built for a lower voltage than the one used at the launch pad, and the temperature inside the tank rose far above its limit. The Teflon insulation on the wiring inside was damaged. When the fans were switched on in flight, a short circuit ignited the insulation, the pressure in the tank rose and the tank failed. The explosion blew a panel off the side of the service module and damaged the neighbouring tank. Within about three hours both oxygen tanks were empty. Odyssey drew its electricity from three fuel cells that used oxygen, so they began to shut down, and the batteries in the command module were needed for the landing on Earth.

The landing on the Moon was given up at once. Flight controllers and the crew decided to use Aquarius as a lifeboat. It had been designed to support two men for about two days, most of it on the lunar surface, and it now had to support three men for about four days. The crew moved into it and shut down Odyssey to save what remained of its batteries.

A second problem was the course. Earlier in the flight the spacecraft had been moved off a free return path, which would have carried it around the Moon and back toward Earth without any engine burn, so that it could reach Fra Mauro. It was now on a path that would miss Earth. About five hours after the explosion, the crew fired the descent engine of Aquarius for 30 seconds to put the craft back onto a free return. After passing behind the Moon at a height of about 250 kilometres, they fired it again for four minutes and 23 seconds to shorten the trip by about ten hours. On the way round, Apollo 13 reached a distance of 400,171 kilometres from Earth, which remains the greatest distance reached by a crewed spacecraft.

The days that followed were uncomfortable. Aquarius ran on a small amount of power, and the crew switched off nearly everything else, including the heating. The temperature in the cabin fell to about 3 degrees Celsius, and condensation collected on the walls. Each man was limited to about 200 grams of water a day, so that enough remained to cool the electronics. Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection. The crew slept little.

Carbon dioxide became the next concern. The lithium hydroxide canisters in Aquarius, which remove the gas from the cabin air, were rated for two men for two days, and they were filling up. Odyssey carried spare canisters, but they were square and the sockets in the lunar module were round. Engineers in Houston built an adapter from items they knew were on board, which were plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual cover, hoses from the spacesuits and duct tape. They read the instructions to the crew over the radio, the crew built the adapter, and the carbon dioxide level in the cabin fell.

Navigation also had to be done by hand. The guidance system of the lunar module was switched off to save power, and the cloud of debris around the spacecraft made it hard to take star sightings. Lovell lined up the craft using the day and night line on Earth as a reference, and about 105 hours into the flight the crew fired the descent engine for 14 seconds to correct the course. The correction was needed to bring the capsule into the narrow corridor for re-entry. If the angle was too shallow, it would skip off the atmosphere, and if it was too steep, it would burn up.

Mattingly meanwhile worked in a simulator in Houston with engineers to find a way to restart Odyssey using the limited power in its batteries. The procedure had to bring the systems on in a set order and stay within a tight limit on the current. It was sent up to the crew in pages of notes, and they followed it about four hours before re-entry. Before that, they released the service module, and for the first time the crew could see the damage. A whole panel was missing, and the side of the module was scorched. About an hour before re-entry they also released Aquarius, which burned up in the atmosphere after it had kept them alive.

The command module entered the atmosphere on 17 April. The blackout in radio contact, which is caused by the heat around the capsule, lasted longer than expected, and for some time Houston heard nothing. Then the capsule was seen under three parachutes, and it came down in the South Pacific near Samoa at 1:07 pm Houston time, 142 hours and 54 minutes after launch. It landed about six kilometres from the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The three men were helicopter-lifted to the ship. They were tired, dehydrated and cold, and Haise was the weakest, but none was seriously hurt.

A review board chaired by Edgar Cortright examined the accident. It found that the fault was in the design and testing of the tank and its heaters, and NASA changed the tank, removed the parts that had failed and added a third oxygen tank and a spare battery to the service module. Apollo 14 flew in January 1971 and landed at Fra Mauro. Lovell did not fly in space again.

The mission is often called a successful failure. That is a view about the mission, not a measurement of it, but the facts behind it are not in doubt. The spacecraft lost most of its power and oxygen about 200,000 miles from home, and the crew returned alive after about four days, using equipment that had been built for another purpose and instructions worked out on the ground.
