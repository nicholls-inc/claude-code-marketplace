Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it properly rather than treating it as a decorative leftover from an era of blotting paper. The argument against is not foolish, and it is worth stating at its strongest before answering it: children now spend their adult lives at keyboards and touchscreens, the hours given to looping an e and joining it to a t could go to reading or arithmetic, and the research literature does not show that a cursive hand makes a child cleverer or a better writer than a tidy print hand does. I accept most of that. What I reject is the conclusion that follows from it, because the case for joined writing was never that it improves intelligence. It rests on something more modest and more practical, which is that handwriting remains the main way a child gets thought onto paper for the whole of their school career, that fluency in it is a skill with real consequences for what a child can say and how quickly, and that a joined style is the most reliable route to that fluency for most children.

Start with what actually happens in classrooms, because the debate is often conducted as if schools were about to choose between pens and laptops. They are not. In England, as in most countries, the great majority of written assessment, including the national examinations taken at sixteen and eighteen, is done by hand, in timed conditions, by young people who will be marked partly on whether an examiner can read what they have produced. A child who reaches the age of eleven writing slowly, with every letter built separately and the pen lifted after each one, is carrying a tax on every piece of work they ever do. That tax is invisible to the child, who does not know that other children are writing at nearly twice the speed, and it is invisible to teachers too, who tend to see only the content and conclude that the child is weak at ideas when they may simply be weak at getting ideas down before they evaporate. Researchers who study the development of writing, Virginia Berninger most prominently, have argued for years that when the mechanics of transcription demand conscious effort, they compete for the same limited working memory as composition itself. A child who must think about forming each letter has less left over for choosing a word or shaping a sentence, and it follows that automatic handwriting is a precondition for fluent composition rather than a separate matter.

The question then becomes why joined writing should be the means of getting there, since children can in principle become fast and automatic in print. Some do. The difficulty is that print, written at speed, tends to degrade in unpredictable ways, with letters of uneven size, reversals creeping in under pressure, and a style that is neither the tidy print the child was taught nor anything else with a logic of its own. Joined writing has a logic. Every letter ends in an exit stroke and begins from an entry stroke, so that the hand moves in a continuous rhythm through a word, and a word becomes a single motor unit rather than a string of separate drawings. This matters for spelling as well as speed, because teachers of children who struggle with spelling, including many who work with dyslexic pupils, often value cursive precisely because the hand learns common letter sequences as patterns: the movement of writing ing or tion becomes something the body remembers when the mind has forgotten the rule. I would not claim that the research proves this as firmly as its advocates sometimes imply, and I will come back to the limits of the evidence, but the reasoning is sound and the experience of specialist teachers points the same way.

It is true that nobody has demonstrated in a large controlled trial that a child taught cursive from the start outperforms one taught print, and honesty requires admitting this. The better-known studies that people cite in this argument are mostly about something else. The finding that students who take lecture notes by hand tend to process the material more deeply than those who type it, published by Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer in 2014, concerned note-taking at university and has had a mixed record on replication. Work on young children by Karin James and others, showing that writing letters by hand activates reading-related regions of the brain more than typing or tracing them does, concerns the difference between handwriting and keyboards, not between joined and unjoined hands. What these studies collectively support is that the physical act of forming letters matters in early learning, which is an argument for handwriting in general. I do not pretend they settle the joined question. They do show, though, that the people who say handwriting is obsolete are making a claim for which the evidence is weaker than their confidence suggests, and a school that abandoned it on the strength of the keyboard argument would be running an experiment on children with no good reason to expect it to succeed.

What settles the matter, for me, is a comparison of costs. Teaching a joined script does not take much time. In schools that do it well, it means short, frequent sessions in the early years, perhaps ten minutes a day, with the pencil patterns practised first and the joins introduced as children gain control, and it is largely complete by the time a child is eight or nine. Set against this the cost of not teaching it, which is that a child must invent a personal style under exam pressure at the age of fourteen with nobody to help, and the arithmetic favours teaching. The time argument would carry more weight if the alternative were a curriculum packed with things that handwriting displaces, but the realistic alternative is not an extra half hour of mathematics. It is a few years of children practising print that they will then have to abandon or distort, since almost every adult who writes quickly by hand uses some form of joined or semi-joined writing anyway. The real choice is whether children are taught the thing most of them will end up doing, or left to improvise it badly.

There is a further point about what a school owes its pupils that I find hard to dismiss. Handwriting is among the very few skills that a primary school teaches to everyone, systematically, in the same way, regardless of what a child's parents can provide at home. A middle-class child with a tablet, a typing programme and a parent who sits beside them will probably do fine without a joined hand. A child without those things has a pencil and the instruction the school gives them, and if that instruction is thin, the gap between them and their better-resourced classmates widens in precisely the place where examiners are looking. It is easy for people who write for a living at keyboards to underrate what it is like to be a nine-year-old whose letters will not stay on the line. Fluent handwriting is not a luxury accomplishment, and its absence is felt most by the children who can least afford to lose marks.

I should also say something for the cultural argument, though I put it last because I think it the weakest of the reasons and I do not want it to carry more than it can. Cursive is how most people who lived before the last twenty years wrote, which means that letters, diaries, recipe cards and the marginal notes in a grandparent's books are in it, and a child who cannot read joined writing is shut out of a certain amount of their own family's history. Some American schools that dropped cursive after the Common Core standards left it out have found, as a number of states have since concluded when they legislated it back, that there was public feeling about this that the standards' authors had not anticipated. A signature, too, is still how people authenticate documents in many settings, and a signature is a joined-up gesture. None of this would justify a curriculum on its own, but it adds a small weight to a scale that is already tipping.

Against all this, the best remaining objection is that children differ, and that forcing a joined style on a child who finds it painful does harm. This is fair and the answer is to teach joined writing flexibly rather than to stop teaching it. A pupil with a motor difficulty, or a left-handed child for whom some joins are awkward, should be allowed to adapt the script, to leave out joins that cost more than they save, to settle on a hybrid that works for their hand. That is how many adults end up writing, and a good teacher can steer a child toward a legible, fast version of it without insisting on a model that the child's body resists. The standard to hold schools to is legible, comfortable, reasonably quick writing by the end of primary school, with joined writing as the default route and not an inflexible rule.

So the position I would defend is a plain one. Teach children to form letters carefully in the early years, introduce joins as soon as the basic shapes are secure, practise a little every day until the movements are automatic, and teach keyboard skills alongside, because children will need both and one does not threaten the other. Where the evidence is thin, as it is for the specific advantage of cursive over print, the sensible reading is that a long-standing practice with plausible mechanisms and low cost should not be dropped without a better reason than fashion. The children who gain most from a fluent hand are those who would otherwise struggle, and a school that stopped teaching it would be taking something from them in order to save time that, on honest inspection, was never going to be spent better elsewhere.
