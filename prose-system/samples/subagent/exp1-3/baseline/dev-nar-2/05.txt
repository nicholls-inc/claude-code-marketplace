Apollo 13: Failure and Return

On April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 2:13 in the afternoon, Eastern time. It was NASA's third planned lunar landing mission, and by then the public had grown casual about moon flights. Apollo 11 had been a triumph, Apollo 12 a precise success, and television networks had stopped carrying the crews' broadcasts live. The commander, Jim Lovell, was the most experienced astronaut alive, a veteran of Gemini 7, Gemini 12 and Apollo 8. With him were Fred Haise, the lunar module pilot, and Jack Swigert, a last-minute replacement for Ken Mattingly, who had been grounded days earlier after exposure to German measles.

The launch had one early flaw. The center engine of the second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the remaining engines burned longer to compensate. The crew reached orbit anyway, and for the next two days the flight was routine. They fired the engine that sent them toward the Moon, separated the command and service module, which they called Odyssey, and docked with the lunar module, Aquarius. Their path was a "free return" trajectory, a figure-eight that would swing them around the Moon and back toward Earth even if the main engine never fired again. They would later be very glad of that choice.

Hidden in the spacecraft was a defect that had been built in years before. Each service module carried two oxygen tanks, and inside each tank was a heater and a fan, used to stir the supercold liquid oxygen and keep its pressure steady. The tanks had originally been designed for 28-volt power, but the spacecraft's ground systems were later upgraded to 65 volts. The thermostat switches in the heaters were never upgraded to match. During a pre-launch test, ground crews had trouble draining oxygen from tank number two, and they ran its heater for hours to boil the oxygen off. The higher voltage welded the thermostat contacts shut, and the heater's insulation, a Teflon coating, was badly damaged. Nobody noticed.

On the evening of April 13, about 56 hours into the flight, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the tanks. Swigert flipped the switch. Roughly 90 seconds later, at 9:07 p.m. Houston time, with the spacecraft some 200,000 miles from Earth, the crew heard a loud bang. Sparks from the damaged wiring had ignited the Teflon in the oxygen-rich tank, and the tank failed. The blast blew a panel off the side of the service module and began draining oxygen into space. Lovell reported, in words that became famous in slightly altered form, "Houston, we've had a problem."

For the next several minutes, the crew and controllers struggled to understand what they were seeing. Voltage dropped on the main electrical buses. Oxygen pressure in tank two read zero, and tank one was falling. Lovell, looking out a window, saw a gas venting steadily into space. The command module relied on fuel cells that combined oxygen and hydrogen to make electricity and water, and without oxygen they would die. Within a couple of hours the spacecraft would lose power entirely. The Moon landing was impossible. The only question now was whether the crew could get home.

The answer lay in the lunar module, a fragile craft built for two men to spend a couple of days on the Moon. Its batteries and oxygen could not sustain three men for four days, but it was a lifeboat, and the crew moved into it, powering down Odyssey to save what little battery life remained. Flight director Gene Kranz and his "white team" and other shifts of controllers worked through the night. They had to decide whether to loop around the Moon or attempt a direct abort. A direct return would have required using the damaged service module's main engine, which could not be trusted. They chose to continue around the Moon and used Aquarius's descent engine to speed the return, burning it about five hours after passing the lunar far side.

The flight became a matter of rationing. The lunar module's systems were meant to run for around 45 hours with two men, and now needed to stretch to roughly 90 hours with three. Controllers cut power to almost everything, leaving the spacecraft cold and dark. Temperatures inside fell to near freezing, and condensation dripped from the walls. Water was strictly limited, and the crew drank little, which left Haise with a urinary infection and the others dehydrated. They slept little, huddled in the cold.

A second crisis was building in the air they breathed. Carbon dioxide, which the crew exhaled, is removed from cabin air by canisters of lithium hydroxide. The lunar module's canisters were round, sized for two men. The command module had plenty of spares, but they were square and would not fit the lunar module's round sockets. As the carbon dioxide level crept toward dangerous territory, engineers on the ground gathered the same materials the crew had aboard, including cardboard, plastic bags, suit hoses and duct tape, and designed an adapter. They read the instructions up to the crew step by step, and the astronauts built what came to be called "the mailbox." The carbon dioxide level dropped.

Other problems followed. After the Moon swing, a second burn was needed to correct the trajectory, and the crew had to do it without the usual guidance computer, which was shut down to save power. Lovell aligned the spacecraft by sighting on the Earth's terminator, the line between day and night, and the burn was made manually. Later, with the ship drifting off its alignment and the Sun no longer a usable reference, controllers asked the crew to realign using the Moon's position. Everything had to be restarted with precisely sequenced power-ups of systems that had never been designed to be started from such a cold state.

The most worrying task was bringing Odyssey back to life. Its battery power was only just enough to sustain reentry, and the order in which systems were switched on had to be written from scratch. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded, spent days in a simulator in Houston working out a procedure that would draw as little power as possible. The checklist was read to the crew line by line.

Before reentry the crew discarded the damaged service module. As it drifted away, they saw for the first time the scale of the damage, an entire side panel gone and equipment hanging out. Photographs were taken for the investigators. Then they left Aquarius, which had saved them, and jettisoned it shortly before reentry.

Reentry brought a communications blackout expected to last about three minutes. It stretched past four, and then past five. In Houston, no one spoke. Then, at about 1:07 p.m. on April 17, the capsule's radio came alive and Swigert's voice was heard. The parachutes bloomed, and Odyssey splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, within sight of the recovery ship, USS Iwo Jima. The crew had been gone nearly six days.

An investigation led by Edgar Cortright traced the cause to the tank's thermostat and the damaged wiring, and NASA redesigned the tanks, removing the fan wiring and adding a third oxygen tank. The Apollo program continued, and Apollo 14 flew the following year. Apollo 13 is often called a "successful failure," a mission that failed in its goal but succeeded at the larger purpose of bringing its crew home alive. That outcome rested on the discipline of the astronauts and the improvisation of thousands of engineers, who had spent years preparing for exactly the kind of day they were never supposed to have.
