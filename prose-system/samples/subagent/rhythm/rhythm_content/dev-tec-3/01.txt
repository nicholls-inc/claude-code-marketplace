A lithium-ion cell is charged with a two-stage protocol called constant-current, constant-voltage, and the reason for its two stages lies in the electrochemistry at the electrodes. During charging, an external supply drives electrons through the circuit from the cathode, typically a lithium metal oxide, to the anode, usually graphite, while lithium ions leave the cathode, cross the electrolyte and separator, and insert between the graphite layers in a process called intercalation. The charger first holds the current fixed, often around the one-hour rate, and lets the terminal voltage rise as the anode fills with lithium and the cathode empties. Voltage here is the sum of the cell's open-circuit voltage, which climbs with state of charge according to the difference in lithium chemical potential between the two electrodes, and an overpotential equal to current times the cell's internal resistance, plus extra polarization terms for slow processes inside the electrodes. When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, about 4.2 V for a common cobalt-based cell, the charger stops regulating current and instead holds the voltage constant, because exceeding that limit risks plating metallic lithium on the anode, oxidizing the electrolyte at the cathode surface, and accelerating degradation and thermal runaway. Once the voltage is clamped, the current must fall, and this is why the final portion of the charge is slow. The open-circuit voltage keeps rising as the cell fills, so the share of the fixed terminal voltage available to drive current across the internal resistance shrinks continuously, and the current decays roughly exponentially, much as in a capacitor charging through a resistor, though the analogy is imperfect because the open-circuit voltage is a nonlinear function of charge. Diffusion adds a second limit: lithium arrives at the particle surfaces faster than it can diffuse inward through the solid, so surface concentration saturates before the particle interior does, and the remaining capacity can only be filled at the pace set by solid-state diffusion, which falls as the host lattice approaches full occupancy. The charger terminates when the current drops to a small threshold, commonly a twentieth or a tenth of the initial rate, because the last few percent of capacity would take disproportionately long while contributing little energy. In practice the constant-current stage delivers roughly 70 to 80 percent of the capacity in well under an hour, and the constant-voltage stage takes comparable time to finish the remainder, which is why fast chargers advertise a charge to 80 percent rather than to full.
