**Scrap the Parking Minimums**

Walk through almost any American suburb and you will find a monument to a rule nobody remembers voting for. It is the parking lot, vast and mostly empty, sitting in front of a pharmacy, a dentist's office, a taco place. Someone decided decades ago that every business must supply a fixed number of spaces, and cities have been enforcing that decision ever since. It is time to stop. Cities should remove minimum parking requirements from their planning rules entirely.

Start with what these rules actually do. A minimum parking requirement does not say "provide parking if you need it." It says "provide this much parking or you may not build." The numbers look scientific, but they rarely are. Many trace back to a handbook of trip-generation surveys, often drawn from a handful of suburban sites with free parking and no transit. Planners then treated the peak-hour figure as a floor for every project, everywhere. The result is that a city's zoning code quietly dictates how much land a restaurant must surrender to cars before it can serve a single meal.

That land is not free. A surface space takes roughly 300 square feet once you count the aisles and entrances. A structured space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build; an underground one, far more. Those costs do not vanish. They get folded into rents, into menu prices, into the sticker price of a new apartment. Renters who own no car still pay for the spaces they never use. The researcher Donald Shoup has spent a career documenting this, and his conclusion is blunt: minimum requirements hide the price of parking inside the price of everything else, so everyone subsidizes driving, including people who do not drive.

Housing is where the damage shows most. Every required space is a space that is not a bedroom. In a city short on homes, requiring two spaces per unit can make an otherwise viable apartment building impossible, or shrink it by a third. Small lots in older neighborhoods, the kind that gave us corner stores and duplexes, often cannot meet modern parking math at all. Their owners cannot rebuild, cannot convert, cannot add a unit. The rule freezes the neighborhood in place while the housing shortage deepens.

Then there is the effect on the places we say we want. Cities spend years and millions trying to build walkable downtowns, then require every new building to set back behind a moat of asphalt. Shops drift apart. Walking gets longer and less pleasant. Driving becomes the only sensible choice, which, conveniently, justifies the next round of parking requirements. It is a loop, and the rule is what keeps it spinning.

The usual objection is that without minimums, developers will build nothing, and everyone will circle the block hunting for a spot. This deserves a serious answer. It is also mostly wrong.

Removing a minimum does not ban parking. It removes the mandate. Developers who think their tenants or customers need spaces will still build them, because a building with no parking in a car-dependent area will struggle to fill. Lenders often insist on it. What changes is that the decision moves from a rulebook to a market, and the amount of parking matches the actual project rather than a formula. Some buildings will have more than the old code demanded. Many will have less.

The evidence supports this. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017. Developers kept building parking where it made sense, and several projects that had been stalled became feasible. Minneapolis, Anchorage, and a growing list of cities have followed with similar reforms. California and Oregon have moved at the state level. None of them has descended into the gridlock that critics predicted. Cities that scrapped the rule are still standing.

What about the spillover problem, the real worry that cars will overflow onto residential streets? That is a management problem, and there are better tools than a blanket mandate. Price curbside parking at the level that keeps one or two spaces open per block. Offer residential permits where neighbors want them. Share the revenue with the street that earns it. These policies address congestion directly, rather than forcing every private owner to pay for an insurance policy against it.

Notice, too, what the mandate assumes: that the city knows better than the person writing the check. A church with a big Sunday crowd and empty weekdays can share a lot with an office next door. A small grocery near a bus line may need very little. A developer building for students or seniors might need almost none. Fixed ratios cannot see any of that. Flexibility can.

None of this means cars are the enemy. Plenty of people need them, and always will. The point is narrower. Whether a particular building needs a particular number of spaces is a question for the people who will build it, finance it, and use it. It is a poor use of a city council's time and an expensive use of everyone else's money.

Eliminate the minimums. Let parking be provided where it earns its keep, and let the land go to homes, shops, and trees where it does not. A city that trusts its builders and prices its streets honestly will end up with better neighborhoods, and lower rents, than one that keeps decreeing asphalt by formula.
