The Case for the Joined-Up Hand

Every few years someone declares joined-up handwriting dead. Finland made it optional in 2016 and moved the effort to keyboard skills. The American Common Core standards, published in 2010, left cursive out entirely, and in many classrooms it quietly stopped being taught. Commentators in Britain ask, more and more often, why seven-year-olds still spend afternoons practising loops and exit strokes when they will spend their adult lives on keyboards and touchscreens. On this view, joined-up writing is like Latin grammar or the slide rule: charming and traditional, and a poor use of scarce lesson time.

I think that view is wrong. Primary schools should go on teaching joined-up handwriting, and should teach it properly, as a fluent, legible, fast hand rather than a decorative exercise. The case does not depend on nostalgia, on the beauty of a copperplate letter, or on any claim that children will someday need to read their great-grandmother's diaries, though some will. It depends on what handwriting does for learning, on how children actually work at school, and on the difference between a skill that is obsolete and one that has simply gone out of fashion.

What we are actually arguing about

The debate is often muddled because people mean different things by "joined-up". Opponents tend to picture ornate cursive: looped capitals, a set of letterforms quite unlike the ones in books, drilled for their own sake. That picture is dated. In England the national curriculum asks pupils from about Year 2 to start using "the diagonal and horizontal strokes needed to join letters", and by the end of Year 4 to join handwriting in a way that improves legibility, consistency and quality. Most schools now teach a simple joined script built on the print letters children already know, with small entry and exit strokes that let the pen move from one letter to the next without lifting.

So the real question is not whether children should learn an archaic second alphabet. It is whether, having learned to form letters, they should be taught to link them into a hand that runs, or be left to print each letter separately for the rest of their lives. Put that way, the answer looks rather different.

Handwriting still matters, and speed is the reason

The first objection to joined-up writing is that handwriting itself is fading, so why refine it? But in schools handwriting has not faded at all. Children write by hand for most of their primary years. In England they still sit handwritten national tests at the end of primary school, and most sit handwritten GCSE and A-level papers years later. Some exam boards are trialling on-screen assessment, and that may grow, but for the present a pupil's ability to get thoughts onto paper quickly and legibly under time pressure has a direct effect on their results.

This is where joining earns its place. Research on writing development has shown repeatedly that handwriting fluency, meaning how quickly and automatically a child can form letters, predicts the quality and length of their compositions, not just how neat they look. The reasoning is straightforward. Working memory is limited. A child who must think about how to form each letter has less attention left for what they are trying to say: the next idea, the right word, the shape of the sentence. When letter formation becomes automatic, those resources are freed for thinking. A joined hand, once learned, is usually faster than print, because the pen leaves the paper less often and the hand moves in a continuous rhythm. Every pen lift is a small interruption. Across a forty-minute exam, those interruptions add up.

Not every child will end up with a fully joined script, and good teachers know that many adults write a practical hybrid, joining some letters and not others. That is fine. The point of teaching joins is to give children the option of speed. You cannot easily take up a skill you were never shown.

The hand and the brain

The second argument concerns what writing by hand does for learning to read and remember. Here the evidence is still developing and should be described carefully, but it points in a consistent direction.

Studies with young children have found that practising letters by hand, rather than typing or tracing them, is associated with stronger letter recognition. Brain-imaging work by Karin James and colleagues at Indiana University found that pre-literate children who practised printing letters by hand showed more activation in reading-related brain areas when they later saw letters than children who had practised by typing. The likely explanation is that producing a letter by hand forces the child to build it from strokes, with all the variation that involves, and this gives them a richer mental model of what makes an "a" an "a".

Research with older students points the same way. Work from the Norwegian University of Science and Technology using EEG has found more widespread connectivity in the brain during handwriting than during typing, in patterns linked to memory and learning. Studies of note-taking suggest that people who write by hand tend to paraphrase and process material rather than transcribe it word for word, simply because they cannot write fast enough to copy everything. That limitation turns out to be useful.

None of this proves that joined-up writing in particular is magic. Most of these findings concern handwriting in general. But they do undermine the main premise of the abolitionists, which is that handwriting is a mere transcription skill that technology has made redundant. If writing by hand supports reading, memory and understanding, then handwriting is worth teaching well. And teaching it well means taking it past laboured printing towards something fluent enough to keep up with thought. For most children that means joining.

There is a further, narrower benefit. Many teachers, and some specialists in dyslexia, favour joined writing because it treats a word as a single movement rather than a string of separate marks. A child who writes "the" or "ing" as one flowing shape builds motor memory for common letter patterns, which can support spelling. It also makes reversals such as b and d less likely, because a letter that has to start from the line and join to the next one cannot so easily be flipped. The evidence here is less systematic than practitioners' experience suggests, but it is a reason for caution before throwing the practice out.

The opportunity cost is smaller than claimed

Critics are right that lesson time is precious. But the time involved is modest: short, regular practice sessions of perhaps ten or fifteen minutes, concentrated in the early junior years, and then applied in ordinary writing across the curriculum. This is not a separate subject competing with maths or science. It is a refinement of a tool children use in nearly every lesson. Neglecting it is a false economy. Pupils who reach secondary school with slow, effortful handwriting are not liberated from the burden of writing; they carry it into every subject, every set of notes and every exam.

Nor is it either-or. Schools can and should teach keyboard skills too. Children who type well have a real advantage, and it is a failure of many curricula that typing is picked up by accident rather than taught. But keyboarding and joined handwriting are not in competition for the same slot. One is best taught in the later primary years once fine motor control is established; the other belongs in the years when children are already learning to write. A child can have both. What we should avoid is a child who has neither: someone who prints slowly and types with two fingers.

The equity argument

There is another reason that is easy to miss. If schools stop teaching joined handwriting, it will not disappear. It will become something children learn at home if their parents happen to care about it and have the time to teach it. Some will arrive at secondary school with a fast, fluent hand; others will not, and the difference will track family background rather than ability. The whole purpose of a common primary curriculum is to make sure that basic tools are given to everyone rather than left to chance. Handwriting fluency is exactly that kind of tool.

The same applies to reading joined script. Handwritten notes, letters, cards, historical documents, a teacher's comments in a margin: children who were never taught joined writing often struggle to read it. That is a small exclusion, but a real one, and it falls on those who had the least help elsewhere.

Answering the hardest objection

The strongest case against joined-up writing comes from children who find it truly difficult: pupils with dyspraxia, developmental coordination disorder or other motor difficulties, for whom insisting on perfect joins can be miserable and counterproductive. This is a serious point, and the answer is flexibility, not abolition. Good practice already allows for it. A child with significant motor difficulties may be better served by a clear print script, or by early access to a keyboard, and should not be penalised for it. But we do not stop teaching reading because some children have dyslexia; we adapt our teaching for those children. The exceptions call for judgement in individual cases, not for removing the skill from everyone else.

It is also worth noticing how the American experiment has gone. After cursive dropped out of the common standards, a number of states moved to bring it back, California among them in 2023. Whatever one thinks of each of those decisions, they suggest that once the practice went, people noticed something missing.

Conclusion

The argument against joined-up handwriting rests on a picture of the future in which children never need to write by hand. That future has not arrived in schools, and the evidence on how handwriting supports reading, memory and composition suggests we should not hurry it along. Joined writing, taught as a practical, fast and legible hand rather than an ornamental one, helps children write more, think more freely while they write, and cope with the handwritten tasks that still decide much of their education. It costs a little time in the early years and repays it for a lifetime.

Keep teaching it. Teach typing as well. Make allowances for the children who need them. But do not drop a useful skill just because it looks old-fashioned.
