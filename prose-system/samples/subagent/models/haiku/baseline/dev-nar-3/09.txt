The Great Molasses Flood

On the afternoon of January 15, 1919, Boston enjoyed an unseasonable warmth. People walked the narrow streets of the North End, and children played near the waterfront on Commercial Street. Nobody there expected that the sweet smell drifting over the neighborhood would soon become a killer.

Looming over the street stood a steel tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company, roughly fifty feet tall and ninety feet across. It held about 2.3 million gallons of molasses, bound for distilleries where it would be turned into alcohol for explosives during the war. The tank had been built quickly and was known to leak. Its seams had been patched, and complaints had been made. Residents found sticky sweetness on the sidewalks, and some had heard the tank groan on cold days.

Around 12:30 in the afternoon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a sharp crack like artillery fire, followed by a low rumble. Then the steel plates burst outward, and a wave of molasses, perhaps fifteen to twenty-five feet high, surged down the street at an estimated 35 miles per hour. It flattened buildings, twisted the supports of the elevated railway, knocked a firehouse off its foundation, and swept horses, carts, and people into the muck.

The molasses was thick and heavy, and it was almost impossible to escape. Rescuers waded through a sticky brown tide that in places rose above their waists, pulling out survivors and the dead. Twenty-one people died, including children and workers, and about 150 were injured. Some victims were not found until days later, beneath the wreckage.

The cleanup dragged on for weeks. Soldiers, sailors, and volunteers shoveled molasses, spread sand over the streets, and hosed down the pavement with seawater. The harbor itself ran brown for a time. Wagons hauled away the mess, but for years afterward, residents said that on hot summer days the North End still smelled faintly of molasses.

The company first insisted that sabotage, perhaps by anarchists, had caused the disaster. That claim did not survive investigation. Inquiries found that the tank had been poorly designed and too thinly built, and that the company had cut corners and ignored warnings. In 1925, a judge found the company liable, and the victims' families received a settlement after years of litigation.

The flood lasted only minutes, but its lessons lasted much longer. It helped prompt stronger engineering standards and tougher rules for industrial storage. It remains one of Boston's strangest and most sobering stories: a sweet, sticky wave that arrived without warning on an ordinary winter afternoon.
