A lithium-ion cell is charged by forcing current through it in the direction opposite to discharge, which pushes lithium ions out of the positive electrode, across an electrolyte-soaked separator, and into the graphite of the negative electrode. The electrons that balance this ion movement travel through the external circuit from the charger. The cell's voltage at rest depends on how full each electrode is, so as lithium accumulates in the graphite and leaves the cathode, the open-circuit voltage climbs from roughly 3.0 V when empty to about 4.2 V when full for a typical cobalt-based cell.

Almost all commercial chargers use a two-stage method called constant current followed by constant voltage. In the first stage the charger holds the current at a fixed value, commonly between 0.5 and 1 times the cell's capacity per hour, and lets the terminal voltage rise freely. The terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage plus the current multiplied by the cell's internal resistance, so some of the voltage the charger sees is simply a drop across that resistance. When the terminal voltage reaches the upper limit, typically 4.2 V, the charger switches to the second stage and holds the voltage there instead. That limit exists because pushing a cell higher accelerates electrolyte breakdown, damages the cathode structure, and in the worst case causes lithium metal to plate onto the anode, which is both a capacity loss and a safety hazard.

Charging slows in the second stage because of this fixed voltage ceiling. Once the terminal voltage is pinned at the limit, any further rise in the open-circuit voltage, which happens as the cell fills, leaves less voltage available to drive current through the internal resistance. The current therefore decays, roughly exponentially, as the cell approaches its full state. A second effect compounds this. Lithium must diffuse into the solid particles of graphite, and as the outer layers of each particle fill, the concentration gradient that drives diffusion toward the interior flattens. The surface can sit near its full value while the core is still catching up, so the particles accept lithium ever more slowly and the cell cannot absorb a high current without the surface overshooting.

The charger normally ends the cycle when the current falls to a small fraction of the original value, often around a tenth or a twentieth of the capacity rate. By then the cell holds nearly all of its usable charge, and the remaining time is spent on a small final gain. This is why a phone or electric vehicle reaches about eighty percent quickly, then takes disproportionately long to finish. Many products deliberately stop short of the full limit to extend life, accepting a little less capacity in return for less stress on the electrodes.
