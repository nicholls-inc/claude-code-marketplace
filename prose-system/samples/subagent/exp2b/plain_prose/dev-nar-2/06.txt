On the evening of April 11, 1970, Apollo 13 lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise. It was NASA's third planned lunar landing mission, and public interest had already cooled; after Apollo 11 and 12, the networks barely bothered to broadcast the crew's television transmissions. Lovell, the commander, was the most experienced astronaut alive, a veteran of two Gemini flights and Apollo 8. Haise was to pilot the lunar module, which the crew had named Aquarius, while Swigert flew the command module, Odyssey. Swigert was himself a late substitute: Ken Mattingly had been grounded days before launch after exposure to German measles, and Swigert had trained in his place with only a few days' notice.

The launch itself brought an early warning. The center engine of the Saturn V's second stage shut down about two minutes early, and the remaining engines burned longer to compensate. The spacecraft reached orbit and then set off for the Moon without further trouble. For two days the flight was routine, even dull. The crew checked systems, ate, and prepared for a landing in the Fra Mauro highlands.

Inside the service module, however, a flaw was waiting. Years earlier, a thermostatic switch in one of the two oxygen tanks had been designed for a lower voltage than the ground power later supplied to it. During a prelaunch test, the tank would not empty normally, and technicians boiled off the oxygen using the heaters, which ran far longer than intended. The overheated switches fused shut, and the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank was damaged. Nobody noticed.

On April 13, about fifty-five hours into the flight and roughly 200,000 miles from Earth, mission control asked the crew to stir the cryogenic tanks, a routine task. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later a spark ignited the damaged insulation inside Oxygen Tank 2, which was filled with liquid oxygen under pressure. The fire raised the pressure until the tank, and part of the side panel of the service module, blew away.

Aboard the spacecraft, the crew heard a loud bang and felt a shudder. Swigert reported, calmly, that they had had a problem. Lovell repeated it for the record in the line that became famous as "Houston, we've had a problem." Warning lights flickered, voltages dropped, and Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas venting into space. The other oxygen tank was failing as well. The service module supplied the command module with electricity, water and breathable oxygen, and within hours both of the tanks were empty. Apollo 13 would not be landing on the Moon. The question became whether the crew would get home at all.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his team in Houston turned immediately to survival. The command module Odyssey had batteries, but they were meant only for the final hours of reentry, and they had to be saved. The lunar module, Aquarius, was a small craft designed to support two men for about two days on the Moon. The decision was made to use it as a lifeboat, and the crew moved into it, powering down Odyssey almost entirely.

The next choice was the route home. A direct abort, turning around at once, would have required using the damaged service module's main engine, which no one trusted. Instead the planners chose to let the ship swing around the far side of the Moon and use the Moon's gravity to bend the trajectory back toward Earth. About five hours after passing the Moon, the crew fired the lunar module's descent engine to speed the return, shortening the trip. Later burns would correct the course, because the spacecraft was drifting slightly off the narrow entry corridor.

The hardest problems came in the following days. Aquarius had been built for two men for a short stay; now three would live in it for four days. Carbon dioxide, which the crew exhaled, began to build up. The lunar module's lithium hydroxide canisters, which scrub the gas out of the air, were running low, and the spare canisters from the command module were square while the lunar module's receptacles were round. Engineers on the ground gathered the same materials the crew had aboard, including plastic bags, cardboard, suit hoses and duct tape, and worked out a way to fit the square canister to the round opening. They read the instructions up to the crew, who built the adapter and watched the carbon dioxide level fall.

Cold set in as well. With most systems off to conserve power and water, the cabin temperature dropped to a few degrees above freezing. Water condensed on the walls. The crew slept little, Haise developed a fever from a urinary infection, and everyone was dehydrated because water was rationed to preserve cooling for the electronics. Lovell later recalled the cold as the worst part of the ordeal.

Meanwhile, on the ground, a second set of engineers faced the question of restarting Odyssey. Never had a command module been powered up from nearly cold after being shut down for days, and nobody had a procedure. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, worked in the simulator with the engineers to write a sequence that would bring the module back to life using as little power as possible. He tried one version after another until the numbers worked.

On April 17, as the ship neared Earth, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module. Only then could they see the extent of the harm: a whole panel was missing, and the wreckage trailed away into space. They photographed it, which gave investigators critical evidence. Then they left the lunar module, climbed back into Odyssey, and powered it up following Mattingly's procedure. Aquarius was released shortly before entry; it burned up in the atmosphere over the Pacific.

Reentry carried one more worry. The heat shield might have been damaged, and the ordinary period of radio silence during the fiery descent was longer than expected. Controllers waited in silence beyond the time they expected to hear from the crew. Then, at last, Swigert's voice came over the radio. The parachutes opened, and the capsule splashed down in the South Pacific near Samoa, on the afternoon of April 17, within sight of the recovery ship Iwo Jima. The three men were weak and thin, but healthy.

A review board led by Edgar Cortright traced the accident to the tank's damaged wiring and the switch that had failed to protect it. NASA redesigned the oxygen tanks, removed the stirring fans, and added a third tank, along with other changes to the service module. The flight was later described as a successful failure. It had not reached the Moon, but it showed what a skilled team on the ground and in space could do when everything went wrong at once.
