Yes: primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it deliberately, not leave it to chance.

The usual case against it goes like this. Children now type more than they write by hand, so a skill that once took a term of lessons is an anachronism. Print letters are easier to learn because each one is separate and fixed. Curriculum time is scarce, and joined-up writing looks like a decorative extra. Each point deserves a fair hearing, but none of them survives a close look at what handwriting is for.

Start with fluency. Handwriting is not really a question of forming letters correctly. It is a question of how quickly and easily a child can get words from their head onto the page. A joined script lets the hand move in one continuous stroke across a word. Pupils who write in print must lift the pen after almost every letter, and each lift is a small decision and a small pause. When a child has to think about where the next letter goes, the thought they were trying to record starts to fade. Joined writing reduces those pauses. Over a school day, and then over years, the difference in speed is large. Anyone who has watched a nine-year-old struggle through a paragraph in print, and then watched a classmate fill a page in a flowing hand, has seen the gap.

Speed matters because of working memory. Children have a limited amount of attention. Research on writing development, particularly the work of Virginia Berninger and Steve Graham, has long argued that when transcription (the physical and orthographic business of producing letters and words) becomes automatic, the mind has room for composition: choosing words, organising ideas, checking meaning. When transcription stays laborious, it crowds out everything else. A child who has to puzzle over every letter has less attention for the story. Graham's meta-analyses found that handwriting and typing instruction both have measurable effects on writing quality and quantity, so the skill is not merely a formality. It is part of how children learn to write at all. Joined-up handwriting is one of the most efficient ways to make transcription automatic, because the letter-to-letter connections become a single practised movement rather than a sequence of separate ones.

Joining also helps with spelling and reading, though the evidence here is more modest and should be stated carefully. Joined words have a visual unity: the shape of a word becomes one gesture, and that gesture can be recalled as a whole. Some teachers report that children who write joined words make fewer spacing errors and fewer letter reversals, since the connecting strokes give each letter a defined starting point and a defined direction. Neuroscience studies, including work by Karin James and her colleagues, suggest that the motor act of forming letters strengthens the brain's recognition of them, which supports reading. That finding concerns handwriting in general rather than joined handwriting specifically, but it points the same way: a child who forms letters with purpose is better placed to read them. The strongest version of this argument is not that joined-up writing is magic. It is that it is a cheap, well-understood practice that supports the systems children use to read and spell.

Consider the objection from typing. Keyboards are everywhere, and many adults rarely write by hand beyond a signature. But the question is not whether children will type. They will. The question is what they need to be able to do without a device. Notes in a lecture theatre, a form at a clinic, a message left on a fridge, a shopping list when the phone battery has died: handwriting remains common in ordinary life, and it will remain so. A person who writes slowly and laboriously is at a disadvantage in all of those settings, and the disadvantage is felt most by the people who have fewest alternatives.

There is also the matter of heritage. Joined-up writing is the script in which much of the historical record was written: letters, diaries, parish registers, wills, the handwritten notes of parents and grandparents. A child who has never learned to read cursive will be cut off from that material unless someone else transcribes it for them. This is not a strong argument for teaching a particular style for its own sake, but it is an argument against letting a generation grow up unable to read the handwriting of their own family. Schools exist in part to transmit what a society has written, not only what it writes now. A child who can read a grandmother's recipe card has access to something that a child who cannot is missing.

Some critics say that print is simpler and that children should master one form before learning another. This sounds sensible, but in practice it often means that children learn print, then face a second, disruptive transition later, often at the point when they are being asked to write longer pieces at speed. Teaching the joined forms from the start, or introducing them in a short, structured sequence, avoids that second transition. Several education systems already take this approach, with pupils expected to be writing joined letters within the first few years of school. The aim is not to make children's handwriting beautiful, though that is a fine bonus. It is to make the mechanical side of writing efficient enough that the child can concentrate on meaning.

The time cost is real but small. A few short sessions a week, carried out over several years, are enough to give most children a fluent joined hand. Compare that with the hours spent on other skills that are rarely questioned. And the benefits accrue across every subject, because every subject involves writing. A child who writes fluently in mathematics, science and history will find those lessons easier, and will be more likely to keep up with the demands of secondary school, where long written answers are standard.

Finally, there is a matter of dignity and self-expression. Handwriting is one of the few skills in which a child's individuality is visible on the page. A good joined hand gives a child a sense of control over their own work and a pride in producing something neat. Those feelings are not trivial. Children who feel competent at writing are more willing to write, and children who write more tend to improve.

The case, then, is not nostalgic. Joined-up handwriting is a practical tool for fluency, for working memory, for the reading and spelling systems that depend on letter formation, and for access to the written record. Its costs are modest and its benefits are broad. Primary schools should keep teaching it, and they should teach it early and well.
