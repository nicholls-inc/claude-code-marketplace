On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an unusually warm winter afternoon. The temperature had climbed to about 40 degrees Fahrenheit after days of bitter cold, and the neighborhood's mostly Italian and Irish immigrant families were going about their business near the harbor. Looming over Commercial Street stood a huge steel tank, 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, owned by the Purity Distilling Company. It held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, which was bound for industrial alcohol and, ultimately, munitions.

The tank had never been trusted by those who lived beside it. It leaked so badly that residents collected the dripping syrup in cans to take home. Rather than fix the flaws, the company reportedly painted the tank brown to hide the stains. It was never properly tested, and the engineers who approved it had little experience with such structures. Only days earlier, a fresh shipment of warm molasses had been pumped in on top of the cold, stored batch, and the contents were expanding.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, the tank gave way. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, the rapid popping of rivets shooting loose, followed by a rumble. Then the tank split open, and a wave of molasses estimated at 25 feet high at its crest rushed out at around 35 miles per hour.

Everything in its path was overwhelmed. The wave tore the Boston Elevated Railway's overhead structure from its supports and pushed a train off its tracks. It crushed buildings and flung wagons, horses, and people through the air. A firehouse was knocked from its foundation, and members of the crew inside were trapped in the wreckage. Dozens of people were caught in a thick, cold sludge that grew stickier by the minute, making escape nearly impossible. Those who were not crushed by the initial surge often found themselves slowly suffocating as the syrup closed around them.

Rescuers, including sailors from a nearby ship, soldiers, police, and Red Cross workers, rushed into the mess. They waded through knee-deep molasses to reach survivors. Doctors worked in makeshift hospitals, and the search for bodies lasted days. In the end, 21 people were dead and about 150 were injured. Among the victims were children, and even a few horses that had to be put down.

The cleanup was a slow ordeal. Crews sprayed salt water from fireboats and spread sand and sawdust over the streets. The harbor ran brown until summer. Residents said the area smelled of molasses for decades, and some insist it still does on hot days.

The aftermath proved just as consequential as the flood itself. Survivors and families brought a class-action suit against United States Industrial Alcohol, the parent company of Purity Distilling. The company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, a claim tied to the fears of the era. After years of hearings and thousands of pages of testimony, an auditor ruled that the tank had been structurally unsound. The company paid out roughly 600,000 dollars in settlements, a modest sum even then.

The disaster also changed American building practice. It helped push states to require that engineers and architects sign off on plans, and it contributed to stricter oversight of construction. A strange, sweet catastrophe had taught a hard lesson about accountability.
