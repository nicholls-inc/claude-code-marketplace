A leap second is a one-second adjustment occasionally added to the world's official clocks so that they stay in step with the turning of the Earth. The need for it arises because there are two ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. Atomic clocks, which count the vibrations of atoms such as caesium, keep time with such extraordinary steadiness that they would lose or gain less than a second over many millions of years. The Earth, by contrast, is a rather unreliable timekeeper, its rotation slowing very gradually because of tidal friction from the Moon and also speeding up and slowing down in small, unpredictable ways because of earthquakes, shifting ice, ocean currents and movements in the planet's molten core.

Since 1972, the world's official time, known as Coordinated Universal Time, has been atomic time that is nudged to stay within a second of the time measured by the Earth's rotation. When the two drifted apart, international timekeepers added a leap second, usually on the last day of June or December, so that the clock read 23:59:60 before ticking over to midnight. Twenty-seven have been added so far, the most recent at the end of 2016.

The system worked well enough for astronomers and navigators, but it has become a headache for the modern digital world. Because computers generally assume that every minute has sixty seconds and that time only moves forward, a sixty-first second can confuse software, and leap seconds have been blamed for crashes and outages at major websites, airlines and data centres. Because the Earth's spin cannot be predicted far ahead, leap seconds are announced only a few months in advance, so engineers cannot plan for them properly. Companies such as Google and Meta have adopted their own workarounds, spreading the extra second across several hours, but these differ from one another and add more complexity. Financial markets, power grids, satellite navigation and telecommunications all depend on timing precise to tiny fractions of a second, and a mishandled leap second carries real risks.

In 2022, the international body that sets measurement standards, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. Under the plan, the gap between atomic and Earth-based time will be allowed to grow well beyond a single second, and a decision about how to correct it will be left to the future, perhaps decades or even a century from now. Scientists also noted a surprise: the Earth's spin has recently been running slightly faster, which could one day require a negative leap second, a more dangerous change that software has never faced. Abandoning the practice, supporters argue, makes everyday timekeeping safer and simpler, while the slow mismatch with the Sun matters to almost no one.
