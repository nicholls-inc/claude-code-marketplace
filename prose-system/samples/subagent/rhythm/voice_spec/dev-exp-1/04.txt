A heat pump does not make heat by burning anything. It moves heat that already exists outdoors into the house, and it can do this even when the air outside is well below the temperature indoors. The idea sounds odd until you recall that "cold" is only a relative term. Air at minus 5 degrees Celsius still holds a large amount of thermal energy. It is far above absolute zero, which is minus 273 degrees, and a machine can extract part of that energy if it has a way to make heat travel the "wrong" way, from cold to warm.

The way it does this is the same way a fridge works. A sealed circuit contains a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature, often well below minus 20 degrees Celsius at normal pressure. In the outdoor unit, a fan blows outside air across a coil carrying this refrigerant. Because the refrigerant is colder than the air, even on a frosty day, heat flows from the air into the fluid, and the fluid boils into a gas. This is the same process as water turning to steam, only it happens at a much lower temperature.

The gas then passes through a compressor, which is an electric pump. Squeezing a gas raises its temperature, as you will have noticed when a bicycle pump warms up. The compressor takes the refrigerant from just above freezing to somewhere between 35 and 55 degrees Celsius, which is hot enough to heat radiators or underfloor pipes. The hot gas goes through a second coil inside the house, where it gives up its heat to the water in your heating system and condenses back into a liquid. An expansion valve then drops the pressure, the liquid cools sharply, and it returns to the outdoor coil to start again.

The electricity goes into the compressor and fan, not into creating the heat itself. A typical modern heat pump delivers about three units of heat for every one unit of electricity it uses. This ratio is called the coefficient of performance, or COP. A gas boiler cannot exceed 100 per cent efficiency, and most older ones manage 75 to 85 per cent. So a heat pump with a COP of 3 produces roughly three times as much heat per unit of energy bought. Electricity usually costs more per kilowatt hour than gas, so the running cost depends on the price gap. In the UK electricity has recently cost around three to four times as much as gas per unit, which means a heat pump with a COP of 3 comes out roughly level with a gas boiler, and a better one comes out cheaper.

Cold weather does reduce performance. At 7 degrees Celsius outside, a good unit might reach a COP of 3.5 to 4. At minus 5 it may fall to about 2.5. The unit also has to defrost its outdoor coil now and then, which uses some energy. Modern units keep working at minus 15 or lower, but they work harder as it gets colder, and the seasonal average, known as SCOP, is the figure that matters for your bills. Look for a SCOP of 3.5 or more.

For a homeowner, the practical questions are about the house rather than the machine. Heat pumps deliver water at lower temperatures than a gas boiler, so they work best with good insulation and larger radiators or underfloor heating. A survey will show whether your radiators are big enough and how much heat the house loses. Installation typically costs between 8,000 and 14,000 pounds before grants, though the UK government's Boiler Upgrade Scheme currently offers 7,500 pounds towards it in England and Wales. The unit also needs outdoor space and a hot water cylinder.

My view is that a well-insulated house with a gas boiler near the end of its life is a good candidate, while a draughty house with small radiators should be improved first. A heat pump can heat a house in cold weather, but how well it does so depends on how much heat your house loses.
