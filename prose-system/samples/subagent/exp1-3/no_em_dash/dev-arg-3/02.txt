Keep the Joined-Up Hand

Every few years, someone declares cursive dead. Keyboards have won, the argument goes, so primary schools should stop spending precious hours teaching children to loop one letter into the next. I think this is mistaken. Primary schools should still teach joined-up handwriting, and teach it well. The case does not rest on nostalgia or on a fondness for neat exercise books. It rests on what the skill does for children's writing, their thinking, their reading, and their access to the written world.

Begin with the most practical point. Handwriting is still how children demonstrate what they know. Whatever the future holds, a seven-year-old in a classroom today will spend years writing by hand in lessons, in tests, and in the exams that decide their prospects. In many countries, including England, the major school exams are still handwritten. A child who writes slowly, illegibly, or with painful effort is at a real disadvantage, because examiners cannot reward what they cannot read, and a child whose hand tires after a page cannot show what they understand. Fluent handwriting is not a decoration on top of learning. It is a tool for it, and a tool that has to be built early.

Joined-up writing is the most efficient way to build it. The central mechanical advantage is simple. When letters are joined, the pen stays on the page for the length of a whole word instead of lifting after every letter. That reduces the number of separate movements, which in turn speeds the hand and smooths the rhythm. Print can be written quickly, but it asks the writer to start and stop constantly. A joined hand lets a child develop flow. Teachers who work with children who struggle often say that joining helps with spelling too, because a word becomes a single motor pattern, a shape the hand learns as a unit. Children who write letter by letter sometimes treat the word as a string of unrelated parts. Children who write it in one movement are practising the word as a whole.

There is a respectable body of research on the link between handwriting and cognition, and it is worth stating carefully rather than overclaiming. Studies comparing handwriting with typing have found that writing by hand tends to support letter recognition in young children, and that note-taking by hand can encourage students to process and compress ideas rather than transcribe them word for word. Brain imaging work has shown richer activity in areas associated with reading and memory when people write by hand than when they type. None of this proves that cursive in particular is a cognitive superpower, and honest advocates should concede that the evidence for joined writing over print is thinner than the evidence for handwriting over typing. But the direction of the evidence is clear. The motor act of forming letters by hand does something useful for learning that tapping a key does not. If we accept that handwriting deserves a place in the primary curriculum, the next question is which style, and the practical answer is the one that most children end up needing anyway.

That brings me to the argument I find most persuasive, which is about equity. Children who are never taught a fluent joined hand do not stop needing to write. They simply invent their own style, and the results vary enormously. Some develop a quick, legible hybrid. Many develop something cramped, inconsistent, and hard to read. The children most likely to be left to work it out alone are those whose homes offer the least support: no one at the kitchen table reminding them how to hold a pencil, no stationery, no culture of letter-writing. A school that drops explicit handwriting instruction does not remove the skill from the world. It removes the guarantee that every child will acquire it. Teaching it to everyone, systematically, is the only way to make sure fluency does not become another quiet advantage of the well-supported.

There is also a point about children with particular needs, and it cuts against a common objection. Critics say joined writing is harder for children with dyslexia or dyspraxia, and some individual children certainly do better with print or with a keyboard. Teachers should absolutely adapt for them. But the existence of exceptions is an argument for flexibility, not for abolition. Several specialists in dyslexia have in fact argued that a consistent joined style helps some children, because it reduces the reversals of letters like b and d and gives each word a distinct shape. The sensible position is to teach joined writing as the default and to make informed adjustments for the child in front of you.

Now consider the strongest argument on the other side, which is that time is finite. Every hour spent practising joins is an hour not spent on reading, mathematics, science, or learning to type. I take this seriously. A crowded curriculum forces trade-offs, and nobody should defend handwriting at any cost. But the trade is smaller than it looks. Teaching joined writing does not require a large block of dedicated time. Short, frequent, well-structured practice, ten minutes a day in the early years, is enough for most children to reach fluency by the end of primary school. Handwriting also overlaps with other goals, since it is practised through writing sentences, spelling words, and composing stories. The time is not simply lost. And teaching typing is not an alternative to teaching handwriting. Children can and should learn both, and the keyboard skills that matter most, such as touch typing, are learned best at around nine or ten, once the hand has already learned what writing feels like.

A related objection is that cursive is a relic with no future. This overlooks how much of the world remains handwritten. Think of the birthday card, the condolence note, the signature on a document, the quick list on the fridge, the margin note on a draft. Think of the historical record: letters, diaries, and archives written in cursive that a generation of children may be unable to read if they never learn it. A child who cannot read joined-up writing cannot read a grandparent's letter, and cannot read many primary sources in a history class. Teaching joined writing is, in part, teaching children to read a form of writing that most of the past was written in, and that plenty of the present still is.

I should also address the claim that cursive is a matter of taste. It is not entirely, but one aspect of it is. There is a pleasure in good handwriting, a sense of pride and ownership that comes from producing something that looks cared for. That is not trivial for a young child. Few things give a six-year-old a stronger feeling of competence than seeing their own writing become tidy and grown-up. Schools talk a great deal about motivation and about the dignity of effort. A skill that rewards patience visibly, week after week, in the child's own exercise book is a rare and useful thing to have in the day.

None of this means the way we teach handwriting is above criticism. Too many schools still treat it as a copying exercise, with children filling rows of identical letters and little feedback on how they hold the pen or form the movement. Good instruction starts with posture, grip, and the direction of strokes. It introduces joins gradually, once letters are secure, and it connects practice to real writing instead of isolated drills. It stays alert to the left-handed child who needs the paper turned, and to the child whose pencil grip has not yet matured. Done badly, handwriting teaching is a chore. Done well, it is brief, purposeful, and satisfying. The solution to bad handwriting teaching is better handwriting teaching, not none.

Nor should we pretend that the choice is between pen and screen. The real question is what a child needs to be able to do by age eleven, and the answer is: write legibly and at speed by hand, and type competently on a keyboard. Both skills matter. They serve different purposes, and the first is the one that has to be taught first, because it is the one a young child's body and brain are best placed to learn, and the one that most obviously supports early literacy.

So my answer is yes. Primary schools should teach joined-up handwriting, as a default, with sensible flexibility for those who need something different. It is efficient, because it lets the hand move in flowing units. It is equitable, because it ensures every child leaves with a skill that otherwise depends on luck and home support. It keeps children connected to the written record of the past and to the personal writing that still matters in the present. And it supports the broader work of learning to read, spell, and compose. The keyboard is a wonderful tool and children should master it. But a tool does not replace a skill, and the pen still has work to do. We should hand it to children properly, and teach them how to make it flow.
