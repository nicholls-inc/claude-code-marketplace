In Defence of the Joined-Up Line

Every few years a headline announces the death of cursive. Keyboards are everywhere, the argument runs, so why should seven-year-olds spend precious hours looping their ls and linking their ts? Some education systems have already dropped joined-up writing from their requirements, and plenty of teachers quietly let it slide. I think this is a mistake. Primary schools should keep teaching joined-up handwriting, and they should teach it properly. The reasons are practical rather than nostalgic. A fluent joined script helps children write faster, think more freely, spell more reliably and take part fully in the written culture around them. The cost is modest and the benefit is lasting.

Start with the objection, because it deserves a fair hearing. Most adult writing now happens on screens. Children will type essays, emails and reports for the rest of their lives, and the curriculum is already crowded. If a skill is rarely used, the sensible course is to teach it less. This is a reasonable principle, but it rests on a false premise. Handwriting is not rarely used. Children handwrite constantly in school, in exams, in notes, in cards, on forms and on the whiteboard. In many countries, high-stakes examinations are still written by hand, and they will remain so for the foreseeable future because paper is cheap, secure and fair. A child who cannot write quickly and legibly by hand is handicapped in precisely the moments that matter most. The question is therefore not whether handwriting survives but whether children will be good at it.

Speed is the first argument for joining. Print letters require the pen to lift after every character, and each lift costs a fraction of a second and a small interruption in the flow of thought. Over a long exam answer, those fractions accumulate. A joined script lets the hand travel in a continuous movement, with the pen leaving the page once per word rather than once per letter. Research on handwriting fluency repeatedly finds that children who write quickly and automatically produce longer and better compositions than those who struggle with the mechanics. This is not surprising. Working memory is limited, and every scrap of attention spent on forming a letter is attention not spent on choosing a word or structuring an argument. Automatic handwriting frees the mind. Joined writing, taught well, is one of the most effective routes to that automaticity.

The second argument concerns spelling. When children write in a joined script, they learn words as movements. The pattern of a common letter string such as ing or tion becomes a single motor habit, practised until the hand produces it without conscious effort. Teachers of early literacy often observe that children who join their letters tend to spell common patterns more consistently, because the hand carries a memory the eye might lose. The evidence here is suggestive rather than conclusive, and honest advocates should say so. But the mechanism is plausible and fits what we know about how motor memory supports learning. Typing builds a similar habit, yet it does so for the fingers on a keyboard, a movement that has no connection to the shape of the word. The handwritten word has a visible, personal form that reinforces the way it looks on the page.

Third, there is the matter of what handwriting does for thinking. Studies comparing note-taking by hand and by laptop have produced mixed results, and the most famous claims have been challenged on replication. Still, a consistent theme runs through the better work on the subject. Writing by hand slows people down slightly, which forces them to summarise and select rather than transcribe. Brain imaging studies of children and adults suggest that handwriting engages wider networks than keyboarding, including regions linked to reading and to letter recognition. Young children who practise forming letters by hand appear to recognise those letters faster and more accurately than children who only type or trace them. None of this proves that cursive is magic. It does suggest that the hand and the mind are connected in ways a keyboard does not replicate, and that early childhood is a poor time to abandon the connection.

Fourth, consider the children who most need help. Dyslexic and dyspraxic pupils are often cited as the strongest case for joining. Print letters invite reversals, since b and d are mirror images of one another, and a child who confuses them in print may find the confusion reduced in a joined script, where each letter begins on the baseline with a lead-in stroke and the shapes become more distinct. Because the pen moves continuously, there is also less ambiguity about where one letter ends and the next begins, and spacing between words becomes more natural. Specialist teachers have long recommended joined handwriting for these children for exactly these reasons. A curriculum that drops cursive for everyone removes a tool that the most vulnerable pupils rely on, and it does so in the name of a convenience that benefits no one.

Fifth, and more broadly, there is the question of access to the written record. A great deal of the world is written in cursive. Letters from grandparents, family recipes, historical documents, comments in the margin of a teacher's mark book and the signature at the bottom of a contract are all joined. A child who cannot read cursive is cut off from these sources, and a child who cannot write it has no signature of their own beyond a block-capital scrawl. Literacy has always meant being able to take part in the written culture around you, and that culture is not purely digital. It would be odd to define a literate person as someone who can read only the typefaces of their own decade.

Opponents sometimes respond that reading cursive can be taught separately from writing it. This is true in theory but unconvincing in practice. We learn to read most fluently the scripts we write, because writing trains the eye to recognise variation. A child who writes cursive meets its quirks from the inside and learns that an a may be open or closed and that an r may dip or peak. A child who only reads it must decode each unfamiliar hand as a puzzle.

None of this means that schools should teach joining badly. The case for cursive has been weakened by dreadful execution, with rigid copybooks, punishing drills and an obsession with a single approved style that no adult actually uses. The best programmes are different. They teach a simple, legible, slightly slanted script with minimal flourishes. They introduce joins gradually, after children have mastered the basic letter shapes, and they link the practice to real writing rather than endless repetition of meaningless strings. They also accept that mature handwriting will vary. The goal is not copperplate. The goal is a fluent personal hand that is quick, clear and comfortable. Ten minutes a day, taught with care, is enough to achieve this by the end of primary school.

Nor should teaching handwriting displace keyboard skills. This is a false choice. Children should learn to type, and they should learn it properly, with attention to technique and accuracy. Schools are capable of teaching two skills to the same child, just as they teach both reading and mathematics. The real danger is not that cursive will crowd out typing but that both will be neglected in favour of a vague assumption that children will pick up whatever they need. They will not. Fluency in any motor skill requires deliberate instruction and practice, and the early years are when that instruction works best.

There is a final consideration that is easy to overlook, and it concerns the pleasure of the thing. A well-formed line of handwriting is satisfying to produce. Children who can write beautifully take pride in their work, and pride is not a trivial educational outcome. Many teachers report that pupils who have mastered a fluent joined hand look after their exercise books more carefully, because the page now looks like something worth keeping. This is hard to measure and impossible to put on a spreadsheet, yet it is real. Education is not solely a pipeline of measurable skills. It is also an introduction to crafts worth having, and handwriting is one of the oldest we possess.

So the case stands. Joined-up handwriting makes children faster, supports spelling, helps struggling learners, keeps them connected to a long written tradition and gives them a skill that remains useful whenever a pen is picked up. Against this, the objections amount mainly to the observation that keyboards exist. They do, and children should learn to use them. But the existence of one tool is not a reason to let another wither, particularly when the second tool builds habits of mind that the first does not. Schools should keep teaching cursive, teach it well and teach it early. The cost is a few minutes a day. The return is a lifetime of fluent writing.
