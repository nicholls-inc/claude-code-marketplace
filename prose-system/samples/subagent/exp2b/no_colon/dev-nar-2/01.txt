The Long Way Home

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rocket lifted off from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Jim Lovell, the commander, was already a veteran of three spaceflights. Fred Haise was making his first. The third seat belonged to Jack Swigert, a late substitute. The original command module pilot, Ken Mattingly, had been grounded days earlier after exposure to German measles, and Swigert stepped in with little time to prepare. Americans had landed on the Moon twice already, and the public had begun to treat the journeys as routine. Apollo 13 would change that view.

The crew named their command module Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. For the first two days almost nothing went wrong. The spacecraft coasted outward, the crew ran checks, and they even gave a television broadcast that most networks chose not to carry. Then, on the night of April 13, about fifty-six hours into the flight and roughly 200,000 miles from home, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the supercold liquid oxygen and hydrogen stored in the service module. The tanks needed regular stirring so that the gauges would read accurately.

Swigert flipped the switch. A little over a minute later the crew heard a loud bang, and the spacecraft shuddered. Warning lights flared. Swigert reported that they had a problem, and Lovell repeated it for the controllers in Houston.

The cause lay in a chain of small errors that had begun years before launch. The oxygen tank had been built with heating elements and thermostat switches rated for a lower voltage than the one used at the launch site. During a ground test, the heaters ran far too long and damaged the Teflon insulation on the wiring inside the tank, and nobody knew. When Swigert stirred the tank in space, bare wires sparked. The fire inside the pure oxygen was brief and violent, and it burst the tank and blew a panel off the side of the service module. The second oxygen tank was damaged and began to leak. The fuel cells that made the spacecraft's electricity and water depended on that oxygen.

At first the controllers could not understand what they were seeing. Instruments gave conflicting readings, and some engineers suspected a faulty sensor. Then Lovell, looking out of a window, saw gas streaming into space. It was oxygen, the ship's lifeblood, and it was disappearing. Within a couple of hours the command module was dying, running on a small battery meant only for the final descent to Earth.

Flight director Gene Kranz and his teams of controllers faced a stark choice. The Moon landing was gone. The question now was whether three men could be brought home at all. The answer began with an idea that had been studied but never used in earnest, which was to treat the lunar lander as a lifeboat. Aquarius had its own oxygen, power and water, though it was built to support two men for about two days on the Moon, not three men for four days in deep space. The crew crowded into it and shut down most of Odyssey to preserve its battery.

The next problem was the route. Apollo 13 had been placed on a path that would have missed Earth if nothing were done, and it was heading around the Moon on a trajectory that would carry it back toward home only in a roundabout way. Controllers decided that the quickest and safest path was to swing around the Moon and use the lander's descent engine to speed the return. That engine had never been meant for such a job, and the crew would have to fire it with almost no navigation help, since the guidance computer in Odyssey was off. Lovell and Haise lined the ship up by sighting the Sun and the Earth, and they fired the engine by hand and by feel.

On April 14 the ship passed behind the Moon, and for a time the crew was out of radio contact. They passed about 160 miles above the far side. At the point of greatest distance, nearly 250,000 miles from Earth, they were farther from home than any humans have ever been, a record that still stands. Lovell later said that seeing the Moon up close was almost beside the point, because the only thing in their minds was getting back.

The trip home was a test of endurance. The lander's systems were running at a minimum so that the supply of power and water would last. The cabin grew bitterly cold, with temperatures falling to a little above freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The crew had little to drink. Haise developed a fever and a painful infection. None of the men slept well, and the cold made rest nearly impossible.

Then came the carbon dioxide. Every breath the three men took raised the level of the gas in the lander's air, and the canisters that scrubbed it out were designed for two people. Odyssey held spare canisters, but they were square, and the lander's receptacles were round. Without a fix the crew would be poisoned by their own breath. On the ground, a team of engineers gathered the same sort of material the astronauts had aboard, which included plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a spacesuit hose and a lot of tape. They built an adapter and read the instructions up to the crew, who copied the assembly step by step. Within hours the carbon dioxide level began to fall.

A further challenge waited. Odyssey would have to be woken up from cold before reentry, using a battery that could not be allowed to drain. The standard procedure for starting the spacecraft took hours and had been designed for a ship with full power. Mattingly, the astronaut who had been grounded, worked in a simulator with engineers to write a new procedure that would bring the systems up with the minimum of electricity. He tested it again and again, and on the final day it was read to the crew. Mattingly never flew on Apollo 13, but his work helped save it.

On April 17 the crew prepared for the end. First they jettisoned the service module, and as it fell away they saw the damage for the first time. A whole side panel was gone, and torn equipment hung out of the wound. Lovell reported it to Houston with quiet understatement. Then Aquarius, which had served so well, was cast off, having done a job it was never built for. Odyssey was left alone with three men aboard, headed for a narrow corridor in the atmosphere. If the angle was too shallow, the capsule would skip off and be lost. If it was too steep, the heat shield would burn through.

The reentry blackout, when the heat of friction cuts off radio, lasted longer than expected, and people in Mission Control waited in silence for a voice to come through. Then Swigert's voice returned, and cheers broke out. Three parachutes opened over the South Pacific, and the capsule dropped into the sea near Samoa shortly after one in the afternoon, Eastern time. A helicopter and the carrier Iwo Jima were waiting. The crew had been gone nearly six days.

Apollo 13 is remembered as a successful failure. The mission did not land on the Moon, but it showed what trained people could do under pressure. Investigators redesigned the oxygen tanks, removed the flawed wiring and added a third oxygen tank away from the others. Later Apollo missions went on to land and explore. For three men and thousands of people on the ground, the lasting lesson was less about the Moon than about calm, ingenuity and the stubborn will to bring everyone home.
