A leap second is an extra second added now and then to the world's official time, so that clocks stay in step with the turning of the Earth. From 2035 at the latest, the practice will end.

The need for it comes from having two ways of measuring time. The older one is astronomical: a day is one rotation of the planet. The newer one is atomic: since 1967 the second has been defined by the vibrations of caesium atoms, which atomic clocks count with extraordinary steadiness. The Earth is not so reliable. Its spin is gradually slowing, mainly because of the drag of the Moon's tides, and it also speeds up and slows down unpredictably as the planet's core, oceans and ice sheets shift.

So the two clocks drift apart. Since 1972 the remedy has been to keep Coordinated Universal Time, the atomic-based standard behind civil time everywhere, within nine-tenths of a second of astronomical time. When the gap grows too wide, the international service that monitors the Earth's rotation orders an extra second at the end of June or December. On those nights, official clocks read 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight. This has happened 27 times, most recently on 31 December 2016.

For most people the adjustment passes unnoticed. For computers it is a recurring hazard. Software generally assumes that every minute has 60 seconds, and leap seconds cannot be programmed in far ahead because they are announced only about six months in advance. The 2012 leap second knocked out websites including Reddit and disrupted an airline reservation system, delaying flights in Australia. The one in 2016 caused faults at Cloudflare, a large internet infrastructure company.

Technology firms have improvised their own fixes. Google, for example, "smears" the extra second across many hours. These fixes differ from one company to the next, which means that systems meant to agree on the time briefly do not. Satellite navigation, telecommunications networks and financial trading, which depend on timing far finer than a second, are all exposed.

A further worry is that the Earth has recently been spinning slightly faster, raising the prospect of a first-ever negative leap second, with a second removed instead of added. That has never been tried.

In November 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures, the treaty body responsible for international units, voted to let the gap between atomic and astronomical time grow beyond one second by or before 2035. The International Telecommunication Union, which governs time signals broadcast by radio, endorsed the change the following year.

The drift will not be ignored forever. The new permitted limit has yet to be set, but it is expected to be large enough that no correction is needed for at least a century. Until then, noon by the clock will slip away from noon by the Sun by about a minute, too little for anyone to notice.
