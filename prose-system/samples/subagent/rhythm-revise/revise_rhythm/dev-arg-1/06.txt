Cities should scrap their minimum parking requirements, and the sooner the better.

The case begins with the uncomfortable fact that most of these rules were not the product of careful study: planners in the mid-twentieth century leaned on a handbook of parking generation rates that was built from a handful of suburban sites, often with free parking and little transit, and then wrote the resulting numbers into zoning codes as if they were laws of nature. A restaurant must provide one space for every hundred square feet of dining room, and an apartment building must provide two spaces per unit. Nobody asked whether those figures matched what people actually needed, and nobody had to, because the rule applied automatically and the cost landed on someone else.

That someone else is almost everyone, since a required parking space is not free just because the driver does not pay a meter. Land has to be set aside, concrete poured, and in a dense neighborhood a structured or underground space can cost tens of thousands of dollars to build. Developers do not absorb that cost out of generosity but pass it on in higher rents and higher purchase prices, which means a tenant who owns no car subsidizes the parking of the neighbor who owns two. Economists who have studied this estimate that mandated parking raises the cost of housing by a meaningful margin, and it falls hardest on the households least able to afford it, so a rule sold as a public convenience works in practice as a hidden tax on being poor and carless.

The mandate also warps what gets built. Minimums turn small lots into impossible puzzles, so the corner shop, the narrow infill townhouse, and the converted warehouse never get proposed in the first place, and when a developer must devote a third of a site to parking, projects shrink, units vanish, and the buildings that do appear spread out to make room for asphalt. Old downtowns that grew before these codes are the places people most admire and pay the most to visit, and many of their beloved buildings could not legally be rebuilt today; we have effectively outlawed the form of the neighborhoods we spend our vacations seeking out.

Defenders raise a reasonable worry, which is that if cities stop requiring parking, cars will spill onto residential streets and make life miserable for existing residents, and it deserves a serious answer rather than a wave of the hand. The first part of the answer is that removing a minimum is not the same as banning parking: developers will still build it wherever customers want it, because a building with no parking in a place that needs it will struggle to rent or sell. Lenders, tenants, and retailers all have strong incentives to get this right, and they know their own market far better than a code written decades ago. What changes is that the number becomes a decision rather than a decree, and it will land lower in walkable areas and higher in car-dependent ones, which is exactly what we want.

The second part of the answer is that spillover is a pricing problem, and cities already have the tools to solve it. If curb space on a busy street is scarce, charge for it: residential permit programs, metered spaces that adjust with demand, and revenue returned to the neighborhood all turn a free-for-all into a managed resource. Requiring every building to hide a private lot behind it is a clumsy, expensive way to avoid ever confronting that choice, whereas pricing the street directly is cheaper, fairer, and far more flexible.

Experience backs this up. Buffalo eliminated its citywide minimums in 2017, and in the years since, builders have used the freedom mostly to propose smaller, denser projects while still including parking where it made sense. Cities from Minneapolis to Edmonton to Auckland have followed with their own reforms, and the predicted chaos has not materialized; what has appeared instead are more homes on the same land, more small businesses able to open in modest buildings, and developers who finally get to build for the people who actually want to live there. The sky has not fallen in any of these places, and the evidence of reform has been consistently quiet and boring, which is the best compliment a policy can receive.

There is also a larger matter of what a city is for, because every acre of mandated parking is an acre not used for homes, workplaces, gardens, or anything that generates tax revenue and human activity. Cities that are strapped for money and short of housing can hardly afford to hand over their most valuable land to idle vehicles by default. Free parking is not free, and a rule that pretends otherwise has been quietly draining cities for generations.

None of this requires hostility to drivers, since plenty of people will keep driving and plenty of buildings will keep their garages. The point is that those choices should be made by people who bear the costs and know the local conditions, not dictated by a formula that was shaky when it was written. Cities that repeal their parking minimums are not abandoning planning; they are retiring a rule that never earned its authority, and trusting builders, residents, and prices to do what the mandate never could.
