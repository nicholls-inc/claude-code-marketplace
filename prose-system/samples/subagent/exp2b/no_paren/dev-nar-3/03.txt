On January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston woke to an unseasonably warm day. The temperature had climbed above forty degrees after a brutal cold snap, and the sun was out. Just after noon, workers and families went about their business near the waterfront, unaware that a giant tank looming over Commercial Street was about to fail.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol. It stood about fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, and it held more than two million gallons of molasses. The sticky cargo had come up by ship from the Caribbean. Its purpose was to be turned into industrial alcohol, which the war effort and the coming era of Prohibition both made valuable. The company had built the tank quickly and cheaply. Neighbors noticed it groaned, and that brown syrup seeped through its seams. Rather than repair the leaks, the company is said to have painted the tank brown to hide them.

At about 12:30 in the afternoon, the tank ruptured. Witnesses described a sound like a machine gun, which was probably the rivets shooting loose, followed by a rumble. Then a wave of molasses roughly twenty-five feet high at its start burst outward. It moved at around thirty-five miles an hour, and it carried with it a shock of air that knocked people off their feet.

The wave tore through the neighborhood. It flattened buildings, lifted a train off its elevated tracks, and shoved a firehouse off its foundation. Wagons, horses, and people were swept along. Because molasses is heavier than water and grows thicker in the cold, anyone caught in it found movement nearly impossible. Rescuers waded in to their knees and struggled to free the trapped. The syrup chilled and stiffened as it spread, turning a rescue into a desperate crawl.

Twenty-one people died, and about 150 more were injured. Among the dead were children playing in the street and workers eating lunch. Horses perished by the dozens. Searchers spent days pulling bodies out of the muck, and some victims were so thoroughly coated that they were hard to identify. Sailors from a nearby ship and members of the Red Cross and local police helped in the effort. Cleanup went on for weeks. Crews used salt water from fire boats and sand to cut through the mess, and the harbor ran brown into the summer. For decades afterward, residents claimed that on hot days the neighborhood still smelled faintly sweet.

The aftermath became a landmark in American law. The company argued that anarchists had bombed the tank, hoping to shift blame. Residents sued, and a court-appointed auditor spent years hearing testimony from hundreds of witnesses. Engineers showed that the tank had been poorly designed and never properly tested. The company had not even filled it with water to check for leaks before use. In 1925 the company was found liable, and it paid out settlements to victims and families.

The disaster also changed how buildings are made. It helped push Massachusetts toward requiring licensed engineers and architects to sign off on construction plans, and it strengthened the idea that companies must answer for the safety of their structures. What began as an ordinary afternoon in a crowded neighborhood ended as one of the strangest and most instructive catastrophes in American history.
