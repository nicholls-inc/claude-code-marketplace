Apollo 13: The Flight That Came Home

On the afternoon of April 11, 1970, a Saturn V rose from Kennedy Space Center carrying three men toward the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be the third lunar landing, and by then the public had begun to treat such trips as routine. Commander Jim Lovell, a veteran of three earlier flights, led the crew. Fred Haise was a rookie who would pilot the lunar module, and Jack Swigert, a bachelor and former test pilot, flew the command module. Swigert was a late replacement. Ken Mattingly had been grounded days before launch after exposure to German measles, and Swigert had trained hard to take his place.

The first two days went smoothly. The crew named their command module Odyssey and their lunar lander Aquarius. They sent home a television broadcast on the evening of April 13, joking and showing viewers around the ship. Few networks carried it. Almost no one was paying close attention, which would change within the hour.

About 200,000 miles from Earth, Mission Control asked the crew to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine step that keeps the supercold contents from stratifying. Swigert flipped the switch. A little over a minute later the crew heard a loud bang and felt a shudder. Warning lights lit up. Swigert radioed, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated it, adding that there was a main bus B undervolt.

What had happened was not understood for a while. Oxygen tank 2 had ruptured, and the blast had damaged tank 1 and blown a panel off the service module. Both tanks supplied oxygen for breathing and also fed the fuel cells that generated the spacecraft's electricity and drinking water. As the oxygen drained into space, the fuel cells died one by one. Lovell, looking out a window, saw gas streaming away from the ship and said so. The news was grim. Within hours the command module would have only its battery power, and those batteries were needed to survive reentry.

The root cause traced back years. Tank 2 had been dropped slightly during handling in 1968, and later a ground test at Kennedy left its heaters running far too long at the wrong voltage, scorching the insulation on the wiring inside. No one noticed. When Swigert flipped the stirring switch, a spark from the damaged wires ignited the insulation in the pure oxygen, and the tank failed.

In Houston, flight director Gene Kranz and his teams worked the problem through the night. The idea that saved the crew was the lunar module. Aquarius had its own oxygen, batteries and water, and it was designed to support two men for roughly two days on the Moon. Controllers decided to use it as a lifeboat for three men for the four days needed to get home. The crew powered up the lander and shut down Odyssey to save what little power it had left. The moon landing was abandoned, and the only goal now was survival.

The spacecraft was on a path that would loop around the Moon and return to Earth, but not on a safe path. Controllers needed to speed the trip home and aim for a proper splashdown spot. About five hours after passing behind the Moon, the crew fired the lander's descent engine, a job it was never designed to do while attached to the rest of the stack. The burn went well. At one point, on the far side, the ship reached a distance of nearly 248,700 miles from Earth, farther than any humans had ever traveled, a record that still stands.

The ordeal that followed was long and miserable. Aquarius was running on a bare minimum of power, so the crew switched off almost everything. The cabin grew cold, with temperatures dropping close to freezing, and moisture condensed on the walls. The men slept little. Water was strictly rationed to a few ounces a day each, because the lander's cooling system needed the rest. Haise developed a fever and a urinary infection, and Lovell and Swigert also suffered from dehydration and cold. All three lost weight.

Then came a more subtle danger, the air itself. Exhaled carbon dioxide has to be filtered from the cabin, and the lander's canisters of lithium hydroxide were rated for two men over two days, not three men over four. The command module carried plenty of canisters, but they were square and the lander's sockets were round. Engineers on the ground assembled a workaround from items the crew had on board, using plastic bags, cardboard from a flight manual, a spacesuit hose and duct tape. They read the instructions up to the crew, who built the contraption, and the carbon dioxide level began to fall. Crews later nicknamed it the mailbox.

Meanwhile Mattingly, the astronaut who had been bumped from the flight, worked in a simulator in Houston with engineers to devise a way to restart Odyssey using the smallest possible amount of electricity. Everything had to be reordered, and the procedure was written, tested and read to the crew near the end of the flight. Few people had ever tried to power up a spacecraft this way in space.

Navigation also became a problem. The ship drifted, and a small leak or venting could push it off course. Without the usual computer help and with the engine alignment hard to read, the crew performed a manual course correction on April 14 and another burn on the way back. Lovell steered by lining up the Earth against a window, holding the planet's terminator, the line between day and night, in a fixed position while Haise ran the burn. It worked.

On April 17, the last day, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module, a separation that gave them their first good look at what had happened. One entire side panel was gone, and the wreckage trailed wires and debris. Lovell reported the damage with plain astonishment. Then the crew moved into Odyssey, restarted it as rehearsed, and cast loose the lunar module that had kept them alive. Lovell later remarked that Aquarius had earned a salute.

Reentry brought one last tense stretch. During every return from space, a radio blackout occurs for a few minutes as plasma surrounds the capsule. For Apollo 13 the blackout dragged on longer than expected, and the control room waited in near silence, unsure whether the heat shield had survived the damage from the explosion. Finally, nearly a minute and a half past the predicted time, a voice came through. Swigert's transmission was heard, and people in Houston cheered. Three parachutes opened and Odyssey settled into the South Pacific near Samoa on April 17, 1970, within sight of the recovery ship USS Iwo Jima. The mission had lasted almost six days.

Afterward, a review board led by Edgar Cortright traced the failure to the tank and the sequence of mistakes before launch. NASA redesigned the oxygen system, removed the faulty thermostat switches, added a third oxygen tank and a spare battery, and the Apollo program continued. Apollo 14 flew the next January and landed on the Moon.

The flight was officially called a successful failure. No one reached the lunar surface, but three men came home alive after a crisis that tested nearly everyone involved, in space and on the ground. The lesson that people took from it was not that machines were safe, but that a team facing a problem with no precedent could still find a way through, one improvised piece at a time.
