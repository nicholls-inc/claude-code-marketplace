On a bright, unseasonably warm Wednesday in January 1919, Boston's North End smelled of the sea and of coal smoke. Shortly after 12:30 in the afternoon, a steel storage tank owned by the Purity Distilling Company, standing near the waterfront at Commercial and Foster Streets, gave way.

The tank was about 50 feet tall and 90 feet across, and it held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses. Purity used the molasses to make industrial alcohol, which was in demand as the country's war production wound down. The tank had been filled quickly and had leaked before. Workers had complained about its groaning and seeping, and they were ignored. That afternoon, a rivet line let go with a crack like artillery fire.

What came out was not a flow but a wall. Witnesses later estimated the wave at 15 to 30 feet high, moving at perhaps 35 miles an hour. It tore the tank apart and swept across the streets, lifting a freight train's cars off the rails, knocking buildings from their foundations, and crushing a firehouse and the supports of an elevated railway. Horses were trapped and drowned in the sticky mass. People who were caught in the flood were pinned under debris or carried into the harbor. Those who were not hit by the wave were coated in molasses that was thick, slow, and nearly impossible to wade through.

Rescue crews, many of them firefighters, sailors, and neighbors, worked for hours. Some dug through the mess with shovels, and some pulled survivors out by hand. The molasses was so heavy that it held onto everything, and in the January cold it began to harden as the work went on. In the end, 21 people died and about 150 were injured.

Cleanup took weeks. Crews used seawater, sand, and salt to scrub the streets, and sailors from a nearby Navy ship helped hose down the pavement. For years afterward, people in the North End said that on hot summer days the air still smelled faintly sweet and sour, and that the harbor water near the site took on a brownish tint.

The disaster prompted a lawsuit that dragged on for years. In 1925, a judge found Purity responsible, pointing to poor construction, inadequate testing, and a company that had ignored warnings. The payouts were modest compared with the suffering, and the case became a lesson in corporate negligence and the limits of the law at the time.

The flood is remembered today as one of the strangest disasters in American history: a city street turned into a river of syrup, and a reminder that the most ordinary materials can become deadly when they are stored carelessly.
