Just after half past noon on January 15, 1919, the North End of Boston was enjoying an oddly mild winter day. The temperature had climbed above 40 degrees Fahrenheit after a bitter cold snap, and along Commercial Street workers sat outside eating lunch, children played near the freight sheds, and horses stood waiting at their wagons. Towering over them all was a steel tank fifty feet tall and ninety feet across, holding roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, which turned molasses into industrial alcohol, much of it used in munitions during the First World War. It had been built in a hurry in 1915 and never properly tested. It leaked from the start, so freely that neighborhood children collected the drips in pails. The company's response, by most accounts, was to paint the tank brown so the streaks would not show. Residents said it groaned and rumbled whenever it was filled.

That day it gave way. Witnesses recalled a deep roar and a rapid series of sharp cracks, like machine-gun fire, as the rivets shot out of the seams. The steel walls burst apart and a wave of molasses, estimated at 25 feet high at its peak, rolled outward at about 35 miles an hour. There was no outrunning it.

The wave smashed buildings off their foundations, crushed a firehouse, and buckled a steel support of the elevated railway, nearly throwing a train off the tracks. People, horses and wagons were swept up and buried. As the molasses spread and cooled in the winter air it thickened, trapping survivors in a waist-deep, hardening mass that filled several blocks.

Rescuers arrived within minutes. Cadets from a training ship docked nearby waded in, followed by police, firefighters, Red Cross nurses and soldiers. They struggled for hours to pull out victims who could barely be distinguished from the wreckage around them. The search for bodies went on for days. In the end 21 people died and about 150 were injured. The dead ranged from laborers and teamsters to two ten-year-old children.

The cleanup took weeks. Crews hosed the streets with salt water from fireboats and spread sand to soak up the syrup. Rescuers and sightseers tracked molasses throughout the city, onto streetcars, into homes and across telephone receivers. The harbor ran brown until summer.

The company blamed anarchists, claiming a bomb had destroyed the tank. Victims and their families sued, and the case became one of the first great class-action suits in Massachusetts history. After years of hearings and testimony from thousands of witnesses, a court-appointed auditor concluded in 1925 that the tank had been structurally unsound, its walls too thin for the load they carried. The company paid out about $628,000 in damages.

The disaster had a lasting effect. Massachusetts and later other states began requiring that engineers review and sign building plans and that inspections be carried out. And for decades afterward, locals insisted that on hot summer days the North End still smelled faintly of molasses.
