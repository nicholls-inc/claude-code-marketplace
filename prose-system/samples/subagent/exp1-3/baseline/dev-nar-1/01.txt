In late August 1854, cholera struck Soho, a crowded district of London. Within ten days, more than five hundred people living within a few hundred yards of one another had died. Cholera is a disease of violent diarrhoea and dehydration that can kill a healthy adult in a day. In 1854 nobody knew what caused it.

Most educated people, including most doctors, believed in miasma. This was the idea that disease spread through foul air rising from rotting waste, filthy streets and sewers. The theory seemed sensible in a city that stank. London was full of cesspools, overflowing drains and a river that carried its own sewage past the city.

John Snow, a physician who lived and worked in Soho, did not accept this. Snow was a quiet, teetotal man best known for pioneering the use of anaesthesia. He had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. He had also studied cholera in the epidemic of 1848-49 and reached an unfashionable conclusion. Cholera, he argued, attacked the bowels first, not the lungs, so it must be swallowed, not breathed in. Something in contaminated water or food, passed from the sick to the healthy, was the likelier culprit. Few people took him seriously.

When the 1854 outbreak erupted almost on his doorstep, Snow went to work. He did not start with a theory about the air. He started with the dead. He obtained the death records from the local registrar and found where each victim had lived. Then he walked the streets and knocked on doors, asking families where they had got their water.

At that time, most Londoners had no water pipes in their homes. They drew water from public pumps, which were fed from wells dug into the ground or from water companies that piped in supplies from the Thames. The pump at the corner of Broad Street and Cambridge Street was popular. Its water was cool and clear, and people thought it tasted sweet.

Snow marked each death on a map of the district. The deaths clustered thickly around the Broad Street pump, and the pattern became clearer the more closely he examined it. Almost everyone who had died lived closer to that pump than to any other. Some of the exceptions taught him even more.

Near the pump stood a workhouse housing more than five hundred people. Only five of them died. Snow learned that the workhouse had its own well and so did not rely on the Broad Street pump. A brewery on Broad Street employed seventy workers, and none of them died of cholera. The men were given free beer, and the brewery had its own well. There was also a woman who lived far from Soho, in Hampstead, and who died of cholera. Snow discovered that she had once lived in Broad Street and so liked the taste of the water that she had it carried to her house every day. A niece who drank the same water died too.

By the evening of 7 September, Snow was convinced. He went to the board of guardians of St James's parish, the local authority, and presented his evidence. They were sceptical, but they agreed to act. On the following day, the handle was removed from the pump, so that it could not be used. New cases fell sharply soon afterwards.

The story is often told as if the removal of the handle ended the epidemic, but the truth is less tidy. The outbreak had already been declining, partly because many residents had fled the district. Snow himself admitted that the fall in cases might have happened anyway. The handle was a sign of his conviction, not a decisive experiment.

The map's real value was its power to persuade. Snow did not stop there. He and the Reverend Henry Whitehead, a local curate who started out as a sceptic, kept investigating. Whitehead interviewed residents and traced the first case to a baby girl who had fallen ill with diarrhoea just before the outbreak. Her mother had washed the baby's soiled nappies and thrown the water into a cesspool, which lay only a few feet from the Broad Street well. When the well was examined, the brickwork was found to be cracked, allowing sewage to seep in.

Snow also carried out a larger study during the same epidemic. He compared households in south London supplied by two water companies. One drew its water from a part of the Thames contaminated with the city's sewage. The other had moved its intake upriver, to a cleaner stretch. Households served by the first company suffered far more deaths. People on the same streets, living in similar conditions, had very different fates depending only on their water.

Even so, official bodies rejected his conclusions. A committee investigating the outbreak found no proof that the pump was to blame, and miasma remained the accepted view. Snow died in 1858, aged forty-five, still largely unconvinced of his own vindication in the eyes of his peers. It was not until the 1880s, when Robert Koch identified the cholera bacterium, that his work was fully accepted.

Today Snow is remembered as a founder of modern epidemiology, the study of how disease spreads through populations. His method was simple. He counted, mapped, compared and asked questions, and he followed the evidence even where it led against received opinion.
