In the late summer of 1854, a terrible sickness struck the crowded London district of Soho. Within days of its start in the last week of August, hundreds of people living near Broad Street were dying of cholera, a disease that causes violent vomiting and diarrhoea and can drain the body of fluid so quickly that a healthy person may be dead within a day. Most of the district's doctors believed, as most educated people then did, that cholera was caused by "miasma," a poisonous vapour rising from rotting rubbish, damp ground and foul air. The germ theory of disease had not yet been accepted, and nobody had seen the organism responsible.

John Snow, a physician who lived a few streets away, was not persuaded by the vapour theory. He was already well known in medical circles as a pioneer of anaesthesia, and he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria during childbirth. Years earlier, during the cholera epidemic of 1848 and 1849, he had reasoned that the illness must be swallowed rather than breathed in, because it attacked the stomach and bowels long before it affected anything else. He thought some invisible poison passed from the bowels of the sick into water or food and then into the bodies of others. He had published this idea in a short book, but it had been politely ignored. The Soho outbreak gave him a chance to test it.

Snow began by asking where the dead had lived. He obtained the official list of deaths from the local registrar and then went door to door, questioning grieving families about what they had eaten and, above all, where they had gotten their drinking water. Many households in the neighbourhood relied on a public pump in Broad Street, whose water was prized for its clear, cool taste. As he plotted the deaths on a street map, using a small bar to mark each victim at the address where they had lived, he saw that the marks piled up thickly around that pump and thinned out with distance from it.

The pattern was suggestive, but Snow knew that suggestion was not proof, and he worked hard to deal with the cases that did not fit. Nearby there was a workhouse housing more than five hundred poor residents, and only a handful of them had died. Snow found that the workhouse had its own well inside the building and so did not need the Broad Street pump. A brewery on the same street had also escaped almost untouched, and its workers, it turned out, were given beer to drink and also had their own supply of water. On the other hand, he learned of a widow living far away in Hampstead who had died after cholera struck her household. She had once lived in Soho and liked the taste of the Broad Street water so much that she had a bottle of it delivered to her each day. A niece who drank from the same bottle also fell ill and died. These puzzling exceptions, which pointed to the pump all the more clearly, were exactly the sort of evidence that vapour in the air could not explain.

On 7 September, Snow took his findings to the board of guardians that governed the parish, a group of local officials who had little reason to take instruction from a doctor with an unfashionable theory. They listened, though, and the next day they ordered that the handle be taken off the pump so that no one could draw water from it. By then the outbreak was already fading, partly because so many residents had fled the area and partly because many of the most vulnerable had already died, so sceptics could and did argue that the removal of the handle had changed nothing. Snow himself admitted that the decline did not prove his case.

A crucial piece of the puzzle came from an unlikely ally. Henry Whitehead, a young curate who knew the neighbourhood and its people well, had begun by doubting Snow entirely. But his own careful questioning of local families led him to the house at number 40 Broad Street, where an infant girl had fallen ill with diarrhoea just before the epidemic began and had later died. Her family had washed her soiled linen and thrown the water into a cesspool beneath the house. Inspection later showed that the brickwork of the cesspool was decayed and that sewage could seep through it into the ground only a few feet from the well that fed the pump. Whitehead became a convert, and he and Snow eventually worked together.

Official opinion was slower to change. A committee of inquiry looked into the outbreak and declined to endorse Snow's explanation, and he died in 1858, still largely unrecognised on this point. Yet his method of mapping cases, interviewing residents and hunting down the exceptions became the foundation of modern epidemiology, and within a generation London was building the great sewers that finally separated its drinking water from its waste.
