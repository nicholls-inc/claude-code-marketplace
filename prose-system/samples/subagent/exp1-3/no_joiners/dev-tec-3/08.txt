A lithium ion cell stores energy by moving lithium ions between two electrodes. During charging, ions leave the cathode, cross a liquid electrolyte, and insert themselves into the graphite anode, a process called intercalation. The electrons take the external route through the charger, which is how energy enters the cell. Cell voltage rises as the anode fills with lithium and the cathode empties.

Chargers use a two stage protocol known as constant current followed by constant voltage. In the first stage the charger forces a fixed current, commonly about one times the cell capacity per hour. The charger raises its output voltage as needed to hold that current, and the cell voltage climbs from roughly 3.0 volts toward 4.2 volts for a typical cobalt based chemistry. This stage delivers most of the capacity, often 70 to 80 percent.

The second stage begins when the terminal voltage reaches its upper limit. That limit is a hard design boundary. Above it the electrolyte oxidizes at the cathode and metallic lithium can plate onto the anode, which shortens life and creates a safety risk. So the charger stops controlling current and holds the voltage fixed. Current then falls on its own, and charging ends when it drops to a small threshold, typically a few percent of the capacity rating.

Why does the current fall? The terminal voltage is not set by the chemistry alone. It equals the open circuit voltage of the electrodes plus the current multiplied by the internal resistance. That resistance comes from the electrolyte, the electrode interfaces, and the slow diffusion of ions inside the electrode particles. In the first stage a large share of the applied voltage is spent on this resistive drop. As the cell fills, its open circuit voltage rises toward the fixed limit. With the terminal voltage pinned, the headroom left for the resistive term shrinks, so the current must shrink with it. An engineer will recognize a first order relaxation, with current decaying roughly exponentially toward zero.

Diffusion makes the effect stronger. Lithium arrives at particle surfaces faster than it can spread inward. The surface becomes nearly full while the core stays partly empty, so the surface potential rises early and the cell reaches the voltage limit before the bulk is truly full. The constant voltage stage gives the lithium time to migrate inward and equalize.

Temperature matters as well. Cold cells have higher resistance, so they reach the limit sooner and spend longer in the taper. Pushing harder in the final stretch would save a few minutes at the price of faster aging, so the taper is a deliberate protection and not a flaw.
