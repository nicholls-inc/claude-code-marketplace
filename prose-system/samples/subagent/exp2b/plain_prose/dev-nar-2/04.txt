On the evening of April 11, 1970, three men lifted off from Cape Kennedy aboard a Saturn V rocket, bound for the Moon. Apollo 13 was meant to be NASA's third lunar landing, and by then the public had begun to treat such flights as routine. Commander James Lovell, a veteran of three earlier missions, was joined by Fred Haise, who was to walk on the lunar surface, and by Jack Swigert, a last-minute replacement for Ken Mattingly, who had been grounded after exposure to German measles. The launch had one early hiccup, when a center engine on the second stage shut down too soon, but the other engines burned longer to make up the difference, and the spacecraft settled into its path toward the Moon.

For two days the flight was quiet enough that television networks declined to broadcast the crew's transmissions. The astronauts joined their command module, Odyssey, to the lunar module, Aquarius, and coasted outward. On the night of April 13, roughly 200,000 miles from home, Mission Control asked Swigert to stir the oxygen and hydrogen tanks in the service module, a routine step that agitates the supercooled contents so the gauges read accurately. Swigert flipped the switch. Moments later, at about 9:08 in the evening Houston time, the crew heard a loud bang and felt the ship shudder.

Swigert's words, "Houston, we've had a problem," and Lovell's repetition a moment later, became famous, but what followed was a slow and unwelcome discovery. Warning lights flickered. Power on the main electrical buses dropped. The thrusters fired on their own as the spacecraft jolted. Lovell, looking out a window, saw a stream of gas venting into space. In the control room, flight controllers watched their data scroll in contradictions, at first suspecting a faulty instrument rather than a failing ship. But the readings kept pointing the same direction. Oxygen tank number two was empty, tank one was draining, and the fuel cells that made the ship's electricity and water depended on that oxygen.

The cause lay years in the past. Damaged wiring inside the tank, its insulation scorched during a ground test when the heaters were run far beyond their intended temperature, had been left undetected. When Swigert switched on the stirring fans, a spark ignited the insulation in the pure oxygen, the tank ruptured, and the blast blew off a panel of the service module and crippled its neighbor. The Moon landing was gone. The question now was survival.

Gene Kranz, the flight director leading the white team, gathered his controllers and set the tone for what followed: the crew would come home, and no one would hear otherwise. Odyssey's remaining oxygen was bleeding away, and its batteries could not last long. The crew's only lifeboat was the lunar module, designed to carry two men for about two days on the Moon, now asked to sustain three for four. Lovell and Haise floated into Aquarius and began bringing it to life, while Swigert shut down Odyssey to save every watt of the batteries it would need for reentry. Before leaving the command module, Lovell carried over the navigation alignment, a delicate transfer of information, because the lunar module's guidance system would now steer the whole stack.

The trajectory presented a second problem. Apollo 13 was on a free-return path that would swing around the Moon and back toward Earth, but a correction made earlier in the flight had moved it off that safe course. Controllers decided to fire Aquarius's descent engine to speed the return. About five hours after the explosion, as the ship passed behind the Moon, they made the first burn. Then, a few hours after rounding the far side, with Earth still a long way off, a second and longer burn shortened the trip and aimed it for a Pacific splashdown. The crew saw the Moon pass under them, scarred and gray, with no chance to land on it.

The days that followed were a test of endurance. To conserve power, Aquarius was cooled almost to the point of shutdown, and the temperature in the cabin dropped to around 40 degrees Fahrenheit. Water was rationed to a few ounces per man each day, and Haise developed a fever and a kidney infection. Condensation dripped from the walls. Sleep was nearly impossible. Meanwhile the air itself was turning toxic. Carbon dioxide was building up because Aquarius's lithium hydroxide canisters, built for two men, were saturating, and Odyssey's square canisters did not fit the lunar module's round receptacles.

On the ground, engineers assembled in a room with a box of the same materials the crew had on board: plastic bags, cardboard, hoses, spacesuit parts, and duct tape. They designed a way to connect the square canister to the round opening, and read the instructions to the crew step by step. The astronauts built the adapter in space, and the carbon dioxide level fell. Elsewhere in the building, Mattingly, who had been spared the flight and was now working in a simulator, tested the sequence for restarting the command module with the tiny amount of power available, writing a procedure that would bring Odyssey back to life.

Another worry came as the ship neared home. Earth's atmosphere demands a narrow corridor: too shallow and the capsule skips back into space, too steep and it burns up. Without Odyssey's guidance, the crew had to make a final course correction by hand, using the Earth itself, its terminator line, as a reference. Lovell and Swigert held the alignment as Haise fired the thrusters, and the ship settled into the proper angle.

On April 17, the crew jettisoned the damaged service module and saw, for the first time, the extent of the harm: an entire side panel missing, with wreckage trailing from the exposed bay. Then they released Aquarius, thanking the craft that had saved them, and Odyssey stood alone. The batteries held. As the capsule plunged into the atmosphere, communications blacked out, as always, for about three minutes. Controllers waited in silence past the expected moment, then a little longer. At last Swigert's voice came through the static, and cheers broke out in Houston as the parachutes opened over the South Pacific. Odyssey splashed down near the recovery ship Iwo Jima, and the three men, thinner, dehydrated, and exhausted, were pulled aboard.

The mission was a failure by its original aim, but NASA later called it a successful failure. An investigation traced the accident to the oxygen tank and led to redesigns of the tanks and wiring. What lingered in public memory was the improvisation: engineers and astronauts, short of time, power, and air, working out solutions that no one had rehearsed, and bringing three men back alive across a quarter of a million miles.
